What lead generation for finance brokers involves

Lead generation for finance brokers is the work of getting qualified business owners to ask you about finance, so that you spend your time advising and arranging deals rather than searching for people to talk to. For a commercial finance broker that means a steady flow of UK limited companies and LLPs who need funding for growth, cash flow, equipment, stock, property or refinancing, and who are willing to take your call.

It sounds simple, but it is really four separate jobs. You have to be visible to business owners at the moment they are thinking about finance. You have to give them a reason to make contact. You have to capture their details in a form you can act on. And you have to follow up quickly enough that the enquiry is still warm when you ring.

Most brokers who struggle with leads are not failing at all four. They are usually strong at one or two and weak at the rest. A broker with a great network of accountants may have no way to catch an enquiry at midnight. A broker with a good website may be slow to ring back. Seeing the whole chain makes it easier to spot where your pipeline leaks.

There is also the question of who owns each step. With introducers, someone else makes the first contact and you inherit the relationship. With your own marketing, you own every step but carry every cost. With bought leads, a supplier handles visibility and capture and you handle the conversation. None of these is automatically better. Each shifts effort and risk to a different place.

This guide walks through each route that finance brokers use, with honest pros and cons, then covers the practical questions that decide whether any channel pays back: lead quality, exclusivity, consent, speed, and the cost of a completed deal rather than the cost of a lead. It then explains where Loanleaf fits, and where it does not.

The main channels for lead gen for finance brokers

Most finance brokers draw on a mix of the following routes. They differ sharply in how fast they produce enquiries, how much they cost to start, how much of your time they need and how predictable the output is.

  • Introducers and accountant referrals: other professionals send you clients they already advise.
  • Your own SEO and content: a website and guides that rank for the searches business owners make.
  • Pay-per-click search advertising: paying to appear when someone searches for finance.
  • Paid social: adverts on social platforms aimed at business owners.
  • Cold outreach: approaching businesses that have not asked to hear from you, by phone, email or message.
  • Events and networking: meeting owners and referrers in person or online.
  • Buying leads: paying a supplier for enquiries from owners who have asked about finance.

The sections below take each in turn. As a general rule, the routes that cost the least money tend to cost the most time and patience, while the routes that cost the most money tend to deliver sooner. Almost nothing is both cheap and quick, which is why most established brokers combine two or three routes.

A useful way to think about it is control versus speed. Referrals and SEO build assets you keep, but they take months. Paid routes switch on within days but stop when you stop paying. Your plan should include something from each side so that a slow month in one does not empty your diary.

Introducers and accountant referrals

Introducers are professionals whose clients sometimes need finance: accountants, bookkeepers, business consultants, solicitors, commercial agents and other brokers who do not cover your product area. When an accountant tells a client to speak to you, the trust transfers to you before you have said a word.

Pros. Referred clients often convert well because they arrive with a recommendation. The cost is usually modest and tied to completed business, if you agree a referral arrangement at all. Good relationships can last for years and keep delivering without further spend.

Cons. Volume is hard to predict and hard to scale. A handful of active introducers might send a few enquiries a quarter, and the flow depends on their own workload and memory. Building the network takes months of meetings, calls and follow-up. Introducers also expect feedback and good service, because your conduct reflects on them, and any arrangement involving payment needs careful handling so that you stay on the right side of your own regulatory obligations.

Practical effort. Expect regular, personal contact. The brokers who do well here make it easy to refer: a short explainer of what you do and do not fund, a named contact, a quick response and a note back to the introducer once the client has been looked after. A sixty second summary an accountant can read aloud to a client is worth more than a glossy brochure.

Introducers suit brokers who enjoy relationship building and can wait for results. They are rarely enough on their own if you need a reliable number of conversations every week, but they form a strong base to add other channels to.

Your own SEO and content

Search engine optimisation means building a website that appears when business owners search for things like business loans, asset finance, invoice finance or help after a decline. Content such as guides, calculators and plain English explainers attracts those searches and shows that you know the subject.

Pros. Visitors arrive because they are already looking for finance, so intent can be high. Once a page ranks it can keep producing enquiries without a per-click cost. The asset is yours, and it builds brand recognition in the market you serve.

Cons. It is slow. New sites generally take months before meaningful traffic arrives, and there is no guarantee any given page will rank. Finance is a competitive subject, with lenders, comparison sites and large brokers all targeting the same terms. You need someone who can write accurately about finance, keep pages up to date and technical know-how to keep the site fast and crawlable. Content that makes claims about products or rates also needs to be accurate and fair, which takes review time.

Practical effort. A realistic plan is to pick a narrow focus first, such as one product, one sector or one region, and cover it properly rather than writing thinly about everything. Add clear enquiry forms, make them quick to complete on a phone, and make sure every enquiry reaches a person promptly. Track which pages produce enquiries that actually convert, not just visits.

SEO suits brokers who are playing a long game and have either time or budget for content. It pairs well with paid search, which can cover the gap while organic rankings build.

Pay-per-click search advertising

Pay-per-click, or PPC, means bidding to appear at the top of search results for terms such as business loan, asset finance or invoice finance. You pay each time someone clicks, whether or not they enquire.

Pros. It is fast. You can be live within days and tweak the targeting daily. You reach people actively searching, which is as close to demand as advertising gets. You also get clear data on which searches and adverts produce enquiries.

Cons. Finance keywords are often expensive because many advertisers want the same clicks. Without careful management, budgets can disappear on broad terms, on people looking for consumer loans, or on clicks that never convert. Landing pages and forms matter as much as the advert itself: a slow or confusing page wastes the click. Platforms also have rules for financial services advertising that you need to meet. When the budget stops, the enquiries stop.

Practical effort. Someone needs to own the account. That means keyword research, negative keywords to filter out irrelevant searches, advert testing, landing page work and weekly review of the numbers. Many brokers use a specialist agency, which adds a fee, though a poorly run in-house account can cost more in wasted clicks.

The key discipline is to judge PPC by completed deals, not clicks or even enquiries. A campaign that looks cheap per enquiry but produces mostly unfundable requests will cost you more per completion than one with a higher cost per enquiry and better fit. Start with a small test budget, a tight list of search terms and a clear target cost per funded deal, then expand only what works.

Paid social advertising

Paid social means advertising on social platforms, usually with a short message and a link to a form or page. Targeting options let you aim at business owners, certain sectors or regions.

Pros. It can generate a lot of enquiries quickly and at a lower cost per enquiry than search, because you are interrupting people rather than catching a search. It is useful for building awareness and for reaching owners who have not started looking for finance yet but would respond to an offer. Formats such as video can explain a product simply.

Cons. Intent is usually lower. Someone scrolling past an advert is not necessarily in the market, so enquiries may be less ready, and more of them may drop out when you call. Quality can vary a lot with the message and the audience. Finance advertising is also subject to platform restrictions, and adverts can be rejected or accounts restricted if the wording crosses a line. Creative needs refreshing regularly because audiences tire of the same advert.

Practical effort. You need decent creative, a landing page that matches the promise of the advert, a form that qualifies without scaring people off, and fast follow-up. Because enquiries from social channels can cool quickly, the speed of your response matters even more than with search. Without that, you end up paying for lists of names that never pick up.

As with PPC, measure results through to funded deals. A form that asks only for a name and number will produce plenty of enquiries and plenty of wasted calls. A form that asks sensible questions about amount, trading history and revenue will produce fewer, better ones. Neither is right in every case, so test, and keep the one that produces completions.

Cold outreach

Cold outreach means approaching businesses that have not asked to hear from you: telephone calls, emails, messages on professional networks or letters. It is a numbers game that rewards persistence.

Pros. You choose exactly who to approach, whether that is a particular sector, size of business or region. There are no media costs beyond tools and time. It can work well when you have a specific offer for a specific type of firm, and it can open conversations with owners who are not yet looking.

Cons. Most people you contact will not be interested, and most will not reply. It takes sustained effort, thick skin and good records. More importantly, it comes with legal rules. Calling or emailing businesses for marketing involves data protection and privacy regulations, including rules on telephone preference services for some contacts, on consent for electronic marketing, and on the need to give people a clear way to opt out. These rules are not trivial and the consequences of getting them wrong can be serious. This is not legal advice, so take advice from a qualified professional on your own position.

Practical effort. Building a good list takes time, writing messages that get replies takes testing, and the follow-up sequence needs discipline. Many brokers find the hit rate low compared with the hours put in, and their best people spend their days dialling rather than advising.

Cold outreach can still be a useful supplement, especially for niche targeting. It is rarely the best main engine for a broker who wants predictable, compliant volume, and it works best when each message is relevant to the person receiving it rather than a broad blast.

Events and networking

Events cover industry conferences, local business networking groups, chamber of commerce meetings, sector exhibitions, webinars and sponsored sessions with accountants or trade bodies. The aim is to meet owners and referrers in a setting where a conversation is natural.

Pros. Face to face trust is hard to match. People who have met you and heard you speak are far more likely to take your call later. Events are also a good way to meet introducers, and a single useful conversation can lead to years of referrals.

Cons. They are expensive in time, and sometimes in money. A day out of the office is a day without calls, and sponsored stands can cost serious sums with no guaranteed return. Results are lumpy and hard to attribute: a client who signs six months after you chatted over coffee may not even remember the event. Some groups are mainly populated by other people selling to each other, which gives you plenty of conversations and few buyers.

Practical effort. Pick a small number of events where your target owners or introducers genuinely gather, and go consistently rather than sampling many once. Prepare a short, plain description of who you help. Follow up within days, not weeks, and keep track of who you met. Running your own small webinar or workshop for accountants or a local business group can be more effective than sponsoring someone else's.

Events work as a trust builder and a feeder for introducer relationships. Like referrals, they tend to complement rather than replace channels that produce enquiries on a more regular schedule.

Buying finance broker leads

Buying leads means paying a supplier for enquiries from business owners who have asked for finance. The supplier handles the marketing and capture, and you pay for the result, usually per lead or through a subscription of credits.

Pros. It is quick to start and easy to scale up or down. There is no need to build a website, learn advertising platforms or wait for rankings. The cost is clear and tied to enquiries received. It lets a small team put its time into conversations rather than marketing.

Cons. Quality varies enormously between suppliers, and the market has a mixed reputation for good reason. Some leads are sold to several brokers at once, some come from lists rather than live enquiries, and some contain bad phone numbers or businesses that never asked to be contacted. You are also dependent on the supplier's marketing, and you do not build an asset of your own.

What to check before buying.

  • Where do the enquiries come from? Look for owners who completed a form themselves, not lists, scraped data or purchased contacts.
  • Is each lead sold to one broker or shared with several?
  • What consent did the owner give, and is the wording and timestamp supplied with the lead?
  • What is checked before you pay: valid phone number, working email, repeat enquiries removed?
  • What do you see before buying, and how is price set?
  • What happens when contact details turn out to be wrong?
  • Do you need a long contract, or can you stop when you want to?

A supplier that answers these questions plainly is easier to trust than one that leads with promises about volume. Bought leads do not replace your own reputation and referrals, but they can give a steady base of conversations while slower channels develop.

Shared leads versus exclusive leads

A shared lead is sold to more than one broker, often three, four or more. An exclusive lead is sold to one broker only. The distinction affects almost everything about how you work the lead.

With shared leads, the owner may receive several calls within minutes. By the time you ring, they may already have spoken to someone, decided who they like, or simply stopped answering. Speed becomes a race, and your competitors see the same details you do. Shared leads are usually cheaper per lead, but you pay for the same enquiry that others are also chasing, so the cost per completed deal can end up higher than the price suggests.

With exclusive leads, nobody else has the details. The owner hears from you, not from a queue of brokers. You can take a sensible amount of time to prepare, write a good first message and call at a considered moment. The enquiry often costs more up front, but you are not splitting the opportunity.

There is also an experience point. An owner who filled in a form and then received four calls and six texts in ten minutes may well form a poor view of every broker involved, and of the whole process. A single, well prepared call from one broker tends to land better.

Exclusivity is not a cure-all. An exclusive lead still needs quick, professional follow-up, and exclusive does not mean guaranteed to complete. But it does remove one source of waste. If you are weighing a supplier, ask directly how many brokers receive each enquiry and whether it is ever resold. The exclusive leads page explains how this works at Loanleaf.

What makes a good commercial finance lead

A good lead is one you can place with a lender and complete. That depends on facts about the business, not on how enthusiastic the enquiry sounds. The more of those facts you can see before you pick up the phone, the less time you waste on cases that were never going to work.

At Loanleaf, every lead comes from an owner completing the enquiry form on the Loanleaf website. Each lead includes:

  • Loan amount, chosen from £5,000 to £2m+.
  • Purpose: growth and expansion, cash flow, equipment or vehicles, stock and inventory, commercial property (buy, build or refurbish), refinancing existing debt, a tax or VAT bill, or something else.
  • Timeline: as soon as possible, within 2 weeks, within a month, or just exploring options.
  • Company structure: limited company or LLP.
  • Annual revenue band, from £50k to £100k up to £5m+.
  • Time trading band, from 6 to 12 months up to 5+ years.
  • Credit history: whether there have been missed payments, defaults or CCJs in the last 6 years.
  • Existing finance: whether the business has any, with the amount left to repay and monthly repayment bands.
  • Contact details: name, business name, UK mobile and email, plus the source page, timestamp and consent record.
  • A quality score from 0 to 100, based on credit history, time trading, revenue, urgency and existing debt.

Before an enquiry is submitted, the form turns away sole traders, ordinary partnerships and businesses not yet trading. It requires at least 6 months of trading and annual revenue of £50,000 or more, and it checks that the mobile is a valid UK mobile number and that the email domain can receive email.

Just as important is what the form does not ask. It does not ask about card takings, property value or loan to value, planning status, sector, director names or bank statements. Those are things you establish on the first call. A good lead gives you enough to decide whether a conversation is worth having and what to ask in it, not a finished file.

Before buying you see every live lead's details, with masked contact details and the valid UK mobile and domain verified checks, and the full details unlock on purchase. If the same business enquires again within 7 days, the repeat goes back to the broker who already bought it, at no extra cost. After that, a broker never receives the same lead again and never pays for the same lead twice. The quality score is a guide to priority, not a verdict, so use it to decide whom to ring first and let the conversation decide the rest. Consent and data protection for leads

Whatever route you use to generate leads, you are handling personal data, and you need a lawful basis for contacting people. This section is general information, not legal advice. You are responsible for your own regulatory position, including data protection and any FCA authorisation your activities require, so take professional advice where you are unsure.

A few practical principles apply across channels.

  • Know where the data came from. You should be able to say, for any enquiry, how the person came to you and what they agreed to.
  • Keep the evidence. The wording shown to the person and the time they agreed are more useful than a vague statement that consent was obtained.
  • Contact people in line with what they agreed to. If someone agreed to a call about business finance, a different kind of marketing message may not be covered.
  • Offer a way to opt out and act on it promptly.
  • Store data securely and keep it only as long as needed. Control who in your firm can see it, and be careful about exporting it to spreadsheets and personal devices.
  • Be careful with purchased or shared lists. A list does not show that anyone wanted to hear from you.

With Loanleaf, the owner ticks two separate boxes before submitting. One accepts the terms and privacy policy and confirms that Loanleaf is an introducer, not a lender or broker. The other consents to their details being shared with a matched broker, who may contact them by phone, email or text about business finance. The exact wording and the timestamp are recorded and supplied with the lead, so you have a clear record of where it came from.

That helps, but it does not remove your own duties. You still decide how you contact people, how you store what you receive and how you meet your own regulatory obligations. The practical side of storing and sharing lead information is covered in the guide to handling lead data.

Speed to lead and working the enquiries you get

The best lead in the world loses value every hour it waits. An owner who has just filled in a form is thinking about finance right now. A few hours later, they are back in the day job, and a day later they may have found another route. Calling quickly is one of the few things in your control that costs nothing.

Speed alone is not enough. A quick call with no preparation wastes the opening. A sensible routine looks like this.

  • Get alerted instantly. Leads should reach you in real time, by email and into your CRM, so that nobody has to check a portal.
  • Read the lead before you ring. Look at the amount, purpose, timeline, credit answer and existing finance, so your opening shows you have read what they wrote.
  • Ring within minutes where you can. If there is no answer, leave a short voicemail and send a brief message that refers to what they asked about.
  • Try again at different times. Owners are often in meetings or on site, so a second attempt later in the day or early the next morning can reach them.
  • Keep a record. Log every attempt and outcome, with a clear next step and date for each lead.
  • Follow up beyond day one. Many replies come after a few attempts, spread over a couple of weeks.

Timeline matters too. An owner who ticked as soon as possible or within 2 weeks needs a different level of urgency from one who is just exploring options. Treat the first group as priority, and give the second group a calmer, more educational approach.

For more depth, see the speed to lead guide. If you have a team, agree a standard for who calls, how fast and what happens when they are unavailable. Leads that sit in a shared inbox over a weekend are the most common cause of avoidable waste.

Cost per lead versus cost per completed deal

Cost per lead is easy to measure and easy to be misled by. A cheaper lead that never completes is more expensive than a dearer lead that does. The number that matters is what you spend, across all channels, to produce one completed deal.

To work it out for any channel, track the following over a meaningful period:

  • Total spend, including media costs, supplier fees, tools, agency fees and the time of people who work the channel.
  • Number of enquiries received.
  • Number you reached and had a real conversation with.
  • Number that became applications.
  • Number that funded, and the commission or fee earned on each.

Divide total spend by funded deals to get the cost per completed deal. Compare that with the average commission per deal to see whether the channel makes money. A channel with a high cost per lead and a strong completion rate can be better than one that looks cheap per lead.

Be careful with small samples. A few deals in a month will swing your numbers hard, and commercial finance deals can take weeks or months to complete, so the leads you buy this month may not fund until later. Look at several months and judge each channel on the enquiries it received in that period, however long they took to close.

Also track the reasons that leads fail: no answer, not interested, could not be funded, went elsewhere. If many fail because the numbers are wrong, that is a supplier problem. If many fail because you reached them too late, that is an internal problem. If many fail because the lender appetite is not there, you may be buying the wrong kind of lead.

To get a feel for the arithmetic, try the cost per lead calculator, which lets you test different lead costs and completion rates against your own numbers.

Building a predictable pipeline for lead generation for finance brokers

A predictable pipeline rarely comes from a single source. It comes from layers that behave differently, so that when one dips another carries you. Here is a sensible way to think about the mix.

  • A foundation of relationships. Introducers and past clients take time to build but cost little to maintain and convert well. Keep investing a small, steady amount of time here.
  • An owned asset. A good website with useful content builds slowly and keeps paying. Treat it as a long-term investment.
  • A switchable layer. Paid search, paid social or bought leads can be turned up or down within days. Use these to smooth out peaks and troughs and to fill the gaps while slower channels grow.
  • Selective extras. Events and cold outreach can add colour and open particular doors, but choose them carefully.

Start by working out how many funded deals you want per month and work backwards. If you complete a certain share of the people you reach, and reach a certain share of the enquiries you receive, you can estimate how many enquiries you need. Use your own figures rather than anyone else's, because your product mix, team and lender panel all change the picture.

Then set limits. Decide how much you will spend per month on each channel, and review monthly. Move money towards what produces funded deals and away from what does not, but give new channels enough time to prove themselves before you judge them.

Capacity matters as much as supply. More enquiries than your team can handle quickly will hurt, because the speed of your response falls. It is usually better to match lead volume to the number of people available to work them, and to grow the two together.

Finally, keep a simple record of every enquiry and where it came from. Without source tracking, you cannot tell what is working, and you will end up cutting the wrong channel.

There is no shame in either answer. If you are unsure, it is reasonable to start small, buy a modest number of leads, track what happens and decide from your own results.

Questions

What is lead generation for finance brokers?

What is the best way to get finance broker leads?
There is no single best route, because each trades off cost, speed, control and predictability. Referrals and SEO build lasting assets but take time. Paid advertising and bought leads start faster but stop when the spending stops. A sensible plan combines a relationship base with at least one channel you can switch up or down quickly.
How much does lead gen for finance brokers cost?
It depends heavily on the channel and your own efficiency, so be wary of any single figure. With Loanleaf, each lead costs £20 to £300 in credits depending on how fundable it looks, and plans start at £2,500 + VAT a month for 2,500 credits. Judge any channel by its cost per completed deal rather than its cost per lead.
Are shared leads or exclusive leads better for brokers?
Exclusive leads avoid the race against other brokers calling the same owner, and they give a better experience for the business owner. Shared leads usually cost less per lead, but you pay for an enquiry others are also chasing. Check how many brokers receive each lead and whether it is ever resold, and compare on cost per completed deal.
Where do Loanleaf's finance broker leads come from?
Every lead is a business owner who completed the enquiry form on the Loanleaf website after finding it through search, content or paid online advertising. No data is bought in, scraped or cold called. The owner ticks two separate consent boxes, and the wording and timestamp are supplied with the lead.
What details does a Loanleaf lead include?
Each lead includes the loan amount, purpose, timeline, company structure, annual revenue band, time trading band, credit history, existing finance, and the owner's name, business name, UK mobile and email. You also get the source page, timestamp, consent record and a quality score from 0 to 100. The form does not ask about card takings, property value, sector or bank statements, so you cover those on the first call.
How quickly should I contact a new lead?
As quickly as you can do it well, ideally within minutes of the lead arriving. Owners who have just enquired are thinking about finance at that moment, and interest fades. Read the lead details first, so your call reflects what they told you, and make several attempts across different times of day.
Can I get a refund on a lead?
Only in limited circumstances. Within 72 hours of purchase, you can request a refund if the phone number is not in service or belongs to someone else, or if the email address does not exist. Refunds are not given for any other reason, including leads that do not convert.
Do I need to be FCA authorised to buy leads from Loanleaf?
Loanleaf is an introducer and is not authorised by the FCA, and it does not give advice. You are responsible for your own regulatory position, including FCA authorisation where your activities require it. If you are unsure what applies to your firm, take advice from a qualified professional.
Is buying leads cheaper than running my own marketing?
It depends on your size, skills and goals. Running your own marketing needs time, expertise and a budget that may take months to pay back, though it builds an asset you own. Buying leads is quicker to start and easier to scale, but you pay per enquiry and own none of the marketing. Many brokers do both, comparing the two on cost per completed deal.
How do I measure whether a lead source is working?
Track spend, enquiries, conversations, applications and funded deals for each source, over several months. Divide total spend by funded deals to get cost per completed deal, then compare it with the commission earned. The cost per lead calculator can help you test the numbers.
Who is Loanleaf lead generation not suitable for?
It is not suitable if you work only with sole traders, ordinary partnerships or businesses that have not started trading, because those enquiries are turned away on the form. It is also a weaker fit if you need details such as property value or sector before you ring, or if you cannot follow up quickly. Starting with a small number of leads is a low-risk way to test the fit.
