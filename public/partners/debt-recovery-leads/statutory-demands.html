Why the dispute question matters

Whether a debt is disputed changes the route a matter is likely to take. An undisputed debt may suit a letter before action followed quickly by a claim, while a disputed one may need a closer look at the contract, the correspondence and the evidence. Knowing the answer before you call helps you decide who in your team should speak to the client and how to frame the first conversation.

Age of the debt

The age of the debt helps you judge urgency and spot limitation issues early. In England and Wales, most claims for a contract debt must be brought within six years of the date the money became due, although a written acknowledgment or a part payment can restart the clock. Older debts may also be harder to collect in practice, which is worth exploring with the client.

What clients ask

What is a statutory demand?
It is a formal written demand for payment of a debt, made in a form set by insolvency law. It gives the debtor 21 days to pay, or to secure or reach an agreed arrangement for the debt. If the debtor does not, the creditor may be able to present a winding up petition against a company or a bankruptcy petition against an individual.
Do I need a court judgment first?
No. A statutory demand can be served without first obtaining a judgment, which is one reason some creditors use it. However, it is only suitable for debts that are clearly due and not genuinely disputed. If there is any real dispute, a court claim is usually the safer route.
What is the minimum debt for a statutory demand?
For a winding up petition against a company, the debt must be more than £750. For a bankruptcy petition against an individual, including a sole trader, the minimum is much higher and is set by law. That figure has changed before, so your solicitor will confirm the current amount.
Is a statutory demand issued by the court?
No. It is prepared by the creditor or its solicitor and served directly on the debtor. The court only becomes involved if the debtor applies to set it aside, applies to stop a petition, or if the creditor goes on to present a petition. This is why there is no court fee for serving a demand.
Can I use a statutory demand for a claim for damages?
Usually not. A statutory demand must be for a liquidated sum, meaning a fixed amount that is due, such as an unpaid invoice. A claim for damages that still needs to be valued by the court is not suitable. Your solicitor will check whether your debt fits.
Is a statutory demand the same as a letter before action?
No. A letter before action is a step before an ordinary court claim and gives the debtor a chance to respond. A statutory demand is an insolvency tool that can lead to winding up or bankruptcy. Some creditors send a letter before action first, and then consider a statutory demand if the debt is undisputed and remains unpaid.
Can a statutory demand be used on a debt owed by a sole trader?
Yes. A sole trader is an individual, so a statutory demand served on them can lead to a bankruptcy petition if the debt meets the bankruptcy threshold and is not paid. Different rules on service and challenges apply to individuals. Your solicitor will explain how this works and whether it is proportionate.
How is a statutory demand served on a company?
It is usually served by leaving it at the company's registered office, as shown on the public register. Your solicitor will check the address, arrange delivery, often through a process server, and keep evidence of when and how it was served. That evidence is needed if a petition follows.
How is a statutory demand served on a sole trader?
For an individual, the demand should usually be handed to them personally where possible. If that cannot be done after reasonable attempts, the rules allow other methods, but the creditor must be able to show the court what efforts were made. Your solicitor will arrange this carefully.
Can I email a statutory demand?
Email alone is not usually enough. The rules on service are strict, and a petition based on a demand that was not properly served can fail. Your solicitor will use a method of service that the court accepts and will keep a clear record of it.
What must the demand say?
It must follow a prescribed form and include details of the creditor and debtor, the amount owed and how it arose, any interest or charges and how they were calculated, and information about how the debtor can respond. Your solicitor will prepare the demand so that it meets these requirements.
Can I include interest in the demand?
Yes, if the interest is properly due, for example under your contract or under late payment law for debts between businesses. It must be shown separately with an explanation of how it was calculated. Including sums that are not due can give the debtor grounds to challenge the demand.
What if the registered office is out of date?
A company is responsible for keeping its registered office up to date, and service there is usually valid even if the company no longer uses that address. However, a creditor should not ignore information it knows about. Your solicitor may also send a copy to the trading address to make sure the demand comes to the directors' attention.
What happens if the company does not pay?
If a company owing more than £750 does not pay or deal with the demand within 21 days, it can be treated as unable to pay its debts. The creditor may then be able to present a winding up petition. Many creditors use this point to negotiate before deciding whether to petition.
Do I have to petition after 21 days?
No. The demand gives you the option, not an obligation. Some creditors use the demand to prompt payment or a payment plan and never petition. Others decide that a petition is the right step. Your solicitor will discuss the costs and likely outcome of each option with you.
Can the debtor pay in instalments instead?
The demand can be dealt with by securing or compounding the debt to the creditor's reasonable satisfaction, which can include an agreed payment plan. Whether to accept a plan is the creditor's decision. Your solicitor can help set out the terms in writing.
How long is a statutory demand valid for?
There is no simple expiry date, but a demand that is relied on long after it was served may be looked at critically, especially if circumstances have changed. If you intend to petition, it is usually sensible to do so within a reasonable time after the 21 days end. Your solicitor will advise on timing.
What if the company goes into administration?
If a company enters administration or another insolvency process, there are often restrictions on creditors taking action, including presenting a winding up petition. You would usually need to deal with the insolvency practitioner instead. Your solicitor will explain how this affects your debt.
Will I get paid first if I petition?
Not necessarily. In a liquidation, unsecured creditors usually share what is left after costs and certain preferred debts, regardless of who brought the petition. The petitioning creditor can usually recover its petition costs from the company's assets. The real leverage often comes before the hearing.
What if the debtor pays part of the debt?
A part payment reduces the debt but may not deal with the demand. If the balance owed by a company is still more than £750, a petition may still be possible. If it falls below the threshold, it may not. Your solicitor will look at what has been paid and explain the options for the remaining balance.
What if the debtor disputes the debt?
If the dispute is genuine and substantial, a statutory demand is usually not appropriate. A company can ask the court to stop a petition, and the creditor may be ordered to pay its costs. An individual can apply to set the demand aside. A court claim is usually the better way to resolve a real dispute.
Can a company set aside a statutory demand?
Not in the way an individual can. Instead, a company that disputes the debt can apply to the court for an order preventing the creditor from presenting a winding up petition. If the court finds a substantial dispute, the creditor is likely to face a costs order.
How does a sole trader challenge a demand?
An individual can apply to the court to set aside the demand, usually within 18 days of service. Grounds include a genuine dispute, a counterclaim or set off that equals or exceeds the debt, or the creditor holding enough security. The time for compliance is paused while the application is decided.
What counts as a genuine dispute?
It is not enough for a debtor simply to say it disputes the debt. The dispute must have some real substance, supported by evidence. Courts look at the facts of each case. Your solicitor will review the history of the debt and any complaints made to judge whether a statutory demand is safe to use.
What if the debtor has a counterclaim?
If the debtor has a genuine claim against you that is equal to or more than the debt, that can be a reason for the court to stop a petition or set aside a demand. A small or weak counterclaim may not be. Your solicitor will consider any counterclaim before advising.
Can the debtor raise a dispute for the first time after the demand?
It can try, but courts look at the history. A dispute that appears only after a statutory demand, with no earlier complaint, may carry less weight than one raised at the time. However, it still needs to be considered on its merits. Your solicitor will review any new dispute before you decide whether to petition.
