Where they are in the process

Some clients enquire as soon as an invoice is a few weeks overdue and want a solicitor's letter to prompt payment. Others have chased for months, tried a collection agency and now want to issue a claim. A smaller group already hold a judgment that has not been paid and want help with enforcement. What clients care about

Business creditors want three things. They want to know whether it is worth pursuing, what it will cost and how quickly something will happen. Many have been put off by open ended hourly rates, so a fixed fee for a letter before action or a clear scale for issuing a claim tends to land well. They also want to know whether they can recover interest and costs from the debtor, which you can explain on the first call.

Some clients have already used a collection agency or tried the online money claim service themselves. Knowing what has been tried before helps you explain what a law firm can add, and the description in the client's own words often covers this.

Why the dispute question matters

Whether a debt is disputed changes the route a matter is likely to take. An undisputed debt may suit a letter before action followed quickly by a claim, while a disputed one may need a closer look at the contract, the correspondence and the evidence. Knowing the answer before you call helps you decide who in your team should speak to the client and how to frame the first conversation.

Age of the debt

The age of the debt helps you judge urgency and spot limitation issues early. In England and Wales, most claims for a contract debt must be brought within six years of the date the money became due, although a written acknowledgment or a part payment can restart the clock. Older debts may also be harder to collect in practice, which is worth exploring with the client.

What clients ask

Do I need to send a letter before issuing a claim?
Courts expect parties to exchange information and try to settle before a claim is issued. Going straight to court without a proper letter can lead to costs penalties or a pause in the claim. In almost every debt case, a solicitor will make sure a letter before action has been sent and the deadline has passed before issuing.
Is it worth suing if the debtor has no money?
A judgment is only valuable if the debtor can pay or has assets that can be reached. Before issuing, many solicitors check public records such as filed accounts, existing judgments and insolvency notices. If a debtor appears to have nothing, a solicitor may advise that a claim is unlikely to be worthwhile, which can save you money.
Which court will my claim be in?
Most business debt claims start in the County Court, often through a central online service, and may move to a local hearing centre if defended. Higher value or more complex claims can be started in the High Court. Your solicitor will choose the right court based on the amount, the complexity and any enforcement plans.
Can I bring a claim myself?
Businesses can bring claims without a solicitor, and some do for small debts. A solicitor can help make sure the claim is against the right party, the amount and interest are correct and the procedure is followed. That reduces the risk of delay or costs penalties, especially if the debtor defends.
How long do I have to start a claim?
For a debt under a contract, a claim must usually be issued within six years of the date payment fell due. A written acknowledgement of the debt by the debtor, or a part payment, can restart the clock. If your debt is approaching six years old, speak to a solicitor promptly.
Can I claim against more than one party?
Sometimes. For example, a creditor might claim against a company and against a director who signed a personal guarantee. Claims against several parties can be more complex and may not be suitable for the simplest online route. Your solicitor will explain who can be named and how.
What if the debtor is based outside England and Wales?
Claims against debtors based elsewhere can be more complex. Different rules apply to serving the claim and to enforcing a judgment abroad, and sometimes the contract says which courts should deal with disputes. Your solicitor will check your contract and explain whether a claim in England and Wales is the right approach.
What goes into a claim?
A claim usually includes a claim form and particulars of claim. Together they identify the parties, explain the contract and the debt, set out the amount owed and any interest claimed, and include a statement of truth. Your solicitor will draft these from your invoices, terms and correspondence.
Can I claim interest on the debt?
Usually, yes. Interest may be claimed under your contract, under late payment law for debts between businesses, or at the court's discretion. The basis must be explained in the claim. Your solicitor will choose the right basis and calculate the interest up to the date of issue and the daily rate after that.
How is the claim served on the debtor?
Often the court sends the claim to the debtor by post. In some cases the creditor's solicitor serves it instead. A company is commonly served at its registered office. The date of service matters because it starts the time for the debtor to respond, so it must be done correctly.
What is a statement of truth?
It is a short statement at the end of court documents confirming that the person signing believes the facts in the document are true. Making a false statement without an honest belief in its truth can lead to proceedings for contempt of court. Your solicitor will go through the facts with you before anything is signed.
Can the debtor pay after the claim is issued?
Yes, and many do. If the debtor pays the full amount claimed, including interest, court fees and fixed costs, the claim ends. If it pays only part, the claim may continue for the balance. Your solicitor will check what has been received and tell the court if the claim is settled.
Can I withdraw a claim once it has started?
A claimant can usually discontinue a claim, but doing so may mean paying the debtor's costs up to that point unless the parties agree otherwise. If the debtor has paid or a settlement has been reached, the solicitor will record the terms so that the claim is closed properly.
What happens if the debtor defends?
The court allocates the claim to a track, often after asking both sides for information. It may refer the parties to mediation and will set a timetable for exchanging documents and evidence. If the claim does not settle, it goes to a hearing. Your solicitor will explain each stage and its likely cost.
What is the small claims track?
It is the track for lower value claims, designed to be simpler and less formal. The legal costs that the winner can recover from the loser are very limited, so a business may not get much of its legal spend back. The financial limit for the track is set by the court rules, and your solicitor will tell you whether it applies.
Will I have to go to mediation?
In many lower value money claims, parties are now expected to attend a short mediation appointment run by the court service before the case goes further. In higher value claims, the court expects parties to consider settlement and may impose costs consequences on a party that unreasonably refuses. Your solicitor will explain what applies.
What is summary judgment?
Summary judgment allows the court to decide a claim without a full trial if the defence has no real prospect of success and there is no other reason for a trial. It can save time and cost where a defence is weak, but it is not suitable where there is a genuine factual dispute. Your solicitor will advise if it is an option.
Can the debtor bring a counterclaim?
Yes. A debtor may say it has its own claim against you, for example for late delivery or defective work. A counterclaim is dealt with alongside your claim and can affect the overall result. Your solicitor will review any counterclaim and explain how it changes the picture.
Will I need to give evidence?
If a defended claim goes to a hearing, someone from your business who knows the facts may need to provide a witness statement and attend to answer questions. Your solicitor will help prepare the statement and explain what to expect, so that you are ready if the case gets that far.
Can the claim settle before the hearing?
Yes, and many defended claims do. Settlement can happen at any stage, through negotiation, mediation or a formal offer. The terms are usually recorded in writing, and sometimes in a court order, so that they can be enforced if the debtor does not keep to them. Your solicitor will advise on any offer you receive.
What is a default judgment?
It is a judgment entered because the debtor did not respond to the claim in time. For a claim for a fixed sum, it can usually be obtained without a hearing. It orders the debtor to pay, and if it is not paid it can be enforced using the court's enforcement tools.
What happens if the debtor does not pay the judgment?
The creditor can use enforcement methods, such as instructing enforcement agents to take goods, obtaining a charging order over property, freezing money in a bank account or requiring the debtor to give information about its finances. Your solicitor will recommend the method most likely to work for your debtor.
Will the judgment be made public?
County Court judgments that are not paid within a set period are usually entered on the public Register of Judgments. This can affect the debtor's ability to get credit and is one reason many debtors pay promptly after judgment. Your solicitor can explain how registration works in your case.
Can a default judgment be overturned?
A debtor can apply to set it aside, for example if the claim was not properly served or if it has a real prospect of defending the claim and applies promptly. If that happens, your solicitor will explain the strength of the application and how to respond.
How long does the debtor have to pay after judgment?
A judgment usually orders payment either straight away or within a set period, often 14 days, unless the court has allowed instalments. If payment is not made by the date ordered, the creditor can start enforcement. Your solicitor will tell you the date in your judgment and what can be done once it passes.
Can I enforce a judgment against a sole trader?
Yes. A sole trader is personally liable for the debts of the business, so a judgment can be enforced against their personal assets as well as the business. Some methods, such as an attachment of earnings order, are only available against individuals. Your solicitor will explain which methods are available.
