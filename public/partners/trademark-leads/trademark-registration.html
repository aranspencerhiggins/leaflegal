Exclusive trademark registration enquiries from people who have asked to speak to a solicitor. Registration enquiries come from founders and growing brands who are ready to file, which makes them quick, clear instructions.

What the client is looking for

Registering your brand name, logo or slogan as a UK trademark.

A registered trademark gives you the exclusive right to use your brand for the goods and services it covers, across the whole of the UK. Applications are made to the UK Intellectual Property Office, which examines them, publishes them for two months so others can oppose, and then registers the mark for ten years at a time.

What the client expects from you

  • Checking whether your name or logo is distinctive enough
  • Clearance searches against earlier marks
  • Choosing classes and drafting the goods and services
  • Filing at the UK IPO and dealing with the examiner
  • International filings through the Madrid system

  • Filing, search, opposition, infringement, renewal, copyright or design
  • Whether they have filed or registered already
  • Any UK IPO deadline or letter received
  • Business or individual, and where they trade
  • Verified UK mobile and email
  • Recorded consent to be contacted

Leaflegal sends trademark and IP enquiries from businesses and individuals across the UK who want to pay a professional to protect or defend a brand, creative work or product design. They come from business owners preparing to launch, growing brands, online sellers, product businesses, agencies and creators. Each enquiry goes to one firm only. It is never sent to several firms at once and it is never resold after delivery.

Trademark clients usually come to us at a clear decision point. Some are about to spend money on a name and want to know it is safe. Some have received an examination report, a notice of opposition or a letter from a competitor and are working to a fixed deadline. Others have found copies of their products online and want them taken down. These are people who already know they need help and are ready to talk about it.

Because there is one UK trademark register, enquiries can come from England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. You choose the matter types you want and whether you take enquiries from anywhere in the UK or only certain regions.

We introduce trademark enquiries to solicitors regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and to chartered trade mark attorneys regulated by IPReg. Both are well placed to handle searches, filings and UK IPO proceedings. Where a matter is likely to need court action, firms that can litigate, or that work with litigators, are often the best fit, and you can tell us which matter types you want.

How the enquiries are generated

Our campaigns run on search, social media and helpful content aimed at the moments people need trademark help, such as searching for how to trademark a name, how to oppose a trademark or what to do about a cease and desist letter. Trademark clients are often at the start of a long relationship with their adviser. A business that files its first mark today may need logos, new product names, international filings, watching, renewals and the occasional dispute over the years that follow. When an enquiry goes to several firms at once, the client tends to choose on price alone. When it goes to one firm, you have the space to explain your approach, build trust and win the relationship as well as the first instruction.

Many trademark enquiries also arrive with a deadline. An examination report, an opposition window or a reply date in a cease and desist letter gives the client a reason to act now rather than later. Exclusive introductions mean you are not racing other firms to the phone while the client is trying to meet that deadline.

What clients ask

Do I need to search before I apply?
It is not compulsory, but it is usually wise. The UK IPO does not refuse an application just because a similar earlier mark exists. Instead, the owner of that mark may oppose. A search helps you avoid paying for an application that is likely to be challenged, or a brand that you may later have to change.
Can I apply for a name I haven't started using yet?
Yes. You can apply for a mark you intend to use. You need a genuine intention to use it for the goods and services listed, and if you don't use it within five years of registration it becomes vulnerable to being revoked.
Who should own the trademark?
Usually the business that will use the brand, such as your limited company. Some owners file in their own name and license the company to use it, which can have tax or planning reasons. The firm can explain the options. Getting this right early saves work during investment or a sale.
What makes a mark distinctive?
A mark is distinctive if customers would see it as a sign of where the goods come from rather than a description of them. Invented words and words unrelated to the goods are strongest. Descriptive words, laudatory terms like best or premium and common shapes are weakest. The firm can tell you where your mark sits.
Should I file a word mark or a logo first?
If budget allows only one, many firms suggest the word mark because it protects the name whatever the styling. If the name itself is weak but the logo is distinctive, a logo mark may be easier to register. The firm will explain the trade off for your brand.
Can I trademark my own name?
Often yes, if it is distinctive for your goods or services and nobody has earlier rights in the same or a similar name. Common surnames can be harder. Others with the same name may still be able to use it honestly in some circumstances.
How long before launch should I file?
As early as you can once you are confident in the name. Your rights run from the filing date, so filing before launch reduces the risk that someone else files first or starts using something similar. It also gives you time to deal with any objection before your marketing starts.
Is it worth registering if I am a small business?
Many small businesses find it worthwhile, because a brand is often one of their most valuable assets and copying can hurt a small business more than a large one. A registration also makes it much easier to deal with copies on online marketplaces. The firm can help you decide what level of protection makes sense for your budget.
How do I choose the right classes?
Start from what you sell and plan to sell. The firm will map that to the right classes and draft wording that covers your business properly. Many businesses need two or three classes. Some need more. Each extra class adds to the official fee, so it is worth focusing on the most important ones.
Can I change my application after filing?
You can narrow the goods and services or correct minor errors, but you cannot add new goods or services or make significant changes to the mark. That is why the specification and the representation of the mark are checked carefully before filing.
What is a series application?
A series lets you register up to six marks that are very similar, differing only in small details such as colour or the presence of a word that doesn't change the overall identity, in one application. It is not suitable for every set of marks, and the examiner decides whether they qualify.
Can I file in my business name before the company is formed?
The applicant must be a person or an existing legal entity. If the company isn't formed yet, the mark can be filed in your own name and assigned to the company later. The assignment should be recorded at the UK IPO.
Is my application public?
Yes. Once filed, application details including the owner's name and address appear on the public register. Firms often act as the address for service so that official letters go to them. Be wary of unsolicited invoices that look official after you file.
What if I already have an EU trademark?
EU trademarks registered before the end of the Brexit transition period were cloned into comparable UK marks automatically. Applications filed after that date need a separate UK application to protect the brand in the UK. A firm can check what you already hold.
Can a firm act for me if I am outside the UK?
Yes. A UK address for service is needed for UK trademark matters, and a UK firm can provide it. Overseas businesses often use a UK solicitor or attorney to file and look after their UK marks.
What happens if the examiner objects?
The UK IPO sends an examination report explaining the objection and setting a deadline to reply. The firm can argue that the mark is acceptable, provide evidence that it has become distinctive through use, narrow the specification or suggest changes. Many objections are resolved this way.
What is a notice of threatened opposition?
During the first two months after publication, someone considering an opposition can file a notice of threatened opposition, which extends their deadline to three months. It is often a sign that the other side wants to negotiate. The firm can contact them to understand their concerns.
Can an opposition be settled?
Yes, and many are. Common outcomes include narrowing the goods and services, agreeing how each side will use its mark, or a coexistence agreement. A cooling off period can sometimes be agreed to give time for talks before formal steps are taken.
What if I lose an opposition?
The application may be refused in whole or in part, and you may have to pay a contribution to the other side's costs, which at the UK IPO is usually set on a published scale. You may still be able to use the brand in some circumstances, but the firm should advise on the risk first.
Does the UK IPO tell me about similar marks?
The UK IPO searches its records and may send you a report listing earlier marks that could be similar. You can then decide whether to go ahead. If you do, the owners of those earlier marks may be notified and have the chance to oppose.
How long do I have to reply to an examination report?
The report sets a deadline, usually a couple of months. It can sometimes be extended if there is a good reason. If you don't reply in time, the application may be refused, so pass the report to the firm as soon as you receive it.
Can I use evidence that my brand is already well known?
Yes. If the examiner objects that a mark is not distinctive, evidence that customers have come to recognise it through your use can sometimes overcome the objection. The evidence needs to be detailed, such as sales figures, advertising spend and examples of use over time. The firm will explain what is needed.
How long does a trademark last?
A UK registration lasts ten years from the filing date and can be renewed every ten years indefinitely, as long as the renewal fees are paid. There is a six month grace period after expiry with a late fee.
Do I have to use my registered trademark?
Yes, to keep it secure. If a registered mark is not genuinely used in the UK for the registered goods or services for a continuous five year period, anyone can apply to revoke it. Keep dated evidence of use.
Can I add new products to my registration?
No. To cover new goods or services you need a new application. Many businesses review their portfolio when they launch a new line and file again for the new classes.
Can I sell or license my registered mark?
Yes. A registered mark can be assigned, licensed or used as security, like other property. Assignments and licences should be in writing and recorded at the UK IPO so the register reflects who owns and uses the mark.
Does registration stop all similar names?
Not automatically. Registration gives you the right to act, but you need to watch for conflicts and take action. Others may also have earlier rights or be able to use similar signs in ways that don't cause confusion.
What should I do with my registration certificate?
Keep it safely with your business records, along with the filing receipt and any correspondence. Note the renewal date in your diary. Investors, buyers and online marketplaces may ask for your registration number, which you can also find on the public register.
Can I register a trademark for a brand I sell online only?
Yes. Online sellers can and often do register their marks, and many marketplaces offer extra brand protection tools to owners of registered trademarks. Make sure the specification covers both the goods you sell and, where relevant, your online retail services.
