Everything you need to know

Not every enquiry instructs on the first call. Some people do not answer at all. Some have a helpful conversation and then go quiet. Some say they need to think, talk to a partner, check their finances or wait for a letter to arrive. Following up is how you stay in touch with these people in a way that is helpful, polite and well timed, so that when they are ready, your firm is the one they come back to.

Many firms do not follow up at all, or they try once and stop. Others follow up so often that the client feels chased. The aim of this guide is to help you find the middle ground. A planned sequence of a few calls, texts and emails over about a week, with useful content rather than reminders, and a clear point at which you stop.

Why people go quiet

It is rarely because they have decided against you. More often, life has got in the way. Common reasons include the following.

  • They are at work and cannot take personal calls
  • They do not answer unknown numbers
  • They need to speak to a partner or family member first
  • They are waiting for a document, a letter or a date
  • They are gathering money for fees
  • They are emotionally not ready to take the next step
  • They are comparing a few firms and have not decided yet
  • They simply forgot

Most of these are temporary. A good follow-up sequence gives the client easy ways to get back in touch when the obstacle has passed, without making them feel they owe you a response.

What this guide covers

It sets out a simple sequence you can adapt, gives example messages, looks at how tone should change between practice areas, explains the consent and data points to keep in mind, and covers matters that naturally take weeks or months to come to a decision. For what happens on the call itself, see our first call guide.

A follow-up sequence works best when it is planned in advance and the same for every lead, adjusted only for urgency and the client's preferred time to call. That way nothing depends on someone remembering.

A simple sequence for unanswered leads

  • Straight away, call. If no answer, leave a short voicemail and send a text and an email
  • Later the same day, call again at a different time
  • The next morning, call again and, if no answer, send a short text
  • Two or three days later, send a helpful email with your fees, the next step and how to book
  • About a week after the enquiry, a final call and a short closing message

That gives five or six touches over roughly a week, across three channels and at different times of day. For most practice areas it is enough to reach the majority of people who want to be reached, without becoming intrusive.

A sequence for clients who said not yet

If you have spoken to the client and they want time, the sequence is different. Agree a specific date to check in during the call. Send a summary email the same day with your fees and next steps. Then call on the agreed date. If they are still not ready, ask when would be a better time and agree another date. Two or three of these check-ins is usually reasonable before a final message.

Speed up for urgent matters

Where there is a hearing, refusal date, completion date or licence review coming up, compress the sequence. Several attempts on the first day and a follow-up first thing the next morning are appropriate, because the client's options may narrow quickly. Each Leaflegal lead includes urgency and any known date, so your team can tell which sequence to use.

Know when to stop

After the final message, stop. Continuing to call someone who has not responded to several attempts rarely helps and can damage your firm's reputation. The final message should leave the door open, with a direct number and email, so the client can come back if their situation changes.

Put it in the system

Set each step up as a task in your CRM or case management system, or as calendar reminders, so it happens on time whoever is in the office. Log each attempt with the date, time and method. That stops two people calling the same client within minutes of each other and shows you later which steps actually get responses.

These examples show the kind of tone and length that tends to work. Adapt them to your firm's voice and your practice area. Keep every message short, discreet and clear about who is getting in touch and why.

Text after a missed first call

'Hi Sam, this is Alex from Example Solicitors. You made an enquiry through Leaflegal and asked a solicitor to call you. I tried you just now. Is there a good time to talk today? You can reach me directly on this number.'

Voicemail

'Hello, this is Alex from Example Solicitors, calling for Sam about the enquiry you made through Leaflegal. I will send you a text and an email with my details. My direct number is [number]. Thanks, speak soon.' Keep it brief and avoid describing the matter, in case someone else hears it.

Helpful follow-up email

A good follow-up email does more than say you tried to call. It gives the client something useful. Briefly explain how this kind of matter usually works at your firm, what the first step involves, what it costs and how to book it. Add a link to book a call or a simple request to reply with a good time. Sign it with a named person and a direct line.

Check-in after a not yet

'Hi Sam, I said I would check in this week about your lease extension. No pressure at all. If you are ready to go ahead, I can send the engagement letter today. If you need a bit longer, just let me know when would be a better time to talk.'

Final message

'Hi Sam, I have tried to reach you a few times about your enquiry and do not want to keep chasing you. If you would still like help, my direct number and email are below and I would be glad to talk whenever suits you. All the best.' A friendly, final message is often the one that gets a reply.

What to avoid

  • Messages that sound automated or salesy
  • Details of the matter in texts or voicemails
  • Pressure, such as suggesting the client will lose out if they do not reply today, unless there is a real deadline
  • Long emails full of legal explanation
  • Messages from a generic address with no named person

The same sequence can feel very different depending on the type of matter. Adjust your tone and timing to suit the client's situation.

Family law

Clients are often going through one of the hardest periods of their lives. They may not be ready, and they may not be able to talk freely at home. Keep messages gentle and discreet, never mention the matter in a text, and give them time. Respect the best time to call on the lead. Some clients will come back weeks later because a gentle message made them feel the firm was there when they needed it.

Immigration

Many clients are working to a deadline and may have limited English or be outside the UK. Clear, simple messages work best. If your team speaks other languages, say so. Follow up quickly where there is a refusal or expiry date, and be careful not to imply any view on eligibility in a follow-up message.

Conveyancing

Buyers and sellers are often comparing quotes and want speed. Follow up with a clear itemised quote and an offer to answer questions. A short check-in a day or two after sending the quote is usually welcome, as long as it adds something, such as confirming how quickly you can start.

Licensing and debt recovery

These clients are business owners. They value brief, professional messages that respect their time. Mention any hearing date, deadline or next step clearly, and offer a short call at a time that suits them.

Lease extensions

Flat owners are often weighing up cost and timing, particularly if a sale or remortgage is not yet fixed. They may take weeks to decide. A slower, informative follow-up that explains the usual steps and the role of the valuer tends to work better than frequent calls.

Following up is part of responding to an enquiry, but it still has to be done within the law and your professional obligations.

What the client agreed to

Every Leaflegal client ticks a separate box agreeing that their details can be shared with a firm we match them with, and that the firm can contact them by phone, email or text about their matter. We record the exact wording and the time, and send it with each lead. That consent covers contact about their enquiry.

Following up is not marketing

Calling, texting and emailing about the enquiry the client made is different from sending them marketing. Adding someone to a newsletter, sending general offers or contacting them about other services needs separate consent under data protection and electronic marketing rules. Do not assume the lead consent covers it.

Respect a request to stop

If the client says they do not want to hear from you again, stop straight away and record it. Continuing after a clear request is poor practice and may breach data protection law.

Keep data secure

Once a lead is delivered, your firm is the data controller for your copy and is responsible for handling it in line with UK data protection law and your own privacy notice. Some leads include sensitive details, especially in family and immigration matters. Keep them in secure systems, limit who can see them and follow your retention policy for enquiries that never instruct.

Be careful with shared devices and numbers

A text or voicemail might be seen by a partner, parent or employer. Keep messages discreet and never include details of the matter. If someone else answers, ask for the client by name and do not explain why you are calling.

Some matters naturally take weeks or months to come to a decision. Following up on these is less about a quick sequence and more about staying helpfully in touch.

Which matters take longer

  • Prenuptial and postnuptial agreements, where the wedding may be months away
  • Separation where the couple has not yet decided to divorce
  • Lease extensions where a sale or remortgage is not yet planned
  • Debt recovery where the business is still trying to settle informally
  • Sponsor licences where the business is still planning its recruitment

Agree a timescale

On the first call, ask when the client expects to be ready and agree a date to check in. Put it in your diary or CRM. A client who said they would be ready after Christmas will remember that you remembered.

Send something useful

Where you have their agreement, a short check-in with something relevant, such as a reminder of what documents to gather or how long the process usually takes, is more welcome than a bare question about whether they are ready. Keep it about their enquiry, not general marketing.

Know when the lead has gone cold

If several months pass and repeated check-ins get no response, send a final message and close the file. Record the outcome so you can see how often long cycle enquiries come back, which helps you decide how much effort to put into them in future.

Tell us what you see

If certain matter types usually take a long time to convert for your firm, tell us. We can adjust your criteria or volume so your team spends its time where it gets the best results.

Questions and answers

How many times should we try?
Four or five attempts over a week is reasonable.
Can we add them to a newsletter?
Only with separate consent.
Why should we follow up legal enquiries?
Because many people do not instruct on the first contact, even when they want help. They may not answer an unknown number, need to speak to a partner or be waiting for money or documents. A planned, polite follow-up gives them an easy way back when they are ready. Firms that follow up consistently usually win work that firms who try once never see.
How many times should we try to contact a new lead?
For most matters, five or six attempts over about a week is reasonable, using calls, texts and emails at different times of day. Urgent matters with a hearing or deadline justify more attempts in a shorter time. After your final message, stop. Continuing beyond that rarely helps and can put people off your firm.
How long should a follow-up sequence last?
About a week for clients you have not yet spoken to. For clients who said they need time, agree a check-in date and follow that instead, which might be weeks or months away. Matters like prenuptial agreements or lease extensions without a sale can take a long time to decide, so the follow-up should match the client's own timescale.
Who should do the follow-up?
Ideally the person who made the first contact, so the client hears a familiar name. If that is not possible, make sure whoever follows up has read the lead and any notes from earlier calls. Set follow-up steps as tasks in your CRM or diary so they happen whoever is in the office that day.
What if the client answers but says they are not ready?
Thank them, ask when would be a good time to check in and agree a specific date. Send a short summary email the same day with your fees and next steps, so they have the information to hand. Then call on the agreed date. Respecting their timescale builds trust and makes it more likely they will come back to you.
Is it pushy to keep following up?
Not if it is planned, polite and useful. Pushiness comes from tone and frequency, not from following up as such. A few well spaced messages that offer something helpful and make it easy to reply feel like good service. Many calls in quick succession with no new information, or messages that create false urgency, feel pushy.
Should we call, text or email?
Use all three. Calls start real conversations, texts are often read when calls are ignored, and emails give the client something to refer back to. A typical pattern is to call first, then text and email if there is no answer. Some clients will reply to a text when they would never return a call, so do not rely on one channel.
Do texts work for legal enquiries?
They often do, especially as a way to tell the client who is calling and why. Keep texts short, discreet and from a recognisable sender. Never include details of the matter in a text, because other people may see the phone. A text with your name, firm and direct number often prompts a reply or a returned call.
Should we use WhatsApp?
Some firms do, and some clients prefer it, particularly in immigration matters. Before using it, check your firm's policies on messaging apps, record keeping and data security. Make sure the client is happy to be contacted that way. If you use it, keep the same discretion as with texts and keep a record of what is said.
Is voicemail worth leaving?
Yes, as long as it is short and discreet. Give your name, firm, the fact that you are calling about their enquiry through Leaflegal and a direct number, said slowly. Do not mention the matter itself. Many people listen to voicemail before deciding whether to call back, so a clear, friendly message helps.
What time of day is best for follow-up calls?
It depends on the client. Use the best time to call on each Leaflegal lead as your guide. Otherwise, vary the time of day between attempts, such as morning, lunchtime and just after working hours, to give yourself the best chance of catching them when they are free. Avoid very early or very late calls.
Should we use automated follow-up emails?
Automation can help make sure nothing is forgotten, but fully automated sequences often feel impersonal for legal matters. Many firms use reminders or templates that a named person reviews and sends. If you do automate, keep messages short, sign them from a real person and stop the sequence as soon as the client responds.
What should a follow-up email include?
Your name and firm, a reminder of their enquiry in general terms, a brief explanation of what the first step involves and what it costs, and an easy way to respond, such as a link to book a call or a request to reply with a good time. Keep it short. Sign it with a named person and a direct contact.
How do we write a final follow-up message?
Keep it friendly and short. Say you have tried a few times, that you do not want to keep chasing, and that you would be happy to help whenever suits them. Include your direct number and email. A message that removes pressure and leaves the door open often prompts a response from people who had meant to reply but had not got round to it.
Should we mention urgency in follow-up messages?
Only where it is real. If the client has a hearing, refusal date or completion date, it is helpful to mention that acting soon gives more options. Do not create false urgency or suggest a limited time offer to push a decision. Clients can usually tell, and it undermines the trust you are trying to build.
Can we include useful information in follow-ups?
Yes, and it usually helps. A short explanation of how this kind of matter typically works at your firm, what documents to gather or how long the first stage usually takes is more welcome than a bare request for a reply. Keep it general and about their enquiry, and save specific advice for a consultation.
Should follow-up messages come from a named person?
Yes. A message from a real person with a direct line feels far more personal than one from a generic inbox, and clients are more likely to reply to it. It also tells them who they will be dealing with if they instruct. Where possible, the same person should send each message in the sequence so the client sees a familiar name.
How formal should our follow-up messages be?
Friendly and professional is usually right. Avoid legal jargon and long sentences, but do not be overly casual either. Match the tone to the client and the matter. A business owner chasing a debt may prefer brief and direct messages, while a family client may need a gentler and warmer tone. Read each message back before sending it.
Can we follow up leads without separate consent?
Leaflegal clients agree that their details can be shared with a firm we match them with, and that the firm can contact them by phone, email or text about their matter. That covers following up about their enquiry. It does not cover general marketing, which needs separate consent. If you are unsure, check your own data protection advice.
Can we add leads to our newsletter?
Only if the client separately agrees. The consent given with a Leaflegal lead covers contact about their enquiry, not marketing emails, offers or newsletters. If you would like to send them marketing, ask for consent in a clear and separate way and keep a record. Many firms simply do not add enquiry contacts to marketing lists unless they become clients.
What if the client asks us to stop contacting them?
Stop straight away and record the request in your system so nobody else contacts them. Thank them politely if you are speaking to them. Continuing after a clear request is poor practice and may breach data protection law. If the reason is that they found help elsewhere, note that too, because it helps you understand your conversion.
How long can we keep details of leads that never instruct?
That depends on your own data protection policies and retention schedule. Once a lead is delivered, your firm is the controller of your copy and responsible for handling it lawfully. Many firms keep enquiry records for a limited period and then delete them. Your data protection adviser can help you set a period that suits your firm.
Do we still need to disclose the referral if the client instructs after follow-up?
Yes. However long the client takes to instruct, if they came to you through Leaflegal the SRA Code of Conduct requires you to make sure they are told about the financial arrangement, in writing. Most firms include it in the client care letter. We send our consent wording with each lead so your disclosure can match it.
Can we record follow-up calls?
Many firms record calls for training and quality purposes. If you do, make sure you have a lawful basis, tell clients in line with your privacy notice and keep recordings secure. Sensitive details often come up in family and immigration calls, so limit who can access recordings and follow your retention policy. Your data protection adviser can confirm what applies to your firm.
Does Leaflegal follow up clients for us?
No. Once a lead is delivered, contact is between your firm and the client. We do not chase clients on your behalf. Each lead includes verified UK mobile and email, the best time to call, urgency and the client's own description, which gives you what you need to follow up well. Your feedback about outcomes helps us improve future leads.
Is a lead invalid if the client never answers?
No. Contact details are verified before we send a lead, and a client not answering, deciding not to proceed or choosing another firm does not make it invalid. If contact details are genuinely wrong, tell us within the review period in your partner agreement and we will deal with it as the agreement sets out.
Can we lower our volume if follow-up is taking too long?
Yes. If your team is struggling to keep up with first calls and follow-ups, lower your monthly cap or pause for a while. The minimum is 10 leads a month. It is better to take fewer leads and follow each one up properly than to take more and let some slip through without a proper sequence.
Will you tell us which leads are most likely to respond?
Each lead shows urgency, any known deadline and the best time to call, which helps you prioritise. We do not score leads or predict who will respond, and we do not publish response rates. Your own records, shared with us, are the best guide to which kinds of enquiry respond well for your firm.
Can we tell you why a lead did not instruct?
Please do. Feedback on which leads you reached, which instructed and why others did not is the most useful thing you can give us. There is no platform to update, so a regular email or a call with our team works well. It helps us adjust your practice areas, matter types and locations to match the work you convert best.
Do clients know a firm will follow up with them?
Yes. Before submitting an enquiry, every client agrees that their details can be shared with a firm we match them with and that the firm can contact them by phone, email or text about their matter. They also confirm they understand Leaflegal is an introduction service. So a polite follow-up from your firm is expected rather than a surprise.

What clients ask

What is a conveyancing lead from Leaflegal?
It is an enquiry from a person who is buying, selling or remortgaging a property in England and Wales and has asked to be contacted by a firm about paid conveyancing. They have answered a short set of questions about the transaction, confirmed their contact details and agreed to be contacted by one firm. We send that enquiry to you alone, as soon as it has been checked.
Are the leads exclusive?
Yes. Each enquiry is matched to one firm only. We do not send the same enquiry to several firms at once and we never resell a lead after it has been delivered. If you are the firm we match, you are the only firm that receives that person's details from us. The client can still choose to speak to other firms on their own, as anyone can.
How do you check the leads?
Every client confirms they want paid legal help with a property in England and Wales. We verify their UK mobile number and their email address, and we filter out enquiries that fall outside our scope or your criteria. People we cannot help are told politely and pointed elsewhere, so they never become a lead. Each lead then arrives with the client's answers and the consent record.
What details will I get for each enquiry?
You get the client's name, mobile and email, whether it is a purchase, sale, both or a remortgage, the rough property value, the tenure, whether there is a mortgage, the target timescale and the location. There is also a short description in the client's own words, the best time to call, the source, a timestamp and the exact consent wording they agreed to.
Can I quote from the lead alone?
Usually you can give a provisional figure on the first call, because the lead covers the transaction type, rough value, tenure and whether a mortgage is involved. You will still want to confirm the details with the client and send a written quote. Things like a leasehold management pack, a new build or a gifted deposit may change your figure once you know more.
Do you send leasehold purchases and sales?
Yes, if you want them. Every lead says whether the property is freehold or leasehold, and you can choose to receive leasehold matters, leave them out or ask for them only in certain areas. Firms with an experienced leasehold team sometimes prefer this work, while others choose to focus on freehold transactions. We set your matching to suit you.
Are remortgage leads included?
Only if you ask for them. Remortgages, transfers of equity, purchases, sales and combined sale and purchase matters can each be switched on or off. Some firms take remortgages to keep a steady flow of shorter matters, while others prefer to concentrate on purchases. You can change the mix at any time by letting us know.
Do clients know a firm will call them?
Yes. Every client has agreed to be contacted by a firm about their matter, by phone, email or text, and most are expecting a call soon after they submit. It helps to say early in the call that you are getting in touch because of the enquiry they made through Leaflegal. That puts the client at ease and moves the conversation quickly on to their purchase, sale or remortgage.
