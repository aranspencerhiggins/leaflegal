Each lead is the complete record of what the client told us, in the order they told us. There is no scraped data and no guesswork, just the answers someone gave when they asked to speak to a solicitor.

    The area of law, the type of matter and a short description in the client’s own words.

    How soon they need help, including any deadline, hearing or court date they know about.

    Where they are, so we only send you clients in the areas you cover, or nationally if you work remotely.

    Name, a UK mobile number and an email address on a real domain, plus the best time to call.

    The exact wording they agreed to and when, so you can contact them with confidence.

Converting an enquiry means turning someone who has asked about a legal problem into a client who has signed your terms and paid, or agreed to pay, for your help. It sounds simple, but there are several steps between the two, and clients can drift away at every one of them. Most firms lose more potential instructions in these gaps than they realise.

The good news is that conversion is rarely about clever sales techniques. It is mostly about doing a handful of ordinary things well and doing them every time. Responding quickly, being clear about cost, making the next step easy, following up when people go quiet and learning from the enquiries that do not instruct. Firms that do these consistently tend to win more work from the same number of enquiries.

This guide looks at the whole path from enquiry to instruction. It covers where enquiries usually drop out, how to run consultations that lead somewhere, how to present fees and proposals, how to remove friction from onboarding and how to measure what is happening. It applies whether your enquiries come from Leaflegal, your own website, referrals or paid search.

Why this matters more than volume

When instructions are lower than expected, the first instinct is often to look for more enquiries. Sometimes that is the answer. But if a firm is losing clients between the first call and the consultation, or between the consultation and the engagement letter, more enquiries simply means more people lost at the same points. Fixing the leaks first makes every future enquiry worth more.

What conversion cannot fix

Some enquiries will never instruct. People change their minds, reconcile, find the money is not there, sort the problem themselves or decide to wait. Good conversion work does not change that. Its aim is to make sure that everyone who could reasonably become a client gets a fair, clear and timely chance to do so. Leaflegal provides qualified enquiries, not guaranteed instructions.

The habits that matter most

  • Speak to every enquiry quickly, ideally within minutes
  • Show the client you have read what they told you
  • Explain fees plainly and confirm them in writing
  • End every call with a booked next step
  • Send terms the same day the client agrees to go ahead
  • Follow up politely when people go quiet
  • Record why people did not instruct and act on it

None of these needs new software or a big budget. They need someone in the firm to own the process, check it is happening and keep the team focused on it when things get busy.

It helps to think of conversion as a series of stages. Each one has its own reasons for people dropping out, and each needs a slightly different fix.

1. Enquiry to contact

The first gap is between the enquiry arriving and someone at your firm actually speaking to the client. Slow responses, missed calls and no follow-up all lose people here. This is often the biggest leak and the easiest to fix. Our speed to lead guide covers it in detail.

2. Contact to consultation

Once you have spoken to the client, the next step is usually a consultation, a quote or a document review. People drop out here when the first call is vague, when cost is unclear or when no specific next step is booked. A client who ends the call with a date in the diary is far more likely to continue than one told you will be in touch.

3. Consultation to proposal

After the consultation, the client needs a clear written proposal or engagement letter. Delays here are surprisingly common. A fee earner who means to send the letter tomorrow, then gets pulled onto urgent work, can lose a client who was ready to sign.

4. Proposal to instruction

The final gap is between sending terms and the client signing, completing ID checks and paying any money on account. Complicated paperwork, unclear payment instructions and slow responses to questions all lose people at the very last step.

Find your biggest leak

Every firm loses people at a different stage. Track a few weeks of enquiries through each step and look at where the numbers fall most sharply. That is where your effort will pay off first. It is usually more useful than trying to improve everything at once.

For many practice areas, the consultation is where the decision to instruct is really made. The client meets the person who will handle their matter, hears how it might go and judges whether they trust the firm.

Make it easy to book

Offer specific times on the first call. Give a choice of in person, phone or video where you can. Send a calendar invite and a reminder the day before. Every extra step between agreeing to a consultation and actually attending one loses some clients.

Be clear about what it costs

Whether your first consultation is free, fixed fee or charged hourly, say so plainly before it is booked. A fixed fee first consultation works well in many areas because it is easy to say yes to, sets expectations and shows that your time has value. A free consultation can attract more bookings but sometimes more no-shows. There is no single right answer.

Prepare properly

Read the lead and any notes from the first call before the consultation starts. Ask the client to send key documents in advance where it helps. Arriving prepared shows the client you have taken their matter seriously and lets you spend the time on what matters to them.

Leave the client with a plan

The best consultations end with the client understanding their position in general terms, the options, the likely steps, the cost of each and what happens next. Write it down for them. A client who leaves with a clear plan and a price is much more likely to instruct than one who leaves with a lot of information and no clear path.

Ask for the instruction

Many fee earners explain everything well and then stop, waiting for the client to raise the subject of instructing. Some clients will. Many will not. It is perfectly professional to ask whether they would like you to act for them and to explain what happens if they do.

If the client is not ready

Some clients leave a consultation wanting time to think, talk to a partner or check their finances. That is reasonable. Agree when you will be in touch, send the written summary and fee proposal anyway, and follow up as arranged. Our guide to following up sets out a simple, polite sequence that keeps the door open without pressure.

Private clients are spending their own money, often at a difficult time. How you present fees has a big effect on whether they go ahead.

Plain and early

Clients want to know what their matter will cost before they commit. Explain your fees in plain English, early in the process, and confirm them in writing. Vague answers make people nervous and often send them to a firm that gave a clearer one, even if it was more expensive.

Fixed fees where you can

Where the work is predictable, fixed fees remove the client's biggest worry. Many firms offer fixed fees for conveyancing, uncontested divorce, motoring hearings, premises licence applications, lease extensions and letters before action. For less predictable work, fixed fees for each stage can give some certainty.

Show what is included

Set out what your fee covers and what it does not, such as disbursements, court fees, search fees, valuer's fees or counsel. Clients comparing quotes often choose the one that seems most complete, so a clear breakdown can win against a lower headline figure.

Send the proposal quickly

The engagement letter or proposal should reach the client while the consultation is still fresh. Many firms aim for the same day. Having templates ready for your common matter types makes that much easier.

Make the letter readable

Engagement letters are often long, legalistic and hard to follow. Keep your regulatory and contractual terms, but put a short summary at the top covering who will do the work, what it costs, what happens next and what the client needs to do. A client who understands the letter is far more likely to sign it promptly.

Include the referral disclosure

Where a client came through an introducer such as Leaflegal, the SRA Code of Conduct requires you to make sure they are told about the financial arrangement, in writing. Most firms include this in the client care letter. We send our consent wording with each lead so your disclosure can match it.

Once a client has decided to instruct you, the job is to make it as easy as possible for them to complete the formalities. Every unnecessary step is a chance for them to delay or drop out.

Common sources of friction

  • Engagement letters that must be printed, signed and posted back
  • ID checks that need an office visit
  • Unclear instructions for paying money on account
  • Several separate emails each asking for one thing
  • No named person to contact with questions
  • Long silences between the client signing and work starting

Practical fixes

  • Use e-signature for engagement letters where your terms allow
  • Offer electronic ID verification, with an in person option for those who need it
  • Put payment details and the amount clearly in one place
  • Send a single onboarding email that lists everything needed
  • Name the fee earner and give a direct contact
  • Call or email within a day of signing to confirm work has started

Keep momentum

Clients who sign and then hear nothing for a week sometimes start to wonder whether they made the right choice. A short message confirming what you are doing first, and when they will next hear from you, keeps their confidence up. It also tends to lead to better reviews later, which in turn help future conversion.

Check your own process

Try going through your own onboarding as if you were a client. Fill in the forms, follow the payment instructions and read the letters. Many firms find steps they did not know were confusing. Asking a recent client what they found difficult can be just as revealing.

Reviews and recommendations

A smooth start to a matter tends to lead to a smoother matter overall, and satisfied clients are more likely to leave a review or recommend you. Those reviews then help future enquiries decide to instruct you. Asking for a review at a natural point, such as completion or the end of a hearing, is a simple habit that supports conversion for years.

You do not need a complicated system to measure conversion. A spreadsheet or a few fields in your CRM are enough to start.

The basic numbers

  • Enquiries received, by source and practice area
  • Enquiries reached, meaning a real conversation took place
  • Consultations or quotes booked
  • Consultations attended
  • Proposals or engagement letters sent
  • Instructions received
  • Reasons for not instructing, where known

Work out your own rates

Divide each stage by the one before to see where people fall away. Do this separately for each practice area and source, because they behave differently. A conveyancing quote and a contested children matter will never convert in the same way, and comparing them tells you little.

Do not rely on other firms' figures

Conversion rates vary so much by area, fee level, location and client type that general figures are not much help. Leaflegal does not publish conversion rates for that reason. Your own numbers, tracked consistently over time, are the only ones that really tell you whether you are improving.

Look at value, not only numbers

Some enquiries are worth far more than others. A firm that converts fewer enquiries but wins more of the high value matters may be doing better than one that converts more simple ones. Track the fee value of instructions alongside the count.

Share what you learn with us

Tell us which Leaflegal leads instructed, which did not and why, where you know. If a particular matter type or location rarely converts for you, we can adjust your criteria so you receive more of the work that does. You can find out more about how pricing works and how criteria affect it.

Questions and answers

What is a good conversion rate?
It varies by area and firm. Measure your own and improve it.
Which areas convert fastest?
Deadline-driven matters such as motoring offences, refusals and licence reviews tend to convert quickly.
What does converting a legal enquiry mean?
It means turning someone who has asked about their legal problem into a client who has accepted your terms and instructed your firm. It usually involves several steps, such as the first call, a consultation or quote, a written proposal and onboarding. Clients can drop out at any of them, so conversion is about making each step clear, quick and easy.
What is a good conversion rate for legal enquiries?
There is no single figure that applies to every firm. It depends heavily on the practice area, your fees, your location and how enquiries reach you. A conveyancing quote and a contested family matter convert very differently. The most useful benchmark is your own rate, tracked consistently over time and broken down by area, so you can see whether changes you make are working.
Why do enquiries not turn into instructions?
Common reasons include slow first contact, unclear fees, no specific next step, delays in sending terms, difficult onboarding and no follow-up when the client goes quiet. Some reasons are outside your control, such as a client changing their mind or deciding they cannot afford it. Recording the reasons you hear helps you see which ones you can do something about.
Which matters convert most quickly?
Matters with a fixed date tend to move fastest, such as motoring offences with a court date, licence reviews, immigration deadlines and conveyancing with an agreed sale. Clients facing those dates need to act. Matters without a deadline, such as prenuptial agreements or some lease extensions, can take longer because the client has time to think and compare.
Is conversion mainly about sales skills?
Not really. Most improvements come from simple, consistent habits rather than persuasion. Calling quickly, explaining fees clearly, booking a next step, sending terms promptly and following up politely make a bigger difference than any sales technique. Clients choosing a solicitor are looking for competence, clarity and trust, and those come across through how your firm behaves.
Will buying more leads fix low instructions?
Not on its own. If your firm is losing clients at a particular stage, more enquiries will usually mean more people lost at the same stage. It is worth finding and fixing your biggest leak first. Once your process converts well, additional enquiries become more valuable, and you can scale up with more confidence.
Should our first consultation be free?
It depends on your area and your firm. A free consultation can attract more bookings but sometimes more no-shows and less committed clients. A fixed fee first consultation is easy to say yes to, sets expectations and values your time. Many firms test both. Whichever you choose, be clear about it before the consultation is booked.
How can we reduce no-shows?
Book a specific time on the first call, send a calendar invite and confirmation straight away, and send a reminder the day before. Offer phone or video as well as in person. Some firms take payment for a fixed fee consultation at booking, which tends to reduce no-shows. If someone does miss an appointment, call them promptly and offer another time without blame.
What should happen at the end of a consultation?
The client should understand their position in general terms, the options open to them, the likely steps and the cost of each. Ideally, they leave with a written summary and a clear question about whether they would like you to act. Ending with a plan and a price makes it much easier for them to say yes.
Is it pushy to ask a client to instruct us?
No. Asking whether the client would like you to act for them, and explaining what happens next if they do, is a normal and professional part of the consultation. Many clients appreciate a clear question because they are unsure how instructing a solicitor works. What matters is that you ask without pressure and accept their answer.
Should we ask for documents before the consultation?
Where it helps, yes. Seeing key documents in advance lets you spend the consultation on what matters to the client and shows you have taken their matter seriously. Keep the request short and specific, explain how to send documents securely and do not make the consultation depend on them if the client cannot find everything in time.
Who should run the consultation?
Ideally the fee earner who will handle the matter, or someone who will be closely involved. Clients want to meet the person they will be working with. If a different person runs the consultation, introduce the fee earner clearly and explain how the handover will work. Changes of person between consultation and instruction can unsettle clients.
How should we present our fees?
Plainly, early and in writing. Say how you charge, give a figure or range for the next stage and explain what is included and what is not. Where the work is predictable, fixed fees remove a big worry. Where it is not, fixed fees for each stage or clear estimates help. Avoid vague answers, which tend to push clients towards firms that give clearer ones.
How quickly should we send an engagement letter?
As soon as you reasonably can after the client agrees to go ahead, ideally the same day. Templates for your common matter types make this easier. The longer the gap between consultation and terms, the more chance the client has to lose momentum, have second thoughts or speak to another firm.
How can we make engagement letters easier to sign?
Keep your required terms but add a short summary at the top covering who will do the work, the cost, what happens next and what the client must do. Use e-signature where your terms allow. Put payment details in one clear place. Make sure the client knows who to contact with questions. A readable letter is signed faster.
Do we need to mention the referral in our terms?
Where a client was introduced by Leaflegal, the SRA Code of Conduct requires you to make sure they are told about the financial arrangement, in writing, and that it does not affect your independence. Most firms include this in the client care letter. Our consent wording already tells clients we are paid by the firms we introduce, and we send it with every lead.
Should we offer payment plans?
Some firms offer staged payments or monthly instalments for certain matters, especially in family law. It can help clients who want paid help but cannot pay a large sum at once. Whether it works for you depends on your cash flow and your appetite for credit risk. If you offer it, explain the terms clearly and in writing.
What if a client says our fees are too high?
Ask what they are comparing against and what the other quote includes, because quotes are often not like for like. Explain what your fee covers and the value it brings. If you can offer a limited scope service or staged fees, mention it where it fits. If not, accept the decision politely. Some clients will come back once they have compared properly.
What should we measure?
At minimum, enquiries received, enquiries reached, consultations booked and attended, proposals sent and instructions received, all broken down by source and practice area. Also record reasons for not instructing where you know them, and the fee value of instructions. A spreadsheet or a few fields in your CRM are enough to get started.
How long before our numbers mean something?
It depends on your volume. A firm receiving a handful of enquiries a month will need several months of data before patterns are reliable, while a busier firm may see them within a few weeks. Avoid drawing firm conclusions from small numbers. Look for consistent patterns over time rather than reacting to one good or bad week.
Should we compare our conversion with other firms?
General comparisons are not very useful because conversion varies so much by area, fees, location and client type. Leaflegal does not publish conversion figures for that reason. Compare your firm against itself over time, and compare practice areas within your firm, to see where you are improving and where you are losing people.
How do we find out why clients did not instruct?
Ask them, politely, if you get the chance. A short final follow-up asking whether they found help elsewhere or decided not to proceed often gets an honest answer. Record what you hear. Over time, patterns appear, such as price, timing or a competitor offering a faster service, and those patterns tell you what to change.
Who in the firm should own conversion?
Ideally one named person, such as a practice manager, head of department or intake lead. They do not have to do all the work, but they should check the numbers regularly, notice when things slip and make sure changes happen. When conversion belongs to everyone in general, it often ends up belonging to no one in particular, and the same leaks persist.
How often should we review the numbers?
A short monthly review works well for most firms, with a quicker weekly glance at response times and outstanding follow-ups. Look at each practice area separately and compare with previous months. Keep the review practical and focus on one or two changes at a time, so you can tell which ones actually made a difference.
How does Leaflegal help with conversion?
We send exclusive, pre-screened enquiries from people who have confirmed they want paid help, with verified UK mobile and email, their own description of the matter, urgency, location, the best time to call and the consent record. That gives you what you need to call quickly and have a useful first conversation. What happens next depends on your firm's process.
Can you change the leads we receive if some do not convert?
Yes. Tell us which leads instructed, which did not and why where you know, and we can adjust your practice areas, matter types and locations. If a particular type of matter rarely converts for you, we can stop sending it. Feedback is the most effective way to improve the leads you receive over time.
Is a lead invalid if the client does not instruct us?
No. A valid lead has accurate contact details and matches the practice areas, matter types and locations agreed with you. It stays valid if the client later decides not to instruct or chooses another firm. Your partner agreement sets out the rules on invalid leads, such as genuinely wrong contact details, and the review period for raising them.
Can we limit the number of leads while we improve our process?
Yes. The minimum is 10 leads a month, and above that you set your own cap. If you are working on your intake process or your team is stretched, you can lower your cap or pause for a while. Taking only the volume you can handle well usually leads to better conversion than taking more than you can call back quickly.
Does Leaflegal contact clients after sending the lead?
No. Once a lead is delivered, the relationship is between your firm and the client. We do not chase clients on your behalf or contact them about whether they instructed you. That is why your own follow-up matters, and why your feedback to us is so useful. It is the only way we learn which enquiries turn into work.

We will talk through your practice areas, matter types, locations and monthly volume, show you a sample lead and agree a price per lead. Pricing, billing, delivery and invalid lead rules are then set out in a written agreement before your first lead arrives.

What clients ask

What is a conveyancing lead from Leaflegal?
It is an enquiry from a person who is buying, selling or remortgaging a property in England and Wales and has asked to be contacted by a firm about paid conveyancing. They have answered a short set of questions about the transaction, confirmed their contact details and agreed to be contacted by one firm. We send that enquiry to you alone, as soon as it has been checked.
Are the leads exclusive?
Yes. Each enquiry is matched to one firm only. We do not send the same enquiry to several firms at once and we never resell a lead after it has been delivered. If you are the firm we match, you are the only firm that receives that person's details from us. The client can still choose to speak to other firms on their own, as anyone can.
How do you check the leads?
Every client confirms they want paid legal help with a property in England and Wales. We verify their UK mobile number and their email address, and we filter out enquiries that fall outside our scope or your criteria. People we cannot help are told politely and pointed elsewhere, so they never become a lead. Each lead then arrives with the client's answers and the consent record.
What details will I get for each enquiry?
You get the client's name, mobile and email, whether it is a purchase, sale, both or a remortgage, the rough property value, the tenure, whether there is a mortgage, the target timescale and the location. There is also a short description in the client's own words, the best time to call, the source, a timestamp and the exact consent wording they agreed to.
Can I quote from the lead alone?
Usually you can give a provisional figure on the first call, because the lead covers the transaction type, rough value, tenure and whether a mortgage is involved. You will still want to confirm the details with the client and send a written quote. Things like a leasehold management pack, a new build or a gifted deposit may change your figure once you know more.
Do you send leasehold purchases and sales?
Yes, if you want them. Every lead says whether the property is freehold or leasehold, and you can choose to receive leasehold matters, leave them out or ask for them only in certain areas. Firms with an experienced leasehold team sometimes prefer this work, while others choose to focus on freehold transactions. We set your matching to suit you.
Are remortgage leads included?
Only if you ask for them. Remortgages, transfers of equity, purchases, sales and combined sale and purchase matters can each be switched on or off. Some firms take remortgages to keep a steady flow of shorter matters, while others prefer to concentrate on purchases. You can change the mix at any time by letting us know.
Do clients know a firm will call them?
Yes. Every client has agreed to be contacted by a firm about their matter, by phone, email or text, and most are expecting a call soon after they submit. It helps to say early in the call that you are getting in touch because of the enquiry they made through Leaflegal. That puts the client at ease and moves the conversation quickly on to their purchase, sale or remortgage.
