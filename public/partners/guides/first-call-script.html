The first call with a new enquiry is the moment a stranger decides whether to trust your firm. It is usually short, often unplanned from the client's side, and it shapes everything that follows. A good first call leaves the client feeling heard, clear about what happens next and confident about cost. A poor one leaves them confused, unsure whether you can help and inclined to ring someone else.

This guide gives a simple structure that works across conveyancing, family, immigration, motoring, licensing, lease extension and debt recovery enquiries. It is not a word for word script to read aloud. Clients can tell when someone is reading, and legal problems are too personal for that. Think of it as a checklist of the things a good first call covers, in a sensible order, with example wording you can adapt to your own voice.

What a first call is for

The first call has a narrow job. It is not the consultation, and it is not the moment to solve the whole problem. Its purpose is to confirm you are speaking to the right person, show that you understand their matter, check you can help, explain how your firm works and what it costs, and agree a clear next step. If you do those things well, the instruction usually follows from the consultation or quote that comes next.

Who should use it

Intake teams, trainees, paralegals and fee earners can all use the same structure. Firms that train everyone to follow it find that clients get a consistent experience whoever picks up the lead. It also makes it easier to spot where calls are going wrong, because you can compare like with like.

A note on advice

Whether to give any view of the merits on a first call is your professional judgement. Many firms give a short outline of how this kind of matter usually works and keep advice for a booked consultation, where they have the full facts and have run conflict checks. Whatever you decide, be clear with the client about which conversation they are having.

Two minutes of preparation makes the call faster and far more useful. Most of what you need is already in the lead.

Read the whole lead

Each Leaflegal lead includes the client's name, practice area and matter type, a short description in their own words, urgency and any known deadline, their location, the best time to call and the consent record. Read all of it, including the description. The client took time to write it, and repeating it back shows you have listened.

Spot the urgency

Check for a hearing date, a refusal date, a completion date, a review hearing or anything else with a clock attached. If there is one, that shapes the whole call. You will want to confirm the date early and make sure the next step fits around it.

Have your numbers ready

Know your fee structure for this kind of matter before you call. If you offer a fixed fee, know what it includes and what would cost more. If you charge hourly, know the rates for the fee earner who would handle it and roughly what the first stage usually involves. Clients ask about cost on almost every first call.

Check the diary

Have the relevant fee earner's availability in front of you so you can book a consultation during the call. Offering two or three specific times is much more effective than promising to come back with dates.

Run a quick conflict check where you can

If your systems allow it, run the client's name and any other party they mention through a quick check before calling. You will usually need a full check before giving advice anyway, and it avoids an awkward call back later.

Choose a quiet spot

Family and immigration clients in particular may share personal details. Call from somewhere you will not be interrupted and cannot be overheard. If you are calling from a mobile, make sure your number will display or that you have explained in a text who is calling.

Most good first calls follow the same six stages. Some take seconds and some take several minutes, but skipping one usually causes problems later.

1. Introduce yourself and the introduction

Give your name and your firm, and say why you are calling. For example, 'Hello, is that Sam? My name is Alex from Example Solicitors. You made an enquiry through Leaflegal about your divorce and asked to speak to a solicitor. Is now a good time for a few minutes?' Asking whether it is a good time matters. If it is not, book a time and keep the call short.

2. Show you have read their enquiry

Repeat back the key points. 'I can see you have separated recently and you are worried about what happens with the house.' Or 'I understand you have a court date coming up for a speeding matter and you are already on nine points.' This saves the client repeating themselves and shows them straight away that you are paying attention.

3. Ask a few good questions

Fill the gaps the lead does not cover. Keep questions open and short, and let the client talk. Find out what outcome they are hoping for, what worries them most, whether anything has happened since they enquired and whether anyone else is involved. Take notes as you go.

4. Explain how you can help

In plain English, describe how this kind of matter usually runs and what your firm would do. Avoid jargon and long legal explanations. The client mainly wants to know that you deal with this kind of thing regularly and that there is a clear path forward.

5. Be clear about fees

Tell them how you charge and give them a figure or a range for the first stage. Explain what is included and what might cost more. Say you will confirm it in writing. Clients who hear a clear answer about cost are much more likely to take the next step than those who are told it depends.

6. Agree the next step

End with a specific action and a date. A booked consultation, a list of documents to send, a written quote to review or a time for a follow-up call. Confirm it by email straight after the call, with your direct contact details.

Two topics deserve extra care on the first call because they shape trust from the start. The first is cost. The second is the fact that the client came to you through an introducer.

Talking about cost

Many fee earners feel awkward talking about money, so they delay it or give a vague answer. Clients notice. Someone paying privately for a legal matter needs to know roughly what it will cost before they can commit. Giving a clear answer early shows confidence and honesty.

  • State your fee structure plainly, fixed fee or hourly
  • Give a figure or range for the next stage, not the whole matter if that is unknowable
  • Say what is included and what is not, such as disbursements or court fees
  • Explain when payment is due and whether you take money on account
  • Confirm everything in writing after the call

Handling price objections

If the client says another firm is cheaper, ask what is included in the other quote. Often it is not a like for like comparison. Explain what your fee covers and why. If your price is genuinely higher, be honest about what the client gets for it, such as a named fee earner, faster turnaround or more experience with their type of matter. Do not run down other firms.

Mentioning the referral

The client made their enquiry through Leaflegal and agreed to be contacted by a firm we match them with. Saying so in your opening line helps them recognise the call. Leaflegal's consent wording tells every client that we are paid by the firms we introduce them to, and we send you that wording with each lead.

Under the SRA Code of Conduct you need to make sure clients are told about any financial arrangement with an introducer, in writing, and that the arrangement does not affect your independence or advice. Most firms mention it briefly on the call and confirm it in the client care letter. Our page on the SRA referral rules explains how we fit with them.

Every intake team meets the same handful of difficult situations. Having a plan for each makes them much easier to handle.

The client is upset

Family, immigration and motoring clients are sometimes distressed on the first call. Slow down, let them talk and acknowledge how they feel before moving on to practical points. You do not need to rush the structure. A client who feels listened to will usually be ready to discuss next steps once they have said what they need to say.

They want advice now

Some clients want a full answer on the first call. Be honest about what you can say without the full facts. Give a general picture of how this kind of matter usually works and explain that a consultation is where you can look properly at their situation. If the matter is urgent, offer the earliest consultation you can.

They are comparing firms

That is normal and nothing to worry about. Focus on being the most helpful and the clearest about cost. Ask what matters most to them in choosing a firm. Make it easy to say yes by offering a specific next step, and follow up as agreed.

They cannot afford your fees

Some people discover on the first call that private fees are more than they expected. Explain any options your firm offers, such as staged fees or a limited scope service, if they fit. If you cannot help, be kind and point them towards general sources of information. Every Leaflegal client has confirmed they want paid help, but budgets vary.

You cannot take the matter

Sometimes a conflict, a capacity issue or the matter type means you cannot act. Tell the client promptly and politely. If it is a matter outside your agreed criteria, or you find a conflict, let us know so we can deal with the lead as your partner agreement sets out.

Someone else answers

If a partner, parent or colleague answers, do not discuss the matter. Ask for the client by name and offer to call back. In family and immigration matters especially, the client may not want others to know they have contacted a solicitor.

The structure stays the same, but each area has its own focus on the first call.

Conveyancing

Clients want a clear quote and reassurance you will move quickly. Confirm whether it is a purchase, sale, both or a remortgage, the rough value and whether it is leasehold, then talk through an itemised quote including likely disbursements. Ask about their timescale and the other parties in the chain.

Family law

Lead with empathy. Confirm what the client wants help with, whether proceedings have started and whether there is any immediate concern about safety or children. Offer a clear first step, such as a fixed fee initial consultation, and explain how you charge after that.

Immigration

Confirm the type of matter and any deadline or refusal date early. Leaflegal does not give immigration advice and collects only the matter type, urgency and contact details, so you will need to gather the rest. Explain how your consultations work and what to bring. Make sure the adviser handling the matter is authorised at the right level.

Motoring offences

Check the court date and the current points position straight away. Clients are usually worried about losing their licence and their job. Explain how you usually help with this kind of case and what your fee covers, including attendance at court.

Licensing

Confirm the type of licence, whether it is a new application, variation, transfer or review, and any hearing or opening date. Business owners want to know you understand their venue and the local authority process.

Lease extensions

Check roughly how long is left on the lease and whether a sale or remortgage depends on it. Explain the usual stages and the role of a specialist valuer, and be clear about which costs are yours and which may be payable to the landlord.

Debt recovery

Confirm the amount, the age of the debt and whether it is disputed. Business owners want action and certainty on cost, so explain the usual first step and how fees are charged at each stage.

Questions and answers

Should we give advice on the first call?
That is your professional judgement. Many firms give an outline and book a proper consultation.
Do we need to mention the referral?
Yes. Disclose the arrangement as the SRA Code requires.
Do we need a script for first calls?
A loose structure works better than a word for word script. Clients can tell when someone is reading, and legal problems are personal. A clear checklist of what every first call should cover, with example wording people can adapt, gives consistency without sounding robotic. It also helps new staff learn quickly and lets you compare calls when reviewing how leads are converting.
How long should a first call last?
Long enough to do the job and no longer. Many good first calls take ten to fifteen minutes, but a simple conveyancing quote might take five and a distressed family client might need longer. If the call is drifting into a full consultation, that is usually a sign to book one. The client should finish the call knowing what happens next.
What should we read before calling?
The whole lead. That includes the matter type, the client's own description, urgency and any deadline, the location and the best time to call. It only takes a minute and it lets you open with something specific. Also check your fee information for that kind of matter and the diary of the fee earner who would handle it.
Should we run a conflict check before the first call?
Where your systems allow a quick check, it is worth doing before calling, especially if the client names another party. You will need a proper check before advising in any case. Finding a conflict after a long and friendly first call is awkward for everyone. If you do find a conflict, let us know so the lead can be dealt with under your agreement.
Who in the firm should make the first call?
Someone who is available quickly, knows your fees and processes and can book a clear next step. That might be an intake specialist, a paralegal or a fee earner. Many firms use intake staff for speed and fee earners for consultations. The most important thing is that every caller follows the same structure so clients get a consistent experience.
Should we text before we call?
Some firms send a short text first saying who they are and that they will call in a few minutes, which can help people answer an unknown number. Others call first and text only if there is no answer. Both can work. Try each with your own leads for a few weeks and keep whichever gets more people talking to you.
How should we open the call?
Give your name and firm, say you are calling about the enquiry they made through Leaflegal and ask if now is a good time. That is usually enough for the client to place the call. If it is not a good time, book a specific time to call back and confirm it by text. Keeping the opening short and polite sets the right tone.
What questions should we ask?
Ask what has happened, what outcome they are hoping for, what worries them most and whether anything has changed since they enquired. Fill in the gaps the lead does not cover, such as other parties or key dates. Keep questions open and let the client speak. Most clients will tell you what matters to them if you give them room.
Should we give legal advice on the first call?
That is your professional judgement. Many firms explain in general terms how this kind of matter usually works and keep specific advice for a booked consultation once they have the full facts and have checked for conflicts. Be clear with the client about which kind of conversation they are having, so they do not mistake an outline for advice.
What if the client is distressed?
Slow down and listen. Acknowledge how they feel before moving to practical points, and do not rush through your checklist. If there are any safety concerns, especially in family matters, follow your firm's procedures. Once the client feels heard, they are usually ready to talk about next steps. A gentle, unhurried call is often remembered long after the details are forgotten.
What if someone else answers the phone?
Ask for the client by name and do not say what the call is about. Offer to call back at a better time. Many clients, particularly in family and immigration matters, do not want others to know they have contacted a solicitor. Discretion on the first call builds trust and protects the client.
How do we handle a client who is comparing firms?
Accept it as normal. Ask what matters most to them in choosing a firm and answer that directly. Be clear about cost and what is included, and offer a specific next step with a date. Follow up as agreed. Being the most helpful and the clearest firm is usually more persuasive than trying to talk down the competition.
What should we avoid saying?
Avoid promising outcomes, running down other firms, using jargon or giving vague answers about cost. Do not pressure the client to decide on the spot. Avoid discussing the matter with anyone other than the client. And avoid ending the call without a clear next step, because a client left with nothing to do often drifts away.
How do we end the call well?
Summarise what you have agreed, confirm the next step and the date, and tell the client exactly what you will send them and when. Give your direct number or email so they can reach you without going through reception. Thank them for their time. A clear, warm ending leaves the client knowing what happens next and who to contact.
What if the client has already instructed another firm?
Thank them, wish them well and leave the door open in case things change. Do not criticise the other firm. If they have only instructed someone for part of the matter, there may still be work you can help with, but be careful not to interfere with an existing retainer. Note the outcome so you can see how often it happens.
When should we mention fees?
Earlier than many firms do. Once you understand the matter and have explained how you can help, talk about cost. Waiting until the client asks, or until the end of a long call, can make it feel like something you would rather avoid. Clear fees early on build trust and help the client decide whether to take the next step.
What if we cannot give a fixed fee?
Explain why and give the best estimate you can for the next stage, such as an initial consultation, a review of documents or a first letter. Say what would make the cost go up or down. Confirm the estimate in writing. Clients accept that some matters are uncertain, but they rarely accept no figure at all.
How do we respond if the client says we are too expensive?
Ask what the other quote includes, because comparisons are often not like for like. Explain what your fee covers, such as a named fee earner, faster responses or experience with their type of matter. If you offer staged fees or a limited scope service, mention it where it fits. Stay calm and honest and do not run down other firms.
Do we have to tell the client about the referral?
Yes. Under the SRA Code of Conduct you must make sure clients are told about any financial arrangement you have with an introducer, and it should be confirmed in writing. Leaflegal's consent wording already tells clients we are paid by the firms we introduce them to, and we send that wording with each lead so your client care letter can match it.
How should we explain the referral fee?
Simply and briefly. Many firms say that the client was introduced through Leaflegal, that the firm pays Leaflegal for the introduction and that this does not change the advice they will receive. Confirm the details in the client care letter. Being open about it is usually reassuring to clients rather than off-putting.
Does the referral fee change what the client pays?
Your fees to the client are your own decision. The fee you pay Leaflegal for an introduction is agreed between your firm and us. How you price your services is a matter for you, but the client must be told about the referral arrangement and it must not affect your independence or the advice you give.
Should we take a payment on the first call?
Some firms take payment for a fixed fee consultation when booking it, which can reduce no-shows and confirm the client is serious. Others prefer to send an invoice or payment link with the confirmation email. Either can work. Make sure the client has your terms and the price in writing before paying, and that your process fits your client care obligations.
What should we send after the first call?
A short email the same day confirming who you are, what you discussed, the agreed next step and date, your fee for that step and your direct contact details. If you booked a consultation, include the time, place or link and what to bring. Keep it brief and in plain English. It reassures the client and gives them something to refer back to.
What if the client says they need to think about it?
That is fine. Agree when you will check in, send a helpful follow-up email with your fees and next steps, and call as arranged. Many people need to talk to a partner, check their finances or gather documents before they commit. A planned and polite follow-up often turns a not yet into an instruction.
How do we record first calls?
Log the outcome in your CRM or a simple spreadsheet, including the date, who called, what was agreed and the next action. Note any reason the client gives for not going ahead. Over time this shows you which parts of the call work and where clients drop off, which is the best way to improve your conversion.
Should we review first calls as a team?
Many firms find it useful. A short regular review of a few calls, looking at what went well and what could improve, helps everyone get better. Focus on structure, clarity about fees and whether a next step was agreed. If you record calls, make sure you do so lawfully and in line with your privacy notice.
How do we feed back to Leaflegal?
Tell us which leads you reached, which booked a consultation, which instructed and, where you know, why others did not. That feedback helps us adjust your practice areas, matter types and locations so you receive more of the work you want. There is no platform to update. A regular email or call with our team is enough.
What if the client does not turn up to the consultation?
Call them soon after the missed appointment, without blame. Life gets in the way, especially for people under stress. Offer another time and confirm it in writing with a reminder the day before. If they miss a second appointment, send a polite message letting them know how to get back in touch. Recording no-shows helps you see whether reminders or deposits would help.
What if the lead was outside our criteria?
If a lead does not match the practice areas, matter types or locations agreed with you, or it has genuinely invalid contact details, let us know within the review period in your partner agreement. We will deal with it as the agreement sets out. A client who decides not to instruct you is still a valid lead, which is why the first call matters so much.

What clients ask

What is a conveyancing lead from Leaflegal?
It is an enquiry from a person who is buying, selling or remortgaging a property in England and Wales and has asked to be contacted by a firm about paid conveyancing. They have answered a short set of questions about the transaction, confirmed their contact details and agreed to be contacted by one firm. We send that enquiry to you alone, as soon as it has been checked.
Are the leads exclusive?
Yes. Each enquiry is matched to one firm only. We do not send the same enquiry to several firms at once and we never resell a lead after it has been delivered. If you are the firm we match, you are the only firm that receives that person's details from us. The client can still choose to speak to other firms on their own, as anyone can.
How do you check the leads?
Every client confirms they want paid legal help with a property in England and Wales. We verify their UK mobile number and their email address, and we filter out enquiries that fall outside our scope or your criteria. People we cannot help are told politely and pointed elsewhere, so they never become a lead. Each lead then arrives with the client's answers and the consent record.
What details will I get for each enquiry?
You get the client's name, mobile and email, whether it is a purchase, sale, both or a remortgage, the rough property value, the tenure, whether there is a mortgage, the target timescale and the location. There is also a short description in the client's own words, the best time to call, the source, a timestamp and the exact consent wording they agreed to.
Can I quote from the lead alone?
Usually you can give a provisional figure on the first call, because the lead covers the transaction type, rough value, tenure and whether a mortgage is involved. You will still want to confirm the details with the client and send a written quote. Things like a leasehold management pack, a new build or a gifted deposit may change your figure once you know more.
Do you send leasehold purchases and sales?
Yes, if you want them. Every lead says whether the property is freehold or leasehold, and you can choose to receive leasehold matters, leave them out or ask for them only in certain areas. Firms with an experienced leasehold team sometimes prefer this work, while others choose to focus on freehold transactions. We set your matching to suit you.
Are remortgage leads included?
Only if you ask for them. Remortgages, transfers of equity, purchases, sales and combined sale and purchase matters can each be switched on or off. Some firms take remortgages to keep a steady flow of shorter matters, while others prefer to concentrate on purchases. You can change the mix at any time by letting us know.
Do clients know a firm will call them?
Yes. Every client has agreed to be contacted by a firm about their matter, by phone, email or text, and most are expecting a call soon after they submit. It helps to say early in the call that you are getting in touch because of the enquiry they made through Leaflegal. That puts the client at ease and moves the conversation quickly on to their purchase, sale or remortgage.
