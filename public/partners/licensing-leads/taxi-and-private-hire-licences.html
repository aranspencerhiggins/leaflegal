Leaflegal sends licensing enquiries from businesses and individuals in England and Wales who want to pay a solicitor to help with a licence. They come from pubs, bars, restaurants, late night venues, shops selling alcohol, event organisers, taxi and private hire drivers and operators. Each enquiry goes to one firm only. It is never sent to several firms at once and it is never resold after delivery.

Licensing clients usually have something real at stake. A new venue cannot open without the right licence, a review could put an established business at risk, and a driver whose licence is suspended may lose their income overnight. Many have a hearing date, an opening date or a deadline from the council, and they want representation they can rely on.

Enquiries come from operators, licence holders and applicants, not from residents or other members of the public who want to object to a licence. That means every lead is a potential client who needs help with their own licence or business.

How the enquiries are generated

Our campaigns run on search, social media and helpful content aimed at licensees, venue owners and drivers. Each person answers a short set of questions about the type of licence, what they need, any hearing or opening date, the council area and the name of the business. When an enquiry matches your criteria, we check it and send it to you straight away.

Many licensees do not know where to start when something goes wrong. Some assume they can deal with a review alone and only look for help once the paperwork arrives. Others are not sure whether they need a licensing specialist or a general solicitor. Our questions steer them to a firm that does licensing work, so they reach you with a clear idea of what they need help with, even if they do not yet know the process.

Why licensing suits exclusive leads

Licensing work is often relationship work. A venue owner who is well represented at a review hearing may come back for variations, transfers and new sites for years. Multi site operators and drivers' networks also talk to each other. An exclusive introduction gives you a fair chance to build that relationship from the first call.

What Leaflegal is not

Leaflegal is not a law firm and does not give legal advice. We do not tell a client whether their application will be granted, how a committee is likely to decide or what conditions they should offer. We introduce them to one firm that handles licensing, and the advice comes from you.

Where they are in the process

Some clients are planning a new venue months ahead and want help with the application and the plans. Others have received notice of a review, a letter from the police or a complaint from environmental health and want representation quickly. What clients care about

Licensing clients want someone who knows how their council and its committee work, who can deal with the police and other responsible authorities, and who can give a clear idea of cost. A business facing a review also wants reassurance that its side of the story will be put properly. A calm, practical first conversation goes a long way.

Multi site operators sometimes enquire about one venue while planning changes across several. The description in the client's own words often mentions this, and it can be worth asking about on the first call.

Why the council area matters

Licensing is run by local authorities, and each one has its own statement of licensing policy, its own committee practices and, in some places, special policies for particular areas. Knowing the council before you call lets you speak to the client about what you know of that authority, in general terms, and decide whether the matter fits your coverage.

Taxi and private hire licensing is also run by councils, and each sets its own policies on matters such as driver standards and vehicle requirements. For drivers and operators, the council area tells you which policy and which appeal route are likely to be relevant.

Why the business name helps

Having the business name lets you run conflict checks before the first call and look at publicly available information, such as the existing licence on the council's register. That means you can open the conversation already understanding something about the premises.

What clients ask

What is the difference between a taxi and a private hire vehicle?
A hackney carriage, often called a taxi or black cab, can be hailed in the street and use ranks in the area where it is licensed. A private hire vehicle must be booked in advance through a licensed operator and cannot pick up people who flag it down. They are licensed under different laws and often have different requirements.
Who licenses taxis and private hire vehicles?
Outside London, the district, borough, city or unitary council licenses drivers, vehicles and operators for its area. In London, Transport for London is responsible. Each licensing authority sets its own policy, fees and requirements within the law, so they differ from one area to another.
What checks do I need for a driver licence?
Most councils require an enhanced criminal record check with barred lists, a medical, a DVLA driving record check, proof of the right to work and a knowledge test. Many also require safeguarding and disability awareness training, and some require an English or communication assessment. Your council's policy sets out the full list.
Can I get a licence with a criminal record?
It depends on the offence, how long ago it was and your record since. Most councils have a policy setting out how long a person should normally be free of certain types of offence. Serious violent or sexual offences usually lead to refusal. Each case is still considered individually. A solicitor can look at your history and the policy.
Can I drive for a private hire operator licensed in a different area?
For private hire, the driver, vehicle and operator generally all need to be licensed by the same council. Hackney carriages can be pre-booked to go to other areas. The rules on cross-border working are complex, and a solicitor or the council can explain how they apply to your work.
What does fit and proper mean?
It is the test councils use to decide whether someone can hold a driver or operator licence. There is no fixed definition, but the focus is on passenger safety and whether the person can be trusted. Councils look at convictions, complaints, driving record and conduct. They do not need a criminal conviction to decide someone is not fit and proper.
Do I need to renew my licence?
Yes. Driver, vehicle and operator licences are granted for set periods and must be renewed. Renewal is not automatic, and the council will look again at your record. Applying in good time avoids a gap during which you cannot work. Check your council's renewal deadlines.
Can I transfer my badge to a new council?
No. A badge issued by one council cannot simply be transferred to another. If you want to work in a different area, you usually need to apply to that council and meet its requirements, which may include its own knowledge test and checks. Your history with the first council will usually need to be declared.
Why might the council suspend or revoke my licence?
Reasons include a conviction for an offence involving dishonesty, indecency or violence, breaches of taxi and private hire law, complaints about conduct, driving offences and points, or any other reasonable cause. The council must act fairly and give reasons. What counts as reasonable cause depends on the facts.
Can my licence be suspended immediately?
Yes, if the council decides that the interests of public safety require it. The suspension or revocation then takes effect straight away and you must stop working. You can still appeal. Without an immediate effect decision, the licence usually continues until the appeal period ends or any appeal is decided.
Can the council act before a police investigation finishes?
Yes. The council applies its own test focused on passenger safety, and it does not have to wait for a criminal case to finish. It can act on information from the police or complaints even if no charge is brought. A solicitor can help you respond while being careful about any related criminal case.
Will points on my driving licence affect my badge?
They can. Many councils treat a certain number of points, or particular offences such as using a mobile phone while driving, as a reason to review a licence. A driving ban will usually mean you cannot drive for hire. If you are facing a motoring prosecution, think about the effect on your badge too.
What happens after a passenger complaint?
The council usually investigates, which may involve asking for your account, checking operator records and speaking to the complainant. Minor complaints may lead to advice or a warning. Serious complaints can lead to a hearing, suspension or revocation. It is important to respond to the council promptly and carefully.
Should I attend an interview with a licensing officer?
It is usually wise to engage with the council's process. An interview is often your first chance to give your side, and what you say may be relied on later. Some drivers ask a solicitor to help them prepare or to attend with them. Check the invitation for details of what will be discussed.
Can my vehicle licence be suspended at the roadside?
Yes. Authorised council officers and police officers can suspend a vehicle licence immediately if they find the vehicle unfit, for example because of a defect. It stays suspended until the problem is fixed and the vehicle is re-inspected. You cannot use it for hire while suspended.
Do I have to tell the council if I am arrested?
Many councils include a condition on driver licences requiring drivers to report arrests, charges, cautions and convictions within a short period. The exact requirement depends on your council's conditions. Failing to report when required can itself lead to action against your licence. A solicitor can check what your conditions say.
What happens at a licensing hearing?
A sub-committee of councillors, or sometimes an officer, considers the case. A licensing officer usually presents a report, and you or your representative can respond, give evidence and answer questions. The committee decides and must give reasons. Hearings are less formal than court but are taken seriously.
Can I bring someone to the hearing?
Yes. You can usually be represented by a solicitor or bring a friend, family member or interpreter, depending on the council's procedure. Character witnesses can sometimes give evidence or provide written references. Check the council's hearing procedure, which is often sent with the notice of hearing.
How do I appeal a revocation?
Most driver licence decisions can be appealed to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being notified. The court hears the case afresh and can consider new evidence. Some decisions can be appealed further to the Crown Court. The time limit is strict, so speak to a solicitor as soon as you receive the decision.
Can I keep working while I appeal?
If the council's decision was not given immediate effect, you can usually keep working until the appeal period ends or the appeal is decided. If it was given immediate effect on public safety grounds, you cannot work during the appeal. A solicitor can explain your position and whether the appeal can be heard quickly.
What are the risks of appealing?
If the appeal fails, the court can order you to pay the council's legal costs, which can be significant. The court also gives weight to the council's decision and policy. A solicitor will look at the strength of your case and the likely costs before you decide whether to appeal.
Can I apply to a different council instead?
You can apply elsewhere, but you must usually declare previous refusals and revocations, and councils share this information through a national register and safeguarding duties. Failing to disclose them can lead to refusal or revocation. A solicitor can explain how a previous decision may be viewed.
What does a private hire operator licence involve?
An operator licence allows a person or business to accept and arrange private hire bookings. The operator must be fit and proper, keep records of bookings, drivers and vehicles, and use only drivers and vehicles licensed by the same council. Conditions often cover insurance, premises, complaints handling and safeguarding.
Can operators face action because of their drivers?
Yes. If an operator uses unlicensed drivers or vehicles, fails to keep proper records or does not deal with safeguarding concerns, the council can take action against the operator licence. Problems with individual drivers can lead to questions about the operator's systems. A solicitor can help operators review their processes.
Can an operator appeal a council decision?
Yes. Refusals, suspensions and revocations of operator licences can usually be appealed to the magistrates' court within a strict time limit. The process is similar to a driver appeal. A solicitor can confirm the correct route and help with preparation.
Do operators need to check their own staff?
Councils increasingly expect operators to check that staff in roles such as booking and dispatch are suitable, often through basic criminal record checks, and to have a policy on employing people with convictions. Requirements vary between councils and are often set out in operator licence conditions or the council's policy.
How much are taxi licence fees?
Taxi and private hire fees are set locally by each council and vary between areas. On top of the licence fee, applicants usually pay for medicals, criminal record checks, knowledge tests and training. Fees change from time to time, so check your council's website for current amounts.
How much does a solicitor charge for a taxi licence matter?
Many firms offer a fixed fee for help with a council interview or hearing, and a separate fee for an appeal. More complex cases, such as those linked to a criminal investigation, may cost more. Ask the firm for a written quote before you agree to anything.

No. Leaflegal is free to use. The licensing firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that does not change what you pay them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.

Have your badge number, the name of your council, any letter or email from the council, details of any complaint or police matter and any hearing or appeal deadline. Mention urgent dates on the form so the firm knows to contact you quickly. Give a phone number you can answer during the day.
