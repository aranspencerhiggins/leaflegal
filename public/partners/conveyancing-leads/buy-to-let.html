Leaflegal sends conveyancing enquiries from people who are buying, selling or remortgaging a home in England and Wales and want a firm to handle the legal work. Each enquiry goes to one firm only. It is never shared with a panel of other firms and it is never resold once it has been delivered to you.

Conveyancing clients usually contact a firm at a clear point of decision. An offer has been accepted, a buyer has been found, a fixed rate is coming to an end or a lender has asked for a solicitor to be named. At that moment they want a quote, a sense of how long things will take and someone who will pick up the phone. Our enquiries are built around that moment.

We run campaigns aimed at people at these points in a move, ask them a short set of questions about the transaction and confirm that they want to instruct a firm for paid conveyancing. When an enquiry matches the matter types and locations you have chosen, we check it and send it to you straight away, by email or into your CRM.

Why firms add bought leads to their pipeline

Most conveyancing firms rely on estate agent referrals, repeat clients and their own website. Those channels matter, but agent referrals can come with conditions, and paid search for conveyancing terms is crowded and costly to manage. Exclusive leads give you a source of direct enquiries where you decide the volume and the type of work.

Direct clients also tend to have chosen you rather than been handed to you. That can make the relationship simpler, because the client is not looking back to an agent for updates and you can set expectations from the first call.

What we do not do

We are not a comparison site and we do not run a quote engine that sends a buyer five competing prices. We do not tell a client which firm is cheapest or best. We introduce them to one firm that has chosen to receive their kind of matter, and the rest of the relationship is between you and the client.

Leaflegal is not a law firm, is not regulated by the SRA or the CLC and does not give legal advice. You are responsible for being authorised to carry out reserved conveyancing work and for meeting the requirements of any lender you act for.

Conveyancing enquiries come from a wide mix of people, which is why we let you choose the matter types you want. A firm that only handles freehold purchases can receive just those, while a firm with a strong leasehold team can ask for more of that work.

Typical matter types

  • Purchases, including first time buyers
  • Sales of freehold and leasehold homes
  • A sale and purchase together, often in a chain
  • Remortgages, with or without a change of lender
  • Transfers of equity, for example when a partner is added or removed
  • Leasehold flat purchases and sales
  • New build purchases with a developer deadline
  • Buy to let purchases by individual landlords

Where they are in the process

Some people enquire the day an offer is accepted. Others are still viewing and want to line up a firm early so they can move quickly when they find the right property. Sellers often enquire when they put the house on the market, because agents increasingly ask them to instruct a solicitor before a buyer is found. Remortgage clients usually enquire a few months before their current deal ends.

Each lead tells you the stage the client has reached and their rough timescale, so you can decide who to call first and how to pitch your service.

What clients care about

In our experience of the questions people ask, conveyancing clients care about three things above all. They want a clear quote with no surprises later, they want regular updates without having to chase, and they want to know the firm will not hold up their chain. A first call that speaks to those three points usually lands well.

Many clients are anxious about the process, especially first time buyers, so a calm explanation of the main stages helps. They are rarely choosing on price alone, but they do compare quotes, so speed and clarity matter.

Every lead is the full record of what the client told us, in the order they told us. There is no scraped data and nothing is guessed. The aim is to give your quoting team enough to give a figure on the first call.

Enquiries that fall outside the areas we cover, or outside your criteria, are filtered out and the person is pointed somewhere more suitable. They never reach your inbox.

Each client ticks two separate boxes before submitting. One confirms they have read our terms and privacy policy and understand that Leaflegal is an introduction service, not a law firm. The other gives consent for their details to be shared with one firm and for that firm to contact them by phone, email or text about their matter. Delivery

Leads are sent as soon as they have been checked. You can receive them by email, to a shared intake inbox, or straight into your CRM or case management system using a lead email address or a webhook. There is no platform to log into.

How you handle the first contact makes a large difference to how many enquiries become instructions. The firms that do best treat each lead as a live quote request rather than a name to add to a list.

Call quickly

A conveyancing client who has just had an offer accepted often contacts more than one firm or is being pushed towards the agent's panel firm. Calling while they are still thinking about their move gives you the best chance of being the firm they choose. Set up alerts so the right person sees a lead the moment it arrives.

Quote on the first call if you can

The lead tells you the transaction type, the rough value, the tenure and whether there is a mortgage. That is usually enough for a provisional figure. Follow up the call with a written, itemised quote that sets out your fee, the likely disbursements and anything that could add cost, such as a leasehold supplement or a management pack.

Set expectations

Explain the main stages in plain terms and how you will keep them updated. Many clients have heard stories about slow conveyancing, so telling them how often they will hear from you, and how to reach their case handler, builds trust before they instruct.

Mention the introduction

The client agreed to be contacted by a firm after making an enquiry through Leaflegal. Say so when you introduce yourself. Your client care letter should then cover any referral arrangement you are required to disclose.

Follow up

Not every client instructs on the first call. Some are waiting for a mortgage offer or for the agent to confirm a sale. A short email the same day, a second call a few days later and a final check in after a week often turn a not yet into an instruction. Keep it helpful rather than pushy.

Tell us what works

Let us know which leads instruct and which do not, and why. If remortgages rarely convert for you but leasehold sales do, we can change your matching so you receive more of the work you want.

Firms that review their results every month or so, and share what they see with us, usually find it easier to shape the work they receive over time.

Conveyancing is a reserved legal activity, and buying leads does not change your professional duties. Leaflegal is set up to support those duties rather than work around them.

What clients ask

Is buying a rental property different from buying a home?
The core legal work is the same. Your conveyancer checks the title, orders searches, raises enquiries and handles the money. The difference is in the extra checks around letting, such as lease restrictions, licensing, existing tenancies and the conditions in a buy to let mortgage. The firm will also need to know from the start whether you are buying personally or through a company.
Do I need a buy to let mortgage?
If you plan to let the property and are borrowing, you will usually need a mortgage designed for letting. A standard residential mortgage normally forbids letting without the lender's consent. Letting in breach of your mortgage terms can have serious consequences. Your mortgage broker can explain the options, and your conveyancer will check the offer conditions.
Can I let my old home instead of selling it?
Many people do, sometimes called being an accidental landlord. You will usually need your lender's consent to let, or a new buy to let mortgage. Buying your next home while keeping the old one can mean paying higher rates of Stamp Duty Land Tax. Your conveyancer can explain how the tax applies, and a tax adviser can help with the wider picture.
Can I buy a property at auction to let?
Yes, but auction purchases move quickly. When the hammer falls, contracts are usually exchanged and completion often follows within a short fixed period. That means the legal checks need to happen before the auction. A conveyancer can review the legal pack for you in advance, and you will need your finance ready in time.
Can I buy a house in multiple occupation?
Yes, but these properties carry extra rules. A large house in multiple occupation needs a mandatory licence, and smaller ones may need one under local schemes. Planning permission can also be needed. Many lenders have specific products for these properties. Your conveyancer will raise enquiries about licensing and planning, and it is wise to check with the council too.
Does the conveyancer check whether the property is a good investment?
No. Your conveyancer checks the legal title and raises questions about anything that might affect your ownership or your plans. Whether the rent, price and location make sense as an investment is a commercial decision for you. Many landlords speak to letting agents, mortgage brokers and accountants alongside their conveyancer.
How long does a buy to let purchase take?
There is no fixed timescale. A simple purchase of a vacant freehold house with no chain can move quickly, while a leasehold flat with a tenant in place, a company buyer and a lender with strict conditions will usually take longer. Searches, the speed of the seller's side and the managing agent's response all play a part. Your conveyancer can give a better idea once they have seen the paperwork.
Can I buy through a limited company?
Yes. Many landlords now buy through a company set up to hold property. The company is the legal owner. Your conveyancer will need details of the company, its directors and its owners, and evidence that it can buy and borrow. Whether this route suits you is mainly a tax question for an accountant.
Will the lender want a personal guarantee?
Most lenders who lend to property companies want the directors to give personal guarantees. This means they can be personally liable for the company's mortgage debt. Lenders often require guarantors to have independent legal advice before signing, and this may have to come from a separate solicitor. Your conveyancer will explain the lender's requirements.
Does a company pay Stamp Duty?
Yes. A company buying residential property usually pays the higher rates of Stamp Duty Land Tax that apply to additional properties, and different rules can apply to higher value homes. Rates and reliefs change, so check GOV.UK or ask your conveyancer what applies to your purchase.
What documents will the firm need for a company purchase?
Typically the company's registration details, its articles of association, details of directors and people with significant control, identity documents for those people and evidence of where the purchase money is coming from. Some firms will also ask for board minutes approving the purchase and the mortgage. Having these ready at the start can save weeks.
Can I move my existing rental properties into a company?
It is possible, but the company is treated as buying the property, so it can trigger Stamp Duty Land Tax and capital gains tax, and you will usually need new mortgages. This is an area where specialist tax advice is essential before you start. Your conveyancer can then handle the legal transfers.
Can I buy through a company with a business partner?
Yes. Many property companies have two or more directors and shareholders. It is sensible for the owners to agree in writing how decisions are made, how profits are shared and what happens if one wants to leave, often in a shareholders' agreement. That is separate from the conveyancing, and a company or commercial solicitor usually prepares it. Your conveyancer will need identity checks on everyone involved.
Can I buy a property with a tenant already living there?
Yes. This is often called buying with a sitting tenant or tenanted. You take over the tenancy as it stands. Your conveyancer will ask for the tenancy agreement, deposit protection details, safety records and any notices that have been served. At completion the deposit and paperwork should be handed over so you can step into the seller's shoes.
What happens to the tenant's deposit when I buy?
The deposit usually needs to be transferred to you or your agent on completion and then protected in your name with an approved scheme. The tenant should be told who now holds it. Your conveyancer can deal with the handover on completion, but keeping the deposit protected after that is your duty as landlord.
Can the lease stop me letting a flat?
Yes. Some leases forbid letting, some need the freeholder's consent and some ban short term or holiday lets. A lease might also limit who can live there. Your conveyancer will read the lease and tell you what it says. If the lease does not allow what you plan, it is far better to know before exchange.
How are tenancy rules changing?
In England, the Renters' Rights Act 2025 is reforming private renting, including ending section 21 no fault possession and moving most tenancies onto a rolling basis. Changes are being introduced over time. Wales has its own system under the Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016. Your conveyancer can explain how the rules affect a tenancy you are taking over, and GOV.UK has current guidance for landlords.
Can I use the property as a holiday let?
It depends on the title, any lease, planning rules in the area and your mortgage. Some leases ban short lets and some councils need planning permission for them. Holiday lets usually need a different kind of mortgage and insurance. Your conveyancer can check the title and lease, but you may also need to speak to the council and your lender.
Do I need a licence to let the property?
Some properties do. Larger houses in multiple occupation always need a licence, and many councils run schemes that require licences for other rented homes in certain areas. Your conveyancer can ask the seller about licensing, and you can check the local council's website for current schemes.
What if the tenant is behind with the rent?
Rent arrears on a tenanted purchase are something to raise before exchange. The contract can say how arrears are dealt with, for example whether the seller keeps the right to recover them or the price is adjusted. Taking on a tenant who is already behind is a commercial risk you will want to weigh up. Your conveyancer will ask for a rent statement and explain what the contract says.
What about flats with cladding or fire safety problems?
Some blocks have fire safety defects that affect lending, insurance and service charges. The Building Safety Act 2022 limits what some leaseholders can be charged for certain repairs, but the protection depends partly on how many properties the owner holds, so landlords with several properties may be treated differently. Your conveyancer will ask the managing agent about the building and report on what they find, and your lender may have its own requirements.
Will I pay a higher rate of Stamp Duty?
Usually yes, if you already own a home or are buying through a company. Higher rates apply to most additional residential properties in England. The rates and the rules about when they apply change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask your conveyancer. In Wales, Land Transaction Tax has its own higher rates.
Can I reclaim the higher rate later?
In some cases, if you buy a new main home before selling your old one and then sell the old one within a set period, you may be able to claim back the extra tax. This does not usually apply to buy to let purchases where the new property will not be your home. Your conveyancer or a tax adviser can explain whether a refund could apply.
Why does the firm need so much detail about my funds?
Conveyancers must carry out anti money laundering checks on every purchase. Landlords often use money from several sources, such as savings, remortgages, sale proceeds or company funds. Each source needs to be explained and evidenced. Providing a clear trail early is the best way to avoid delays.
Who should I speak to about tax?
Your conveyancer will deal with the Stamp Duty Land Tax return for the purchase. Wider questions, such as income tax on rent, mortgage interest relief, capital gains tax and whether to use a company, are for an accountant or tax adviser. Many landlords speak to one before they make an offer.
Do I need insurance in place before completion?
If the property is freehold, you usually become responsible for insuring it from exchange of contracts, and your lender will expect cover to be in place. Landlords need a policy that covers letting, as ordinary home insurance often does not. For a leasehold flat, the building is usually insured by the freeholder, but you may still want cover for contents and liability. Your conveyancer will confirm what the lender requires.
