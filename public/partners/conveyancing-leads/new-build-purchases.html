Leaflegal sends conveyancing enquiries from people who are buying, selling or remortgaging a home in England and Wales and want a firm to handle the legal work. Each enquiry goes to one firm only. It is never shared with a panel of other firms and it is never resold once it has been delivered to you.

Conveyancing clients usually contact a firm at a clear point of decision. An offer has been accepted, a buyer has been found, a fixed rate is coming to an end or a lender has asked for a solicitor to be named. At that moment they want a quote, a sense of how long things will take and someone who will pick up the phone. Our enquiries are built around that moment.

We run campaigns aimed at people at these points in a move, ask them a short set of questions about the transaction and confirm that they want to instruct a firm for paid conveyancing. When an enquiry matches the matter types and locations you have chosen, we check it and send it to you straight away, by email or into your CRM.

Why firms add bought leads to their pipeline

Most conveyancing firms rely on estate agent referrals, repeat clients and their own website. Those channels matter, but agent referrals can come with conditions, and paid search for conveyancing terms is crowded and costly to manage. Exclusive leads give you a source of direct enquiries where you decide the volume and the type of work.

Direct clients also tend to have chosen you rather than been handed to you. That can make the relationship simpler, because the client is not looking back to an agent for updates and you can set expectations from the first call.

What we do not do

We are not a comparison site and we do not run a quote engine that sends a buyer five competing prices. We do not tell a client which firm is cheapest or best. We introduce them to one firm that has chosen to receive their kind of matter, and the rest of the relationship is between you and the client.

Leaflegal is not a law firm, is not regulated by the SRA or the CLC and does not give legal advice. You are responsible for being authorised to carry out reserved conveyancing work and for meeting the requirements of any lender you act for.

Conveyancing enquiries come from a wide mix of people, which is why we let you choose the matter types you want. A firm that only handles freehold purchases can receive just those, while a firm with a strong leasehold team can ask for more of that work.

Typical matter types

  • Purchases, including first time buyers
  • Sales of freehold and leasehold homes
  • A sale and purchase together, often in a chain
  • Remortgages, with or without a change of lender
  • Transfers of equity, for example when a partner is added or removed
  • Leasehold flat purchases and sales
  • New build purchases with a developer deadline
  • Buy to let purchases by individual landlords

Where they are in the process

Some people enquire the day an offer is accepted. Others are still viewing and want to line up a firm early so they can move quickly when they find the right property. Sellers often enquire when they put the house on the market, because agents increasingly ask them to instruct a solicitor before a buyer is found. Remortgage clients usually enquire a few months before their current deal ends.

Each lead tells you the stage the client has reached and their rough timescale, so you can decide who to call first and how to pitch your service.

What clients care about

In our experience of the questions people ask, conveyancing clients care about three things above all. They want a clear quote with no surprises later, they want regular updates without having to chase, and they want to know the firm will not hold up their chain. A first call that speaks to those three points usually lands well.

Many clients are anxious about the process, especially first time buyers, so a calm explanation of the main stages helps. They are rarely choosing on price alone, but they do compare quotes, so speed and clarity matter.

Every lead is the full record of what the client told us, in the order they told us. There is no scraped data and nothing is guessed. The aim is to give your quoting team enough to give a figure on the first call.

Pre-screening

Before an enquiry becomes a lead, the client confirms they are looking for paid legal help with a property in England and Wales. Their mobile number and email address are checked. Enquiries that fall outside the areas we cover, or outside your criteria, are filtered out and the person is pointed somewhere more suitable. They never reach your inbox.

Consent

Each client ticks two separate boxes before submitting. One confirms they have read our terms and privacy policy and understand that Leaflegal is an introduction service, not a law firm. The other gives consent for their details to be shared with one firm and for that firm to contact them by phone, email or text about their matter. We record the exact wording and the time, and that record travels with the lead.

Delivery

Leads are sent as soon as they have been checked. You can receive them by email, to a shared intake inbox, or straight into your CRM or case management system using a lead email address or a webhook. There is no platform to log into.

How you handle the first contact makes a large difference to how many enquiries become instructions. The firms that do best treat each lead as a live quote request rather than a name to add to a list.

Call quickly

A conveyancing client who has just had an offer accepted often contacts more than one firm or is being pushed towards the agent's panel firm. Calling while they are still thinking about their move gives you the best chance of being the firm they choose. Set up alerts so the right person sees a lead the moment it arrives.

Quote on the first call if you can

The lead tells you the transaction type, the rough value, the tenure and whether there is a mortgage. That is usually enough for a provisional figure. Follow up the call with a written, itemised quote that sets out your fee, the likely disbursements and anything that could add cost, such as a leasehold supplement or a management pack.

Set expectations

Explain the main stages in plain terms and how you will keep them updated. Many clients have heard stories about slow conveyancing, so telling them how often they will hear from you, and how to reach their case handler, builds trust before they instruct.

Mention the introduction

The client agreed to be contacted by a firm after making an enquiry through Leaflegal. Say so when you introduce yourself. Your client care letter should then cover any referral arrangement you are required to disclose.

Follow up

Not every client instructs on the first call. Some are waiting for a mortgage offer or for the agent to confirm a sale. A short email the same day, a second call a few days later and a final check in after a week often turn a not yet into an instruction. Keep it helpful rather than pushy.

Tell us what works

Let us know which leads instruct and which do not, and why. If remortgages rarely convert for you but leasehold sales do, we can change your matching so you receive more of the work you want.

Firms that review their results every month or so, and share what they see with us, usually find it easier to shape the work they receive over time.

Conveyancing is a reserved legal activity, and buying leads does not change your professional duties. Leaflegal is set up to support those duties rather than work around them.

Referral disclosure

Leaflegal is an introducer. Under the SRA Code of Conduct, you must make sure clients are told in writing about any financial arrangement you have with an introducer, and the arrangement must not affect your independence or your advice. CLC regulated practices have their own rules on referral arrangements, which work along similar lines. Our consent wording tells every client that we are paid by the firms we introduce them to, and we send that wording with each lead so your client care letter can match it.

Independence and client choice

We never steer a client towards a firm for any reason other than the match, and we never give legal advice. The client is free to choose another firm at any point. Nothing in your partner agreement asks you to act in a way that conflicts with the client's interests.

Regulation and lender panels

Only SRA regulated firms, CILEx regulated practices and CLC regulated conveyancers authorised for the work can receive conveyancing leads. You are responsible for being on any lender panel the client's mortgage needs and for your own conflict checks, identity checks and anti money laundering procedures.

Leaflegal is a trading name of Vertical Brands Ltd, which acts as data controller for the information collected through our enquiry forms. Once a lead is delivered, you become the controller of your copy and handle it under your own privacy notice. Leads can only be used to contact the client about their enquiry. Adding them to a marketing list needs separate consent.

Questions and answers

Can I receive only new build purchases leads?
Yes, you can choose new build purchases on its own or alongside other conveyancing matters, subject to the monthly minimum.
Are these leads shared?
No. Each enquiry goes to one firm only.
How quickly do leads arrive?
As soon as the enquiry is checked, so you can call while it is fresh.
Can I quote from the lead alone?
Usually you can give a provisional figure on the first call, because the lead covers the transaction type, rough value, tenure and whether a mortgage is involved. You will still want to confirm the details with the client and send a written quote. Things like a leasehold management pack, a new build or a gifted deposit may change your figure once you know more.
Do you send leasehold purchases and sales?
Yes, if you want them. Every lead says whether the property is freehold or leasehold, and you can choose to receive leasehold matters, leave them out or ask for them only in certain areas. Firms with an experienced leasehold team sometimes prefer this work, while others choose to focus on freehold transactions. We set your matching to suit you.
Are remortgage leads included?
Only if you ask for them. Remortgages, transfers of equity, purchases, sales and combined sale and purchase matters can each be switched on or off. Some firms take remortgages to keep a steady flow of shorter matters, while others prefer to concentrate on purchases. You can change the mix at any time by letting us know.
Do clients know a firm will call them?
Yes. Every client has agreed to be contacted by a firm about their matter, by phone, email or text, and most are expecting a call soon after they submit. It helps to say early in the call that you are getting in touch because of the enquiry they made through Leaflegal. That puts the client at ease and moves the conversation quickly on to their purchase, sale or remortgage.
How many leads will I receive?
That depends on the matter types and locations you choose and the enquiries our campaigns are generating at the time. The minimum is 10 leads a month. Above that, you tell us how many your team can handle and we match to that number. We will give you an honest view on your call of what is realistic for your criteria, but we cannot promise a fixed volume.
Can I choose where my clients are?
Yes. You can choose particular towns, counties or regions, a radius around your offices, or national coverage if you work remotely. Many conveyancing firms act for clients anywhere in England and Wales, in which case wider coverage usually means more matches. If you prefer clients near an office, we can limit matching to those areas.
Can I set a monthly cap?
Yes. You set the number of leads you want each month, as long as it is at least the minimum of 10. Once you reach your cap, we stop matching enquiries to you until the next month. You can raise or lower your cap whenever your team's capacity changes, so you are never sent more enquiries than you can call quickly.
Can I pause leads?
Yes. If your team is at capacity, a case handler is on leave or you are changing systems, tell us and we will pause matching until you are ready. Pausing does not end your partner agreement. When you are ready to restart, let us know and matching begins again as new enquiries come in.
Do you cover the whole of England and Wales?
Our campaigns run across England and Wales, and conveyancing leads relate to properties there. We do not send enquiries about property in Scotland or Northern Ireland, which have their own systems. Within England and Wales, how many leads you receive depends on the areas you choose and the volume of enquiries in those places.
Can leads go to different branches or teams?
Yes. We can route leads by location or matter type, so a purchase near one office goes to that branch and remortgages go to a dedicated team. We can also send everything to a shared intake inbox or your CRM, where you assign leads yourself. We will set up whichever arrangement suits how your firm works.
Do I need to tell clients about the referral fee?
Yes. The SRA Code of Conduct requires you to make sure clients are told in writing about any financial arrangement you have with an introducer. CLC regulated practices have similar rules. Our consent wording already tells every client that we are paid by the firms we introduce them to, and we send that wording with each lead so your client care letter can reflect it.
Is Leaflegal regulated?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service, not a law firm. We are not regulated by the SRA or the CLC and we do not give legal advice. We make this clear to every client before they submit an enquiry. You remain responsible for your own regulatory obligations, including being authorised for reserved conveyancing work.

What clients ask

What is a reservation fee?
It is a payment to the developer that takes a plot off the market for a set time while you arrange your mortgage and legal work. It is usually deducted from the price on completion. Whether you get it back if you pull out depends on the reservation agreement, so read it carefully. Developers registered under the New Homes Quality Code must follow rules about how reservations work.
When should I instruct a conveyancer?
Ideally on the day you reserve, or before. Developers often give a short time to exchange, and every day counts. If you have a firm ready, the developer's solicitor can send the sales pack straight away and searches can be ordered immediately. Waiting even a week can make a tight deadline much harder to meet.
Can I use the developer's recommended solicitor?
You can, but you do not have to. Some developers offer to pay legal fees or other incentives if you use a firm on their list. A firm you choose yourself acts only for you and has no connection to the developer. Whoever you choose, check that the firm is regulated, experienced with new builds and on your lender's panel.
Can I negotiate the price on a new build?
Developers often prefer to offer incentives rather than reduce the headline price, though both are possible. Any incentive must be disclosed to your lender, and some lenders treat incentives as reducing the price for lending purposes. Your conveyancer will check the incentives are recorded properly in the paperwork.
What is the New Homes Quality Code?
It is a code of practice for developers who have registered with the New Homes Quality Board. It sets standards for how homes are marketed and sold, what information buyers are given, how reservations work and how complaints are handled. Registered developers can be referred to the New Homes Ombudsman. Your conveyancer can check whether your developer is registered.
Can I buy a new build home off plan?
Yes. Buying off plan means exchanging before the home is built, often based on plans, a show home and a specification. It can mean a long wait before completion. Your conveyancer will look closely at the long stop date, the developer's right to make changes and what happens to your deposit if the developer runs into problems.
What is part exchange?
Some developers offer to buy your current home as part of the deal, so you do not need to find a buyer yourself. The developer usually values your home and makes an offer, which may be lower than you would get on the open market in exchange for speed and certainty. Your conveyancer would handle the sale to the developer and the new purchase together, and will explain how the two are linked in the contract.
Why are new build deadlines so short?
Developers want sales secured so they can plan their finances and build programme. A deadline to exchange within a few weeks is common. It is not a legal requirement, it is a commercial term. Your conveyancer will tell you what is needed to meet it and let you know early if it looks unrealistic.
What happens if I miss the exchange deadline?
The developer may cancel the reservation and keep some or all of the reservation fee, depending on the agreement. In practice, many developers extend deadlines if the buyer and their conveyancer are clearly working towards exchange and the delay is outside their control. Keep in touch with the sales team and let your conveyancer know if anything is holding you up.
Can the developer change the home after I exchange?
Most contracts allow some changes to materials, specification or layout, as long as the home stays broadly similar. Larger changes may give you rights. Your conveyancer will explain what your contract allows. If a feature really matters to you, such as a particular garden size, it is worth raising before exchange.
What is a long stop date?
It is the last date by which the developer must finish the home. If the home is not ready by then, the contract usually lets you withdraw and get your deposit back. Long stop dates can be many months after the estimated finish date. Your conveyancer will point it out and explain how it fits with your mortgage offer and any rental notice.
What is completion on notice?
On a new build, there is often no fixed completion date when you exchange. Instead, the developer sends notice when the home is finished, and you must complete within the period set by the contract, commonly around ten working days. Your mortgage and deposit need to be ready to go at short notice.
What deposit do I pay on exchange?
Developers often ask for a deposit of a set percentage of the price, though the figure varies and can sometimes be reduced if you have a smaller mortgage deposit. The reservation fee may count towards it. Your conveyancer will confirm what the contract requires and when the money must reach them.
What happens to my deposit if the developer goes out of business?
It depends on how the deposit is held and what protection the warranty provides. Some deposits stay with the developer's solicitor until completion, while others are released to the developer. Warranties often include some insolvency cover for deposits paid before completion, but there are limits and conditions. Your conveyancer will explain the position on your purchase before you exchange.
What if my mortgage offer expires before the home is finished?
Mortgage offers on new builds are often valid for longer than usual, but they still have an end date. If the home is delayed, you may need to ask for an extension or apply again, which could mean different terms. Speak to your broker or lender as soon as delays appear. Your conveyancer can tell you what the contract says about timing.
Can I visit the home before completion?
Many developers offer a pre completion visit or home demonstration so you can see the finished property and point out obvious snags. Access to a building site is usually restricted for safety reasons, so you will need to arrange it with the sales team. Some buyers take a professional snagging inspector with them.
What documents should I get at completion?
Your conveyancer should receive evidence of building regulations sign off and the building warranty documents, which your lender usually requires. You may also receive manuals and guarantees for appliances and systems in the home. Keep everything together, as you will need it when you sell.
Why does Land Registry registration take longer on a new estate?
Each new plot needs to be created as a separate title from the developer's larger title, and HM Land Registry has to approve the estate plan. This can take longer than registering an ordinary purchase. It does not usually affect your ability to live in the home, and your conveyancer will keep you updated.
What should I do about snags after I move in?
Report them to the developer in writing as soon as you find them and keep a copy. Most developers have a customer care team and a period during which they put defects right. If problems are not dealt with, the warranty provider's dispute service or, for registered developers, the New Homes Ombudsman may be able to help.
Can I order upgrades or extras?
Many developers offer upgrades such as different flooring, kitchen finishes or appliances, sometimes as an incentive and sometimes at extra cost. Make sure every extra is confirmed in writing with the price and what is included. Tell your conveyancer, because extras may need to be disclosed to your lender and recorded in the contract. Paid extras can also be relevant to the tax calculation.
What is an estate management charge?
Many new estates have shared areas such as open spaces, private roads, play areas or drainage features that are not adopted by the council. A management company looks after them, and homeowners pay an annual charge. Your conveyancer will explain who runs the company, how the charge is worked out and what happens if it is not paid.
What does road adoption mean?
It means the council takes over responsibility for maintaining the estate roads. Until then, the roads are private and the developer or a management company maintains them. Developers usually sign an agreement with the council, often backed by a bond, to finish the roads to an adoptable standard. Your conveyancer will check what is in place.
Are new build houses ever leasehold?
Some new houses have been sold as leasehold in the past, which drew a lot of criticism. The law on new leasehold houses and on ground rent has been changing. Most new long residential leases granted since mid 2022 can only have a nominal ground rent. Your conveyancer will explain the tenure of the home you are buying and how the current rules apply.
How long does a building warranty last?
A typical warranty lasts ten years from completion. During the early years, often the first two, the developer is usually responsible for fixing defects. Later on, cover is usually limited to certain structural defects. Each warranty provider has its own terms, so ask your conveyancer for the policy details and read them.
Does the warranty cover everything?
No. Warranties usually exclude wear and tear, normal shrinkage cracks, damage caused by the owner and some fittings that have their own guarantees. Claims often have to be made within set time limits. The policy document explains what is covered. Your conveyancer checks the warranty is in place, but questions about a claim go to the provider.
Is buying a new build flat any different?
Yes. A new flat is leasehold, so your conveyancer will review the new lease as well as the developer's contract. They will look at the length of the lease, the service charge estimate, who manages the building, what the reserve fund will be and the rules for residents. Most new long leases now carry only a nominal ground rent. First year service charges are often estimates and can rise once the building is fully occupied.
