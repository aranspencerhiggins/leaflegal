What the client is looking for

Conveyancing for your purchase, from offer accepted to keys in hand.

Once your offer is accepted, a conveyancer does the legal work to make sure you get good title to the property. They check the title deeds, order searches, review the contract and the information the seller provides, and raise questions with the seller’s solicitor about anything that looks wrong.

What the client expects from you

  • Checking the title and any restrictions on the property
  • Ordering local authority, water, drainage and environmental searches
  • Reviewing the contract, fixtures and fittings and property information forms
  • Reporting to you on anything unusual before you commit
  • Exchanging contracts, completing and registering your ownership

Conveyancing enquiries come from a wide mix of people, which is why we let you choose the matter types you want. A firm that only handles freehold purchases can receive just those, while a firm with a strong leasehold team can ask for more of that work.

Typical matter types

  • Purchases, including first time buyers
  • Sales of freehold and leasehold homes
  • A sale and purchase together, often in a chain
  • Remortgages, with or without a change of lender
  • Transfers of equity, for example when a partner is added or removed
  • Leasehold flat purchases and sales
  • New build purchases with a developer deadline
  • Buy to let purchases by individual landlords

Where they are in the process

Some people enquire the day an offer is accepted. Others are still viewing and want to line up a firm early so they can move quickly when they find the right property. Sellers often enquire when they put the house on the market, because agents increasingly ask them to instruct a solicitor before a buyer is found. Remortgage clients usually enquire a few months before their current deal ends.

Each lead tells you the stage the client has reached and their rough timescale, so you can decide who to call first and how to pitch your service.

What clients care about

In our experience of the questions people ask, conveyancing clients care about three things above all. They want a clear quote with no surprises later, they want regular updates without having to chase, and they want to know the firm will not hold up their chain. A first call that speaks to those three points usually lands well.

Many clients are anxious about the process, especially first time buyers, so a calm explanation of the main stages helps. They are rarely choosing on price alone, but they do compare quotes, so speed and clarity matter.

Every lead is the full record of what the client told us, in the order they told us. There is no scraped data and nothing is guessed. The aim is to give your quoting team enough to give a figure on the first call.

Delivery

Leads are sent as soon as they have been checked. You can receive them by email, to a shared intake inbox, or straight into your CRM or case management system using a lead email address or a webhook. There is no platform to log into.

How you handle the first contact makes a large difference to how many enquiries become instructions. The firms that do best treat each lead as a live quote request rather than a name to add to a list.

Call quickly

A conveyancing client who has just had an offer accepted often contacts more than one firm or is being pushed towards the agent's panel firm. Calling while they are still thinking about their move gives you the best chance of being the firm they choose. Set up alerts so the right person sees a lead the moment it arrives.

Quote on the first call if you can

The lead tells you the transaction type, the rough value, the tenure and whether there is a mortgage. That is usually enough for a provisional figure. Follow up the call with a written, itemised quote that sets out your fee, the likely disbursements and anything that could add cost, such as a leasehold supplement or a management pack.

Set expectations

Explain the main stages in plain terms and how you will keep them updated. Many clients have heard stories about slow conveyancing, so telling them how often they will hear from you, and how to reach their case handler, builds trust before they instruct.

Mention the introduction

The client agreed to be contacted by a firm after making an enquiry through Leaflegal. Say so when you introduce yourself. Your client care letter should then cover any referral arrangement you are required to disclose.

Follow up

Not every client instructs on the first call. Some are waiting for a mortgage offer or for the agent to confirm a sale. A short email the same day, a second call a few days later and a final check in after a week often turn a not yet into an instruction. Keep it helpful rather than pushy.

Tell us what works

Let us know which leads instruct and which do not, and why. If remortgages rarely convert for you but leasehold sales do, we can change your matching so you receive more of the work you want.

Firms that review their results every month or so, and share what they see with us, usually find it easier to shape the work they receive over time.

Conveyancing is a reserved legal activity, and buying leads does not change your professional duties. Leaflegal is set up to support those duties rather than work around them.

What clients ask

Can I instruct a conveyancer before I find a property?
Yes. Many buyers choose a firm while they are still looking, so that the firm can carry out identity and money checks early. When an offer is accepted, the estate agent can send the memorandum of sale straight away and the work can begin without delay. This can make your offer more attractive to a seller.
What will my conveyancer need from me at the start?
Expect to provide photo ID, proof of address, details of your mortgage broker or lender, and evidence of where your deposit and any other funds are coming from. If family are giving you money, the firm will need their details too. You will also sign the firm's terms of business and pay money on account for searches.
What is a memorandum of sale?
It is a document the estate agent sends to both conveyancers once an offer is accepted. It sets out the agreed price, the names of the buyer and seller, the property address and the details of each side's conveyancer. It is not a contract and does not commit anyone, but it starts the legal process.
Does the seller's conveyancer act for me too?
No. The seller's conveyancer acts only for the seller. Your own conveyancer acts for you and, if you have a mortgage, usually for your lender as well. Each side's conveyancer looks after their own client's interests, which is why questions pass between them in writing.
Can I buy a property with someone who is not my partner?
Yes. Friends, siblings and parents and children buy together quite often. It is common in these situations to own as tenants in common and to record who has paid what in a declaration of trust, along with what happens if one person wants to sell. Your conveyancer can explain how this works.
Can I buy without a mortgage?
Yes. Cash buyers still need the full legal checks, including the title and searches, because the risks to the buyer are the same. Searches are optional without a lender, but skipping them means you may not learn about issues until later. Cash purchases can be quicker because there is no mortgage offer to wait for.
What if the property is being sold by someone who has died?
When a property is sold by the executors of someone who has died, the executors usually need a grant of probate, or letters of administration, before the sale can complete. Your conveyancer will check who has authority to sell and whether the grant has been issued. Probate sales can take longer, and the sellers may know less about the property's history.
Which searches will my conveyancer order?
Most buyers with a mortgage will have a local authority search, a water and drainage search and an environmental search. Depending on the area, your conveyancer may add others, such as a mining search, a chancel repair check or a flood report. They will explain which searches they recommend and why.
How long do searches take?
It depends largely on the local authority. Some councils return searches within days, while others can take several weeks. Water, drainage and environmental searches are usually quicker. Your conveyancer can tell you the typical turnaround for the area you are buying in.
What if a search shows a problem?
Your conveyancer will explain what the result means and whether it affects the property, your mortgage or your plans. Some results are routine and need no action. Others may need further enquiries, specialist reports or insurance. The decision about whether to go ahead remains yours.
Do I need a survey as well as searches?
Searches tell you about legal and local authority matters. A survey looks at the physical condition of the building. A lender's valuation is not a survey and is mainly for the lender's benefit. Many buyers commission their own survey, especially for older properties or homes that have been altered.
What are restrictive covenants?
They are promises in old deeds that limit what can be done with a property, such as not running a business from it or not building without someone's consent. Some are out of date or unlikely to be enforced, while others still matter. Your conveyancer will point out any that affect you.
What is indemnity insurance?
It is a one off insurance policy that covers the financial risk of a specific legal problem, such as a missing building regulation certificate or a lack of a formal right of way. It does not fix the problem itself. Your conveyancer will explain what a policy covers and its limits.
What is the report on title?
Before you sign the contract, your conveyancer will usually send you a written report. It summarises the property's title, the results of the searches, the seller's answers, the key terms of the contract and anything unusual. Read it carefully and ask about anything you do not understand, as it is your chance to raise concerns before you are committed.
When do I pay my deposit?
Your deposit is usually transferred to your conveyancer shortly before exchange, so that it is ready to be sent to the seller's conveyancer when contracts are exchanged. Your firm will tell you how much is needed and by when. Allow time for the bank transfer to clear and confirm the account details by phone.
What happens if my mortgage valuation is low?
If the lender values the property at less than the agreed price, it may lend less than you hoped. You might renegotiate the price with the seller, put in more of your own money or look at other lenders. Your broker can help with the lending side, and your conveyancer can explain how any price change is handled.
Can family members help with the deposit?
Yes, this is common. Lenders and conveyancers need to know about any gift, and the person giving it will usually sign a gift letter and provide identity and bank statements. If the money is a loan or comes with conditions, it must be disclosed, as it can affect your mortgage.
What if my mortgage offer expires?
Mortgage offers are valid for a limited time. If your purchase is delayed, you may need to ask your lender for an extension or a new offer. Keep your broker informed about the expected timetable, and tell your conveyancer if the offer is close to expiring.
What is a completion statement?
It is a document from your conveyancer showing the money coming in, such as your mortgage and your own funds, and the money going out, such as the purchase price, fees, disbursements and tax. It tells you exactly how much you need to send before completion.
When is Stamp Duty or Land Transaction Tax paid?
Your conveyancer usually collects the tax from you before completion and submits the return and payment after completion, within a strict deadline. If the return is late, penalties and interest can apply. The amount depends on the price and your circumstances, and the rules change, so your firm will confirm the figure.
Why does my conveyancer need so much detail about my savings?
Regulated firms must check the source of funds for every purchase under money laundering rules. That usually means bank statements covering several months and an explanation of where larger sums came from, such as savings, a bonus, an inheritance or the sale of shares. It is a legal requirement and applies to every client, not just you.
What does exchange of contracts mean?
Exchange is the moment the contract becomes binding. Your conveyancer and the seller's conveyancer agree by phone that the signed contracts are exchanged, and your deposit is sent. From then on, if you do not complete, you could lose your deposit and be liable for the seller's losses.
Can I choose my completion date?
The completion date is agreed between buyer and seller before exchange, and in a chain everyone must agree the same date. You can suggest a date, but it needs to work for others too. Avoid booking removals until contracts have exchanged and the date is fixed.
What happens if completion is delayed?
If one side does not complete on the agreed date, the contract usually allows the other side to charge interest and, after a notice period, to cancel the contract and keep the deposit or claim losses. Delays are often short, but your conveyancer will explain what your contract says if it happens.
When do I get the keys?
You can usually collect the keys once the seller's conveyancer confirms they have received the money. The estate agent then releases the keys. In a chain, money often has to pass along several transactions, so the time you get the keys can vary on the day.
Can I visit the property between exchange and completion?
You have no automatic right to enter before completion, but sellers often agree to a visit to measure up or check something. Arrange it through the estate agent. Some buyers like to visit before completion to make sure fixtures and fittings agreed in the contract are still there.
Can I pull out after exchange?
Once contracts are exchanged, you are legally bound to complete. Withdrawing at that stage usually means losing your deposit and possibly paying the seller's extra losses, such as the cost of a delayed sale. That is why your conveyancer will check that your mortgage and funds are in place, and that you are ready, before exchanging.
