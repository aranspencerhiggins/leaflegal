Referral disclosure

Family enquiries go to SRA regulated firms and CILEx regulated practices authorised for the work. People usually contact a family solicitor at one of the hardest points in their life. A relationship has ended, a former partner has made a proposal about money, a court form has arrived or arrangements for the children have broken down. They want to understand where they stand and they often want to speak to someone the same day.

Since legal aid was withdrawn for most private family matters, the great majority of these clients pay privately. Every Leaflegal family enquiry has confirmed that the person is looking for paid help rather than legal aid or free advice, so your first call starts from that shared understanding.

How the enquiries are generated

Our campaigns run on social media, search and helpful content aimed at people going through a separation. Each person answers a short set of questions about the kind of help they need, whether anything has started at court, how urgent it is and where they are. When the enquiry fits the matter types and areas you have chosen, we check it and send it to you straight away.

Why exclusive matters in family work

Family clients are choosing someone to guide them through something personal and painful. If they are called by four firms within an hour, the experience can feel overwhelming, and the firm that wins is often simply the one that called first. Sending each enquiry to one firm gives you room to have a proper, unhurried conversation and to show the client why they should trust you.

Leaflegal is not a law firm and does not give legal advice. We do not comment on a client's options or tell them what to expect from a settlement or a court. We introduce them to one regulated firm, and the advice comes from you.

Where they are in the process

Some people enquire before they have told their partner they want to separate. Others have already started an online divorce and now need help with the finances. Some have received a court application or a letter from the other side's solicitor and need someone quickly. Each lead says whether proceedings have started and whether there is a hearing date, so you can tell the difference before you call.

Since no fault divorce came in, more people start the divorce themselves and come to a solicitor for the finances or the children. Many of the enquiries you receive will therefore be about money or arrangements for children, alongside straightforward divorce work.

What clients want from the first call

Most family clients want three things early on. They want to feel listened to, they want a clear explanation of what usually happens next, and they want to know what it might cost. People are often frightened about fees running out of control, so a fixed fee first meeting or a clear estimate for the first stage helps them say yes.

Clients also differ in how much they already know. Some have read widely and arrive with specific questions about pensions or the family home. Others know very little and need the basics explained gently. The description in the client's own words usually gives a good sense of which kind of conversation to expect.

Planning ahead

Not every enquiry is a crisis. Couples planning a wedding or civil partnership enquire about prenuptial agreements, and separated couples who get on well ask about separation agreements or consent orders. These clients are usually calmer and often want a fixed price for a defined piece of work.

A note on the best time to call

In family matters, the time and way you contact someone can matter more than usual. A client may still live with their partner or may not want to be overheard. Each lead includes the best time to call, and it is worth respecting that closely. If a client does not pick up, a short, discreet message usually works better than a detailed voicemail.

Family enquiries reward firms that respond quickly and with warmth. People contact a solicitor at a moment of crisis, and the first voice they hear often shapes who they instruct.

Respond the same day

Aim to call soon after a lead arrives, at the time the client asked for. A same day call shows that you take their situation seriously. Where a lead mentions a hearing date or a court application, route it to a fee earner who can deal with urgent work.

Listen first

Use what the client has already told us. Opening with a reference to their situation, such as a recent separation and a question about the house, shows you have read their enquiry and lets them talk. Many family clients need to tell their story before they can take in information about process or cost.

Offer a clear first step

A fixed fee first meeting, or a clear estimate for the first stage of the work, makes it easier for anxious clients to commit. Explain what they will get from that meeting and what they might bring, in general terms. Be honest that later costs depend on how the matter unfolds.

Mention the introduction

The client agreed to be contacted after making an enquiry through Leaflegal. Mention that early in the call, and cover any referral arrangement in your client care letter as the SRA Code of Conduct requires.

Follow up gently

Some clients are not ready to instruct straight away. They may be waiting to see how a conversation with their partner goes, or saving for fees. A kind follow up email and a second call a few days later is often welcome. Keep the tone supportive rather than sales led.

Tell us what works

Let us know which leads instruct and which do not. If contested finances convert well for you but simple divorce enquiries do not, we can change your matching so you receive more of the work you want.

Firms that look at their results every month or so, and share what they see with us, usually find it easier to shape the mix of family work they receive. Small changes, such as adjusting areas or switching off one matter type, can make a real difference to how well the leads fit your team.

What clients ask

Is everything split 50 50?
Not automatically. The court looks at a range of factors, including needs, resources, contributions and the length of the marriage. Equal sharing of assets built up during the marriage is often a starting point, but needs, especially housing for children, frequently lead to a different split. A solicitor can explain how this works in your situation.
Do we need a court order if we agree?
An agreement is not legally binding on its own. To make it binding and close off future claims, it is usually turned into a consent order approved by the court. Without one, claims can remain open for years, even after the divorce is final. The order also gives both people a clear way to enforce the terms if they are not followed.
When should we sort out finances?
Many couples start during the divorce, often in the 20 week reflection period. A consent order cannot be approved until the conditional order has been made. Solicitors often discuss delaying the final order until the financial order is in place, to protect certain rights. Starting early can also make the separation feel more settled for everyone.
What if we divorced years ago without a financial order?
Financial claims can stay open after divorce unless a court order has dealt with them. A former spouse may still be able to apply, although delay is one of the things the court takes into account. If you remarried before making a claim, your own claims may be limited. A solicitor can explain your position.
Does it matter whose name things are in?
Less than many people think. In a divorce, the court can look at assets held by either spouse, whether they are in joint names or one name. Legal ownership is still relevant, but it does not decide how assets will be divided. Assets held by companies or trusts can also be relevant. A solicitor can explain how ownership affects your case.
Does the reason for the divorce affect the money?
Usually not. The court only takes conduct into account in rare cases where it would be unfair to ignore it, such as serious financial misconduct. Most of the time, who was to blame for the breakdown makes no difference to the settlement. Under no fault divorce, the reasons for the breakdown are not part of the divorce application either.
Are unmarried couples treated the same way?
No. Unmarried couples do not have the same financial claims when they separate, however long they lived together. Disputes about a shared home are usually decided under property law. There may be claims for the benefit of children. A solicitor can explain the difference. This is why some couples who live together choose to make a cohabitation agreement.
Can we agree a settlement without solicitors?
Some couples do reach agreement themselves, sometimes with a mediator. To become legally binding, the agreement still needs to be turned into a consent order and approved by the court. Many people ask a solicitor to check the agreement and draft the order, so that it does what they intend.
What is Form E?
Form E is the standard financial statement used in financial cases in England and Wales. It sets out your income, assets, debts, pensions and needs, with supporting documents. It is used in court proceedings and is often used voluntarily in negotiation too, so that both people have the same information.
What documents will I need?
Typically twelve months of bank statements, recent payslips or tax returns, pension valuations, mortgage statements, details of savings and investments, and business accounts if you have a business. Your solicitor will give you a checklist. Gathering documents early can save time and costs. If you do not have access to some documents, such as joint account statements, tell your solicitor, as there are ways of obtaining them.
What if my ex will not give full disclosure?
The court can order disclosure, and can draw conclusions about someone's finances if they fail to provide it. Costs orders can also be made against people who do not cooperate. Your solicitor can explain the options for obtaining the information. In serious cases, a settlement reached without full disclosure can later be set aside. Keep a note of anything you think is missing and share it with your solicitor.
Can I look at my partner's private documents?
Be careful. Taking or copying documents that belong to your partner, or accessing their emails or accounts without permission, can be unlawful and can harm your case. Tell your solicitor what you know or suspect, and they will explain the proper ways to obtain information. Documents you already have lawful access to, such as joint statements, are different.
What is a pension report?
In some cases a specialist actuary is asked to prepare a report comparing the value of pensions and suggesting how they might be shared fairly. These reports are often useful where pensions are large or of different types. Your solicitor will explain whether one is likely to be needed. The cost is often shared between both people.
Do I have to disclose money I have received since we separated?
Yes. Disclosure covers your current finances, not just those at the date of separation. New income, an inheritance or a change of job all need to be disclosed, and the duty continues until the case ends. Your solicitor will explain how assets acquired after separation are usually treated. Failing to disclose can be costly later.
Can I stay in the family home?
It depends on the assets available, both people's housing needs, and the needs of any children. Options include one person buying the other out, or staying in the home for a period before it is sold. A solicitor can explain what might be realistic in your circumstances. Mortgage capacity and the cost of rehousing are often key.
Are pensions included?
Yes. Pensions are often one of the most valuable assets, and can be shared, offset against other assets or attached. Pensions built up before the marriage may be treated differently from those built up during it. Your solicitor will explain the options. Pensions are sometimes overlooked because their value is not obvious, so make sure you have up to date valuations before any agreement is reached.
Is an inheritance protected?
Not always. Inherited money and assets may be treated differently from assets built up together, but they can still be shared if they are needed to meet either person's needs or have been mixed with family money. How an inheritance is treated depends on the facts of each case. Expected future inheritances are usually treated differently.
What happens to my business?
A business may need to be valued, and the court will consider how much can be taken from it without harming it. Options include one person keeping the business and the other receiving other assets. Your solicitor will explain how business assets are usually dealt with. Valuations can take time, so it helps to start early. Shares in a family business, partnerships and farms can raise particular issues.
What happens to joint debts?
Joint debts, such as a mortgage or loan, usually remain the responsibility of both people as far as the lender is concerned. A settlement can decide who repays what between you, but it does not change the agreement with the lender unless the lender agrees. It can help to tell lenders about the separation and keep paying while things are resolved, to protect both credit records.
What about savings or property abroad?
Assets abroad are still part of the financial picture and must be disclosed. Enforcing an order over property in another country can be more complicated. A solicitor with international experience can explain how these assets are usually dealt with. The court can still take them into account, and can make orders against a person in England and Wales even if the property is overseas. Valuations and currency changes may need to be considered.
Can I keep my pension if my spouse keeps the house?
This is called offsetting, and it is a common way of dealing with pensions. It can work well, but comparing a pension with a share of a house is not straightforward, because they are very different kinds of asset. A solicitor, and sometimes a pension expert, can help you understand what you would be giving up.
What is spousal maintenance?
It is regular payments from one former spouse to the other to help meet their income needs. It may be ordered for a fixed period or, less commonly, for longer. The court will consider whether the receiving person can adjust to independence over time. How much is paid depends on needs, income and earning capacity. It is not paid automatically, and in many cases there is a clean break instead.
How is child maintenance dealt with?
Child maintenance is usually arranged between parents or through the Child Maintenance Service, which uses a set formula. In some cases child maintenance can be included in a court order by agreement. Your solicitor can explain how it fits alongside a financial settlement. Child maintenance is separate from time with the children, and one does not depend on the other.
Can maintenance be changed later?
Usually yes. Maintenance orders can generally be varied if either person's circumstances change significantly. Some orders include terms that prevent the length being extended. Maintenance for a former spouse normally ends if they remarry. Changes are usually made by agreement or by applying to the court, and the court looks at all the circumstances, including any change in income, health or living arrangements. A solicitor can explain whether a variation might be possible.
What is a clean break?
A clean break means former spouses have no ongoing financial claims against each other once the order is carried out. The court must consider whether a clean break is possible. It is not always suitable, for example where one person cannot meet their needs without support. Where maintenance is needed, a clean break may still be possible later, once the maintenance term ends.
Can I get money to live on before the case ends?
Yes, in some cases. If agreement cannot be reached about temporary support, the court can order interim maintenance to cover essential needs until the case is finished. A solicitor can explain whether this might be appropriate and how it is applied for. Temporary arrangements can often be agreed without going to court.
