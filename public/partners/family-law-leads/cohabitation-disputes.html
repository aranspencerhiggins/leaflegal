Leaflegal sends family law enquiries from people in England and Wales who want to pay a solicitor to help with a separation, divorce, money or children matter. Each enquiry goes to one firm only. It is never sent to several firms at once and it is never resold after delivery.

People usually contact a family solicitor at one of the hardest points in their life. A relationship has ended, a former partner has made a proposal about money, a court form has arrived or arrangements for the children have broken down. They want to understand where they stand and they often want to speak to someone the same day.

Since legal aid was withdrawn for most private family matters, the great majority of these clients pay privately. Every Leaflegal family enquiry has confirmed that the person is looking for paid help rather than legal aid or free advice, so your first call starts from that shared understanding.

How the enquiries are generated

Our campaigns run on social media, search and helpful content aimed at people going through a separation. Each person answers a short set of questions about the kind of help they need, whether anything has started at court, how urgent it is and where they are. When the enquiry fits the matter types and areas you have chosen, we check it and send it to you straight away.

Why exclusive matters in family work

Family clients are choosing someone to guide them through something personal and painful. If they are called by four firms within an hour, the experience can feel overwhelming, and the firm that wins is often simply the one that called first. Sending each enquiry to one firm gives you room to have a proper, unhurried conversation and to show the client why they should trust you.

What Leaflegal is not

Leaflegal is not a law firm and does not give legal advice. We do not comment on a client's options or tell them what to expect from a settlement or a court. We introduce them to one regulated firm, and the advice comes from you.

Family enquiries cover a wide range of work. You choose which matter types you want, so a practice built around complex financial remedy work does not have to take simple uncontested divorces unless it wants them.

Matter types you can choose

  • Divorce and dissolution of a civil partnership
  • Financial settlements and consent orders
  • Child arrangements
  • Cohabitation disputes, including property disputes between unmarried couples
  • Prenuptial and postnuptial agreements
  • Separation agreements

Where they are in the process

Some people enquire before they have told their partner they want to separate. Others have already started an online divorce and now need help with the finances. Some have received a court application or a letter from the other side's solicitor and need someone quickly. Each lead says whether proceedings have started and whether there is a hearing date, so you can tell the difference before you call.

Since no fault divorce came in, more people start the divorce themselves and come to a solicitor for the finances or the children. Many of the enquiries you receive will therefore be about money or arrangements for children, alongside straightforward divorce work.

What clients want from the first call

Most family clients want three things early on. They want to feel listened to, they want a clear explanation of what usually happens next, and they want to know what it might cost. People are often frightened about fees running out of control, so a fixed fee first meeting or a clear estimate for the first stage helps them say yes.

Clients also differ in how much they already know. Some have read widely and arrive with specific questions about pensions or the family home. Others know very little and need the basics explained gently. The description in the client's own words usually gives a good sense of which kind of conversation to expect.

Planning ahead

Not every enquiry is a crisis. Couples planning a wedding or civil partnership enquire about prenuptial agreements, and separated couples who get on well ask about separation agreements or consent orders. These clients are usually calmer and often want a fixed price for a defined piece of work.

Every family lead is the full record of what the client told us, in the order they told us. We keep the questions short because the subject is sensitive, but each lead gives your team enough to make a well prepared first call.

A note on the best time to call

In family matters, the time and way you contact someone can matter more than usual. A client may still live with their partner or may not want to be overheard. Each lead includes the best time to call, and it is worth respecting that closely. If a client does not pick up, a short, discreet message usually works better than a detailed voicemail.

Pre-screening

Every client confirms they want paid help with a family matter in England and Wales, and their mobile number and email address are checked. Enquiries that fall outside our scope or your criteria are filtered out and the person is pointed somewhere more suitable, so they never reach you.

Consent and delivery

Each client ticks one box to confirm they understand our terms, our privacy policy and that Leaflegal is an introduction service, and a separate box to agree to one firm contacting them by phone, email or text. We record the wording and the time and send it with the lead. Leads arrive as soon as they are checked, by email, to a shared inbox or into your CRM or case management system using a lead email address or a webhook.

Family enquiries reward firms that respond quickly and with warmth. People contact a solicitor at a moment of crisis, and the first voice they hear often shapes who they instruct.

Respond the same day

Aim to call soon after a lead arrives, at the time the client asked for. A same day call shows that you take their situation seriously. Where a lead mentions a hearing date or a court application, route it to a fee earner who can deal with urgent work.

Listen first

Use what the client has already told us. Opening with a reference to their situation, such as a recent separation and a question about the house, shows you have read their enquiry and lets them talk. Many family clients need to tell their story before they can take in information about process or cost.

Offer a clear first step

A fixed fee first meeting, or a clear estimate for the first stage of the work, makes it easier for anxious clients to commit. Explain what they will get from that meeting and what they might bring, in general terms. Be honest that later costs depend on how the matter unfolds.

Mention the introduction

The client agreed to be contacted after making an enquiry through Leaflegal. Mention that early in the call, and cover any referral arrangement in your client care letter as the SRA Code of Conduct requires.

Follow up gently

Some clients are not ready to instruct straight away. They may be waiting to see how a conversation with their partner goes, or saving for fees. A kind follow up email and a second call a few days later is often welcome. Keep the tone supportive rather than sales led.

Tell us what works

Let us know which leads instruct and which do not. If contested finances convert well for you but simple divorce enquiries do not, we can change your matching so you receive more of the work you want.

Firms that look at their results every month or so, and share what they see with us, usually find it easier to shape the mix of family work they receive. Small changes, such as adjusting areas or switching off one matter type, can make a real difference to how well the leads fit your team.

Family work raises particular compliance points because clients are often vulnerable and the information they share is sensitive. Leaflegal is set up to support your professional duties.

What clients ask

Do cohabiting couples have the same rights as married couples?
No. In England and Wales, living together does not give the same financial rights as marriage or civil partnership, however long you have been together. The idea of a common law spouse is a myth. Your rights depend mainly on property and trust law and on any agreements you made.
I have lived with my partner for years. Does that give me a claim?
The length of the relationship alone does not create a claim. What matters is how the property is owned, what each of you contributed and what was agreed or intended about ownership. A long relationship may mean there is more evidence of shared intentions, but each case depends on its facts.
Can I claim a share of a house in my partner's sole name?
Possibly. You may have a claim if there was a shared understanding that you would have an interest and you relied on it to your disadvantage. Paying towards the mortgage, the deposit or major improvements can be relevant. A solicitor will look at the evidence and explain your position. Evidence is often key.
We own our home jointly. Do we each get half?
Not always. It depends on whether you are joint tenants or tenants in common, whether there is a declaration of trust and what you intended. Joint owners are often presumed to share equally unless there is good reason to think otherwise, but that presumption can sometimes be displaced. Your solicitor will look at the documents and the history.
What is a declaration of trust?
It is a document that sets out how a property is owned and how proceeds will be divided if it is sold. If you signed one when you bought your home, it is usually the starting point in any dispute. Your conveyancing solicitor may hold a copy if you are unsure.
Does paying household bills give me a share in the home?
Not on its own, as a rule. Paying for food and bills while your partner paid the mortgage can sometimes be relevant, but courts tend to give more weight to direct payments towards buying the property. The overall picture and what you both intended also matter. A solicitor can explain how your contributions might be viewed.
My partner promised I would always have a home. Does that count?
It might. If a clear promise was made and you relied on it in a significant way, such as giving up your own home or working unpaid in a family business, the law may sometimes give you a remedy. These claims depend heavily on evidence, so try to keep any messages or records.
Can I stay in the home while we sort things out?
That depends on whether you own or rent it and in whose name. A joint owner usually has a right to live there. A partner with no legal ownership may have more limited rights. A solicitor can explain your position, and you should tell them if you have any safety concerns.
My partner moved into my home. Can they claim part of it?
A partner does not gain a share of your home simply by living there. A claim usually needs evidence of a shared intention that they would have an interest, and that they relied on it. Payments towards the mortgage or major works, or promises you made, could be relevant. A solicitor can explain the risk in your situation.
What is a TOLATA claim?
It is a claim under the Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996. It allows the court to decide who owns what share of a property and whether it should be sold. Most cohabitation property disputes are brought this way, in the civil courts rather than the family court.
Do we have to go to court?
No. Many disputes are settled through negotiation or mediation. Courts expect people to try to resolve things before starting proceedings, and the threat of paying the other side's costs encourages settlement. Court is usually a last resort. Settling also gives you more control over the outcome, because you agree terms rather than having them decided for you. Your solicitor can explain the options for resolving things without a hearing.
How long does a cohabitation dispute take?
It varies. Some settle within weeks after an exchange of letters. Contested court cases can take a year or more to reach a final hearing. Your solicitor can give you a better idea once they understand the facts and how the other side is responding. Keeping evidence organised and responding promptly can help avoid delay.
What evidence will I need?
Useful evidence often includes the title documents, any declaration of trust, mortgage statements, bank statements showing payments, and emails or messages about who would own what. Notes of important conversations can help too. Gather what you can, but do not take documents that belong only to your partner. Your solicitor will explain what is relevant.
Can the court order the house to be sold?
Yes, the court can order a sale and decide how the money is divided. It will consider factors such as the purpose for which the property was bought, the welfare of any children living there and the interests of anyone with a mortgage over it. It can also decide how sale costs are shared.
What if my partner refuses to sell?
A joint owner cannot usually force a sale without the other's agreement or a court order. If agreement is not possible, either owner can ask the court to decide. Your solicitor can explain the steps and how long they might take. In the meantime, both owners usually remain responsible for the mortgage. Some couples agree that one person will buy the other out instead of selling.
What happens to belongings like the car or furniture?
Generally, items belong to whoever bought them or was given them, though shared purchases can be harder to untangle. Most couples resolve this through discussion because the value rarely justifies court action. A solicitor can help you approach it sensibly. Cars on finance can be more complex, because the finance agreement is with the lender. Pets are usually treated as belongings in law, though many couples agree arrangements that suit everyone.
What is a letter of claim?
It is a letter sent before court proceedings that sets out the claim, the facts relied on and what is being asked for. The other side is given a reasonable time to reply. Courts expect this step, and failing to take it can affect who pays costs later. It is usually prepared by a solicitor.
Can I protect my interest while the dispute is going on?
In some cases a solicitor can register a restriction or notice at HM Land Registry, which can make it harder for the property to be sold or mortgaged without you knowing. Whether this is possible depends on the circumstances. Joint owners may also be able to change how they hold the property.
Can I claim money for our children?
Yes. Child maintenance is usually arranged through the Child Maintenance Service or by agreement. In addition, a parent can sometimes apply to the court for further orders for the child's benefit, such as housing or a lump sum, under Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989. These claims sit alongside any property dispute.
Can I stay in the home because of the children?
The court can sometimes order that a home is provided for a child until they finish their education, after which it may return to the other parent. This is for the child's benefit rather than the parent's. A solicitor can explain whether it is realistic in your case. Each case depends on the family's circumstances.
Does an unmarried father have parental responsibility?
An unmarried father usually has parental responsibility if he is named on the birth certificate of a child registered after 1 December 2003. Otherwise he can gain it by agreement with the mother or by court order. Parental responsibility is separate from questions about money. A solicitor can explain how it can be obtained if needed.
Can I claim maintenance for myself?
Generally no. Unlike married couples, cohabiting partners cannot usually claim maintenance for themselves when they separate. Any claim for money is normally made for the benefit of children. Your solicitor can explain what might be available in your situation. In some cases, orders for the children may indirectly help the parent who cares for them, for example by providing a home. A solicitor can explain how this might apply to you.
How are arrangements for the children decided?
Most parents agree arrangements themselves or with the help of mediation. If that is not possible, a parent can apply for a child arrangements order, usually after attending a mediation information meeting. The court's main concern is the child's welfare. Most parents agree without a court ever being involved. A solicitor can explain the process if agreement is not possible.
Does it matter if the children are not both of ours?
Claims under Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989 are usually made against a child's legal parent. If a child is not your partner's child, claims against them for that child are more limited. Every family is different, so a solicitor will want to understand who is in your household.
