Motoring enquiries come from a broad range of drivers. You choose the offence types you want, so a firm that concentrates on serious charges does not have to take routine speeding matters unless it wants them.

Offence types you can choose

  • Drink driving and drug driving, including failing to provide a specimen
  • Speeding
  • Totting up and exceptional hardship
  • Careless driving and dangerous driving
  • Driving without insurance
  • Failing to identify the driver
  • Using a mobile phone while driving
  • Driving while disqualified or other licence matters

Typical clients

Many motoring clients are professional drivers, such as delivery and HGV drivers, sales staff and tradespeople, for whom a ban could mean losing their job. Others are business owners, carers, parents with school runs or people living in rural areas with little public transport. A good number are new drivers worried about the rules that apply in their first two years on the road.

Where they are in the process

Some drivers enquire as soon as a notice of intended prosecution arrives, before they have responded to it. Others have a court date coming up, have received a single justice procedure notice or have already pleaded and want help with an exceptional hardship argument or mitigation. Each lead says whether there is a court date and what it is, if the client knows it.

What clients want to know

Drivers usually want a clear answer to three questions. Can you help, what will it cost and what happens next. Most also want to understand, in general terms, how the process works in the magistrates' court and what kind of outcomes are possible. A firm that explains this calmly, and gives a fixed fee for the work, tends to win their trust.

Every motoring lead is the full record of what the driver told us. The questions are short, but they cover what your team needs to triage the matter and quote on the first call.

What each motoring lead includes

  • The client's name, a verified UK mobile number and a verified email address
  • The type of offence
  • The court date, if the client knows it
  • The points currently on their licence
  • Whether the client thinks a ban is likely
  • Where they are and which court area is likely to be involved
  • A short description in the client's own words
  • The best time to call
  • The source, a timestamp and the consent record

Why current points matter

Current points help you see quickly whether a matter could lead to a totting up disqualification. Under the totting up rules, a driver who reaches 12 or more points within three years will usually face a disqualification unless the court accepts that exceptional hardship would result. A driver with nine points facing a speeding charge is in a very different position from one with a clean licence, and the lead lets you see that before you call.

Points also matter for newer drivers. A driver who reaches six or more points within two years of passing their test will usually have their licence revoked under the new driver rules. A new driver facing even a modest offence may therefore be very anxious, and the lead helps you spot that.

Whether a ban is likely

We ask the client whether they think a ban is likely. This is the client's own view, not an assessment by us, and it may not be accurate. It is still useful, because it tells you what the client is worried about and what they will want to talk about first.

Call within the hour if you can

A driver with a court date coming up will not wait long. Calling soon after the lead arrives, at the time they asked for, shows you take their deadline seriously. Set up alerts so the right fee earner or solicitor advocate sees each lead straight away, including late in the day.

Start with the date and the points

The lead tells you the offence, the court date and the client's current points. Opening with those shows that you have read their enquiry. It also lets you deal first with anything urgent, such as a response deadline on a notice or a hearing later that week.

Give a clear fee

Most private motoring clients want a fixed fee for each stage, such as advice and representation at a first hearing, an exceptional hardship argument or a trial. Being clear about what is and is not included, and about any travel or advocacy costs, helps the client say yes on the call.

Explain the process in general terms

Many drivers have never been to court. A short explanation of how a hearing in the magistrates' court usually runs, how long it might take and what they should bring in general terms can reduce their anxiety a great deal. Keep it general until you have taken instructions and reviewed the evidence.

What clients ask

What does driving without insurance cover?
It covers using a vehicle on a road or other public place without a policy that covers that driver, that vehicle and that type of use. It also covers causing or permitting someone else to do so. So an owner who lends their car to someone who is not insured can face the same charge as the driver.
I thought I was insured. Is that a defence?
Usually not on its own, because the offence is one of strict liability. However, a genuine and reasonable belief that you were insured may support an argument for special reasons, which can allow the court not to give points. If you were an employee driving a work vehicle, a specific defence may apply. A solicitor will look at the facts.
Who has to prove I was insured?
In most cases it is for the driver to prove that there was a valid policy covering that use. That is why your policy documents, certificate and schedule are so important. If you say you were insured, gather everything you can and give it to your solicitor as early as possible.
What is the employee defence?
It is a statutory defence for some employees. It can apply where the vehicle did not belong to you and was not hired or lent to you, you were driving it in the course of your employment, and you did not know and had no reason to believe it was uninsured. Each part must be shown, and a solicitor can check whether your situation fits.
Can the vehicle owner be prosecuted too?
Yes. A person who causes or permits someone else to use a vehicle without insurance can be prosecuted, and the penalties are the same as for the driver. This often arises where a car is lent to a friend or family member, or where an employer allows staff to drive a vehicle that is not properly insured.
How long after the incident can I be prosecuted?
Insurance offences have different time limits from many other minor motoring offences. This can allow a prosecution to start some time after the incident, particularly where it took a while for the authorities to obtain the evidence. A solicitor can check whether your case was brought within the time allowed.
Does it matter that I was only driving a short distance?
The offence can be committed over any distance, even moving a car a few metres on a public road. However, in some cases the distance driven and the reason for driving, such as a genuine emergency, may be relevant to whether special reasons exist or to the penalty. A solicitor can explain how courts tend to view the circumstances in your case.
Does my policy let me drive other cars?
Some policies include driving other cars cover, but many do not, and where it is included it is often limited to third party cover for certain drivers. It may not apply to cars you own or that are registered to you. Check your certificate and policy wording. A solicitor can look at the exact terms if you have been charged.
My insurer cancelled my policy without telling me. What then?
Insurers usually have to follow the cancellation terms in the policy, including giving notice. Whether you were insured on the day depends on the dates and what the insurer did. Ask the insurer for copies of any cancellation letters and when they were sent. A solicitor can review these and explain how they affect your case.
Is food delivery covered by my normal insurance?
Usually not. Delivering food or parcels for payment normally needs specific cover, often described as hire and reward or delivery cover. Standard social, domestic and commuting policies will not usually cover it. If you were stopped while working for a delivery platform, a solicitor will check your policy and any cover the platform provides.
I had just bought the car. Was I covered?
Insurance does not usually transfer automatically from one car to another. Some policies give short temporary cover for a newly bought vehicle, but many do not. Whether you were covered depends on your policy and what you told your insurer. A solicitor can look at the documents to see whether a defence or special reasons might apply.
What is the Motor Insurance Database?
It is the central record of insured vehicles in the UK. The police use it, often through number plate recognition cameras, to check whether a vehicle is insured. The database can occasionally be out of date or wrong. If you were insured but the database said otherwise, keep your certificate and contact your insurer to correct it.
What is continuous insurance enforcement?
It is the rule that the registered keeper must keep a vehicle insured unless it has been declared off the road with a SORN. The authorities compare vehicle records with insurance records and send letters to keepers of vehicles that appear uninsured. It is a separate matter from driving without insurance, and usually dealt with through penalties rather than points.
Is a learner driver covered by the family car insurance?
Not automatically. A learner usually needs to be named on the policy or have separate learner driver insurance, and must meet the conditions for supervised driving. If a learner was driving without the right cover, both the learner and the person who allowed them to drive could face charges. A solicitor can look at the policy and the circumstances.
How many points will I get?
If you are convicted and not disqualified, the court must give between 6 and 8 points. The number depends on the circumstances, such as how long the vehicle was uninsured and why. The court can disqualify you instead of giving points. If special reasons are found, the court can decide not to give any points at all.
What are special reasons?
Special reasons are facts connected with the offence that can justify the court not endorsing your licence. In insurance cases a common example is being genuinely misled about your cover by someone you were entitled to rely on, such as your employer. Special reasons must be proved with evidence, usually at a hearing where you give evidence.
Do I have to go to court?
If you receive a Single Justice Procedure notice, you can respond in writing. If you want to argue special reasons or plead not guilty, the case will usually go to a court hearing, and you will normally need to attend to give evidence. Your solicitor will tell you when you need to be there.
Can I be offered a fixed penalty?
In some cases the police offer a fixed penalty with points instead of prosecution. This depends on the circumstances and is at the discretion of the police. Accepting it means taking the points without going to court. If you think you have special reasons or a defence, a solicitor can explain whether going to court might be worth considering.
I am a new driver. Will I lose my licence?
If you are within two years of passing your test, six or more points will lead the DVLA to revoke your licence. As an IN10 carries at least six points, a conviction will usually have that effect unless the court disqualifies you instead or finds special reasons not to endorse. You would then need to retake your theory and practical tests.
Can I appeal?
You can appeal to the Crown Court against conviction or sentence. Time limits are short, usually 21 days from sentence. If you were convicted without knowing about the case, there may be other ways to reopen it. A solicitor can explain your options and the risks involved.
Could I be banned instead of getting points?
Yes. The court can choose to disqualify you instead of endorsing your licence with points. This is more likely where the offence is serious, for example where a vehicle was used without insurance for a long time or there was an accident. Some drivers who would otherwise face a totting up ban or revocation ask the court to consider a short ban instead, and the court decides whether that is appropriate.
The police seized my car. How do I get it back?
You will usually need to show that you are the owner or keeper, show valid insurance for the vehicle and pay the release and storage charges. The police or storage provider can tell you the current charges and deadline. If the vehicle is not claimed in time it may be disposed of, so act quickly.
Can I get the seizure charges back?
Usually only in limited circumstances, such as where the seizure was not lawful. Even if you later show you had a defence to the charge, recovering seizure costs is not straightforward. A solicitor can look at how the seizure happened and explain whether there is any basis for challenging it.
What if I had an accident while uninsured?
The victim may be compensated by the Motor Insurers' Bureau, which may then seek to recover the money from you personally. This is separate from the criminal case. Tell your solicitor about any accident, as it affects how serious the court considers the offence to be and may raise other issues.
Will a conviction affect my future insurance?
Yes. Insurers treat no insurance convictions seriously and premiums often rise substantially for several years. Some insurers may decline to offer cover. You must declare convictions when asked. Specialist brokers may be able to help find cover after a conviction.
