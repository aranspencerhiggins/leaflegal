Motoring enquiries reward firms that are quick, clear and confident about fees. The driver is usually anxious and often has a date in the diary.

Call within the hour if you can

A driver with a court date coming up will not wait long. Calling soon after the lead arrives, at the time they asked for, shows you take their deadline seriously. Set up alerts so the right fee earner or solicitor advocate sees each lead straight away, including late in the day.

Start with the date and the points

The lead tells you the offence, the court date and the client's current points. Opening with those shows that you have read their enquiry. It also lets you deal first with anything urgent, such as a response deadline on a notice or a hearing later that week.

Give a clear fee

Most private motoring clients want a fixed fee for each stage, such as advice and representation at a first hearing, an exceptional hardship argument or a trial. Being clear about what is and is not included, and about any travel or advocacy costs, helps the client say yes on the call.

Explain the process in general terms

Many drivers have never been to court. A short explanation of how a hearing in the magistrates' court usually runs, how long it might take and what they should bring in general terms can reduce their anxiety a great deal. Keep it general until you have taken instructions and reviewed the evidence.

Mention the introduction

The client agreed to be contacted after making an enquiry through Leaflegal. Mention that when you call, and cover any referral arrangement in your client care letter as the SRA Code of Conduct requires.

Tell us what works

Let us know which leads instruct and which do not. If speeding matters with clean licences rarely convert for you but totting up cases do, we can change your matching to send you more of the work you want.

Follow up matters too. Some drivers want to talk to an employer or family before instructing. A short email after the call setting out your fee and the next step, followed by a second call a day or two later, often helps, especially when a hearing is close.

Why current points matter

Current points help you see quickly whether a matter could lead to a totting up disqualification. Under the totting up rules, a driver who reaches 12 or more points within three years will usually face a disqualification unless the court accepts that exceptional hardship would result. A driver with nine points facing a speeding charge is in a very different position from one with a clean licence, and the lead lets you see that before you call.

Points also matter for newer drivers. A driver who reaches six or more points within two years of passing their test will usually have their licence revoked under the new driver rules. A new driver facing even a modest offence may therefore be very anxious, and the lead helps you spot that.

Whether a ban is likely

We ask the client whether they think a ban is likely. This is the client's own view, not an assessment by us, and it may not be accurate. It is still useful, because it tells you what the client is worried about and what they will want to talk about first.

What clients ask

What is the drink driving limit in England and Wales?
The limits are 35 microgrammes of alcohol in 100 millilitres of breath, 80 milligrammes in 100 millilitres of blood and 107 milligrammes in 100 millilitres of urine. The limits are lower in Scotland. There is no safe number of drinks that guarantees you will be under the limit, because the level depends on many things, including your weight, what you have eaten and how quickly your body processes alcohol. A solicitor can explain how the limits apply to your reading.
Can I be charged if I was not actually driving?
You can be charged with being in charge of a vehicle while over the limit, even if you were not driving. This can apply to someone sitting in a parked car with the keys. There is a defence if you can show there was no likelihood of you driving while you were still over the limit. A solicitor can explain how this works on your facts.
What does failing to provide a specimen mean?
It is an offence to fail, without a reasonable excuse, to provide a specimen of breath, blood or urine when properly asked. If you were driving or attempting to drive, it carries an obligatory ban like drink driving itself, and the DVLA treats it seriously. A reasonable excuse is usually medical. A solicitor will look at the circumstances and any medical evidence.
Is drink driving a criminal offence?
Yes. Drink driving is a criminal offence, and a conviction is a criminal conviction as well as an endorsement on your licence. It stays on your driving record for eleven years. It may need to be disclosed to insurers and some employers, and it can be relevant to travel to some countries. How long it must be disclosed in other contexts depends on the rehabilitation rules.
What if I was caught the morning after?
Morning after cases are treated in the same way as any other drink driving case. If the evidential reading is over the limit, the offence is made out, even if you felt sober. The circumstances, such as the time since your last drink, can be relevant to mitigation. A solicitor will explain how the court is likely to see it.
Is it different if I drove a very short distance?
Driving a short distance does not stop it being an offence. In rare cases, a very short distance in circumstances where there was little chance of danger can be raised as a special reason not to impose a ban. Courts look at this strictly and consider things like how far you drove, why, and the road and traffic conditions. A solicitor can say whether it is realistic.
Which reading is used against me?
If you gave breath at the police station, the lower of the two evidential readings is the one used. The roadside screening reading is not usually the one relied on in court. If blood or urine was taken instead, the analysed level from that sample is used. Your solicitor will see the printout or the analyst's certificate as part of the evidence.
Why did the police take blood instead of breath?
The police can ask for blood or urine if the breath machine is unreliable or unavailable, or if there is a medical reason you cannot give breath. In some situations, such as a hospital case, the procedure is different again. The reasons and the steps followed are recorded, and a solicitor will look at them as part of reviewing the case.
Can I get my blood sample tested independently?
When blood is taken, you should be offered part of the sample in a sealed container. You can arrange for it to be analysed by an independent laboratory. This is usually done through a solicitor, because the sample needs to be stored and handled correctly. Whether an independent analysis would be useful is something the solicitor can discuss with you.
Can a breath machine be wrong?
Evidential breath machines must be of an approved type and are checked as part of the procedure, but challenges to the reliability of a reading are sometimes made. These need specific evidence, often expert evidence, and are not straightforward. A solicitor can look at the printout, the procedure and any records to see whether there is anything worth exploring.
Should I have asked for a solicitor at the police station?
You have the right to free legal advice at the police station. The police do not always have to wait for a solicitor before carrying out the breath procedure, because the reading could change with time. If you did not get advice at the station, you can still speak to a solicitor about the case before your first court hearing.
What if I drank after I stopped driving?
The law assumes that the alcohol level at the time of the test reflects the level when you were driving. If you drank after driving, it is for you to prove that the later drink took you over the limit. This usually needs expert evidence and careful calculations. A solicitor will explain what evidence would be needed and how the court looks at this argument.
Will I definitely be banned?
For most people, a conviction for drink driving means an obligatory ban of at least 12 months, or three years if there is a relevant previous conviction within ten years. The court can only decide not to ban if special reasons are found, which is rare. A solicitor will explain what is likely in your case after seeing the evidence.
How long will my ban be?
The length depends mainly on the evidential reading, together with other factors such as the standard of driving and any previous convictions. The Sentencing Council guidelines link higher readings to longer bans. The court also considers personal mitigation. A solicitor can give you a realistic idea of the range once they know the reading and your circumstances.
Could I go to prison for drink driving?
A custodial sentence is possible, but it is usually reserved for the most serious cases, such as very high readings, very bad driving, or repeat offences. Many people receive a fine and a ban. Some receive a community order. The solicitor will look at the guidelines and explain where your case is likely to sit.
Can I reduce my ban with a course?
If you are banned for 12 months or more, the court can offer you a drink drive rehabilitation course. If you complete it by the deadline the court sets, your ban is reduced. You pay for the course yourself. The court decides whether to offer it, and the solicitor can tell you whether it is likely to be offered in your case.
What happens at the first court hearing?
You will usually be asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you that day after hearing the facts and your mitigation. If a report is needed, the case is adjourned. If you plead not guilty, a trial date is set. A solicitor can attend with you and explain each step as it happens.
Does a guilty plea reduce the ban?
An early guilty plea reduces the overall sentence, such as the fine or the length of a community order. It cannot take the ban below the legal minimum. Within the guideline range, a plea may affect the length of the ban the court chooses. The solicitor can explain how credit for a plea is likely to work in your case.
What are special reasons?
Special reasons are circumstances connected to the offence that allow a court not to impose an obligatory ban, or to impose a shorter one. Examples raised in court include a spiked drink, a genuine medical emergency or a very short drive. They are not about personal hardship. They need evidence and a separate hearing, and courts are careful about accepting them.
Can I keep driving before my court date?
In most drink driving cases, yes. The ban normally starts on the day the court imposes it, not on the day you were stopped or charged. If you were bailed with conditions, check whether any of them affect driving. Many people use the time before the hearing to arrange other ways of getting to work and meeting family commitments.
How do I get my licence back?
When the ban ends, you usually need to apply to the DVLA for your licence to be returned. If you are classed as a high risk offender, you will need to pass a medical assessment arranged through the DVLA before the licence is returned. The DVLA sends information about this towards the end of the ban.
What is a high risk offender?
It is a category used by the DVLA for some drink driving offenders. It usually covers people with a reading of two and a half times the limit or more, people convicted of failing to provide a specimen, and people with two drink driving convictions within ten years. They must pass a medical assessment before their licence is returned.
Will my insurance go up after the ban?
Almost certainly. Insurers treat drink driving convictions seriously, and many will ask about them for several years. Some insurers decline cover, and specialist insurers may charge much more. Being honest with insurers when they ask is important, because failing to disclose a conviction can cause problems if you make a claim.
Can I drive abroad while banned in the UK?
A UK driving ban means you hold no valid licence during the ban, and an International Driving Permit relies on a valid licence. Different countries also have their own rules about recognising foreign bans. It is not something to assume. Check with the authorities of the country concerned and speak to a solicitor if you need to know more.
Can I appeal a drink driving ban?
There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court against conviction, sentence or both, within 21 days of sentence. In some cases you can apply for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is decided. Appeals need to have proper grounds. A solicitor can look at what happened and tell you whether an appeal is realistic.
Will my employer find out?
The court does not usually contact your employer. However, if you drive for work, your employer may check your licence or ask you to declare convictions, and some contracts require you to tell them. Certain professions also have reporting duties to a regulator. Your contract, staff handbook or professional body can tell you what applies to you.
