Leaflegal sends motoring offence enquiries from drivers in England and Wales who want to pay a solicitor to advise or represent them. Each enquiry goes to one firm only. It is never sent to several firms at once and it is never resold after delivery.

People facing a motoring charge are usually very motivated to get help. Their job may depend on driving, they may run a business that needs a vehicle, or they may care for someone who relies on them for lifts. Many have a court date or a deadline on a letter, and they want to know quickly where they stand and what representation would cost.

Every client confirms that they are looking for paid representation rather than free advice. That means your first call can focus on the client's matter and your service, not on whether they are willing to pay.

Many drivers also find it hard to work out which kind of lawyer they need. By asking a few simple questions, our form gets them to a firm that handles motoring work, rather than to a general practice that may pass them on.

How the enquiries are generated

Our campaigns run on search, social media and helpful content aimed at drivers who have received a notice of intended prosecution, a court summons, a postal requisition or a letter about their points. Each person answers a short set of questions about the offence, their current points, any court date and where they are. When an enquiry matches your criteria, we check it and send it to you straight away.

Why speed matters so much in motoring work

Motoring clients are often working to a fixed date. A summons may give a hearing a few weeks away, and a response to a single justice procedure notice has its own deadline. Drivers in that position usually instruct the first firm that explains things clearly and gives them a price. Because each Leaflegal enquiry goes to one firm, you have the chance to be that firm without racing several competitors to the phone.

What Leaflegal is not

Leaflegal is not a law firm and does not give legal advice. We do not tell drivers whether they have a defence, whether they are likely to be banned or what to plead. We introduce them to one firm that handles motoring work, and the advice comes from you.

Motoring enquiries come from a broad range of drivers. You choose the offence types you want, so a firm that concentrates on serious charges does not have to take routine speeding matters unless it wants them.

Typical clients

Many motoring clients are professional drivers, such as delivery and HGV drivers, sales staff and tradespeople, for whom a ban could mean losing their job. Others are business owners, carers, parents with school runs or people living in rural areas with little public transport. A good number are new drivers worried about the rules that apply in their first two years on the road.

Where they are in the process

Some drivers enquire as soon as a notice of intended prosecution arrives, before they have responded to it. Others have a court date coming up, have received a single justice procedure notice or have already pleaded and want help with an exceptional hardship argument or mitigation. Each lead says whether there is a court date and what it is, if the client knows it.

What clients want to know

Drivers usually want a clear answer to three questions. Can you help, what will it cost and what happens next. Most also want to understand, in general terms, how the process works in the magistrates' court and what kind of outcomes are possible. A firm that explains this calmly, and gives a fixed fee for the work, tends to win their trust.

Every motoring lead is the full record of what the driver told us. The questions are short, but they cover what your team needs to triage the matter and quote on the first call.

Why current points matter

Current points help you see quickly whether a matter could lead to a totting up disqualification. Under the totting up rules, a driver who reaches 12 or more points within three years will usually face a disqualification unless the court accepts that exceptional hardship would result. A driver with nine points facing a speeding charge is in a very different position from one with a clean licence, and the lead lets you see that before you call.

Points also matter for newer drivers. A driver who reaches six or more points within two years of passing their test will usually have their licence revoked under the new driver rules. A new driver facing even a modest offence may therefore be very anxious, and the lead helps you spot that.

Whether a ban is likely

We ask the client whether they think a ban is likely. This is the client's own view, not an assessment by us, and it may not be accurate. It is still useful, because it tells you what the client is worried about and what they will want to talk about first.

Motoring enquiries reward firms that are quick, clear and confident about fees. The driver is usually anxious and often has a date in the diary.

Call within the hour if you can

A driver with a court date coming up will not wait long. Calling soon after the lead arrives, at the time they asked for, shows you take their deadline seriously. Set up alerts so the right fee earner or solicitor advocate sees each lead straight away, including late in the day.

Start with the date and the points

The lead tells you the offence, the court date and the client's current points. Opening with those shows that you have read their enquiry. It also lets you deal first with anything urgent, such as a response deadline on a notice or a hearing later that week.

Give a clear fee

Most private motoring clients want a fixed fee for each stage, such as advice and representation at a first hearing, an exceptional hardship argument or a trial. Being clear about what is and is not included, and about any travel or advocacy costs, helps the client say yes on the call.

Explain the process in general terms

Many drivers have never been to court. A short explanation of how a hearing in the magistrates' court usually runs, how long it might take and what they should bring in general terms can reduce their anxiety a great deal. Keep it general until you have taken instructions and reviewed the evidence.

Mention the introduction

The client agreed to be contacted after making an enquiry through Leaflegal. Mention that when you call, and cover any referral arrangement in your client care letter as the SRA Code of Conduct requires.

Tell us what works

Let us know which leads instruct and which do not. If speeding matters with clean licences rarely convert for you but totting up cases do, we can change your matching to send you more of the work you want.

Follow up matters too. Some drivers want to talk to an employer or family before instructing. A short email after the call setting out your fee and the next step, followed by a second call a day or two later, often helps, especially when a hearing is close.

What clients ask

What is a notice of intended prosecution?
It is a formal warning that you may be prosecuted for an offence such as speeding. It is usually sent to the registered keeper within 14 days of the offence, unless you were warned at the time or there was an accident. It normally comes with a request to name the driver. A solicitor can check whether it was served correctly.
How long do I have to name the driver?
The request usually gives 28 days to reply. Failing to name the driver without a good reason is a separate offence that carries six points, more than a typical speeding offence. If you are not sure who was driving, or the car is used by several people, it is worth getting advice before the deadline passes.
What if the notice arrived after 14 days?
The 14 days are counted from the offence to the date the notice is treated as served on the registered keeper. If the registered keeper is a company or hire firm, later notices to the named driver can lawfully arrive after 14 days. There are also exceptions, such as accidents. A solicitor can look at the dates and explain whether they matter.
What if I was not the registered keeper?
The notice goes to the registered keeper shown on DVLA records. If you sold the car, moved house without updating your details, or drive a company or hire vehicle, the paperwork can take an unusual route. Keep everything you receive and note the dates. A solicitor can explain how the rules apply to your situation.
What if I cannot remember who was driving?
The law expects the keeper to use reasonable diligence to find out who was driving. If, after genuine efforts, you still cannot say, there may be a defence to the offence of failing to name the driver. It is narrow and depends on the steps taken. A solicitor can explain what is expected and what evidence may be needed.
Can I ask to see the photo?
Some police forces will provide a photograph if you ask, often to help identify the driver. They are not always required to, and many camera photos are taken from behind. Asking for a photo does not usually extend the deadline to name the driver, so it is important to keep track of the date.
What if the car is a company car?
Where a company is the registered keeper, the company must name the driver. The notice to the driver then follows. Companies that do not keep proper records of who uses their vehicles can find it hard to respond, and the company itself can be prosecuted for failing to name the driver. Many fleet managers ask a solicitor to review their process.
How many points will I get for speeding?
Speeding carries between three and six points. A fixed penalty is normally three points. If the case goes to court, the number of points depends on how far over the limit you were, along with other factors, using the Sentencing Council guideline. For higher speeds the court can impose a short ban instead of points.
Can I be banned for speeding?
Yes, in two main ways. The court can impose a discretionary ban for the speeding offence itself, usually where the speed was well above the limit. Or the points for the offence can take you to 12 or more within three years, which normally means a totting up ban of at least six months.
Who decides whether I get a speed awareness course?
The police decide, following national guidance. A course is usually offered where the speed was not much above the limit and you have not done a similar course recently. You cannot insist on one, and it is not available once a case has gone to court. If you are offered a course, you pay the provider directly.
Does a speed awareness course go on my record?
Completing a course means no points and no conviction. The police keep a record that you attended, which is used to decide whether you can be offered another course. Some insurers ask whether you have attended a course, so it is worth answering their questions accurately.
How is the fine worked out?
If the case goes to court, the fine is based on the seriousness of the offence and your weekly income, following the Sentencing Council guideline. You will be asked for details of your finances. If you do not provide them, the court can make assumptions about your income. The court also adds a victim surcharge and usually prosecution costs.
What happens if I am a new driver?
If you passed your test within the last two years and reach six or more points, the DVLA will usually revoke your licence. Two speeding offences can be enough. You would then need to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again. The court cannot prevent this if the points are imposed.
Will speeding affect my insurance?
A speeding conviction or points usually needs to be declared to insurers when they ask, and it may increase premiums, particularly if there is more than one. A speed awareness course is not a conviction, but some insurers ask about courses too. Answering insurers' questions accurately protects your cover if you need to make a claim.
How long do speeding points last?
Speeding points stay on your driving record for four years from the date of the offence. For totting up purposes, the court looks at offences committed within three years of each other. So points can still show on your record for a while after they stop counting towards a totting up ban. You can check your record on GOV.UK.
What is a Single Justice Procedure notice?
It is a way of dealing with some offences on paper. A single magistrate decides the case using the written evidence and your reply, without a hearing in open court. You have a deadline to respond and can plead guilty with mitigation, plead not guilty or ask for a hearing. If a ban is being considered, you will usually be given the chance to attend.
Do I have to go to court for speeding?
Not always. Many cases are dealt with by fixed penalty or by post under the Single Justice Procedure. You may need to attend if you plead not guilty, if a ban is being considered, or if the court has told you to attend. A solicitor can tell you whether you need to be there and can represent you.
What happens if I plead not guilty?
The case is listed for trial at a magistrates' court. The prosecution must prove that you were driving and that you were over the limit, usually with device evidence and officer statements. You can give evidence and call witnesses. A trial takes more time and preparation, so a solicitor will usually review the evidence carefully before a plea is entered.
Can I be convicted on an officer's opinion alone?
No. The law says a person cannot be convicted of speeding solely on the opinion of one witness about their speed. There is usually evidence from a camera or device, or more than one source. A solicitor can look at the evidence served and explain whether it meets the requirements.
What are special reasons in a speeding case?
Special reasons are circumstances connected to the offence that allow the court not to add points or impose a ban. A genuine medical emergency is one example sometimes raised. The court looks at these strictly and needs supporting evidence. They are not about personal hardship. A solicitor can explain whether such an argument might apply.
Can I keep driving until the court decides?
In most cases, yes. A ban starts from the day the court imposes it. The court can sometimes impose an interim ban if a case is adjourned before sentence. Until then you can usually drive, as long as your licence is otherwise valid and you remain insured.
What if I am close to 12 points?
If the points for the new speeding offence would take you to 12 or more within three years, the case will usually go to court and a totting up ban of at least six months is likely. The court can decide not to ban, or to ban for a shorter time, if it accepts that a ban would cause exceptional hardship. A solicitor can explain this argument.
Can speed cameras be challenged?
Speed devices used for enforcement must be of an approved type and used in line with the manufacturer's instructions and police guidance. Challenges to device evidence are possible but need a proper basis and often expert evidence. A solicitor will look at what has been served and explain whether there is anything genuinely worth exploring.
Do speed limit signs have to be in place?
Speed limits generally need to be signed in line with the regulations, although some limits, such as the usual limit on roads with street lights, do not need repeater signs. Where signage is relevant, a solicitor can look at the location and the rules that apply. It is a technical area and depends on the road.
Is dashcam footage useful?
It can be, depending on what it shows. Footage may help establish the conditions, the speed of other traffic, or what was happening at the time. Keep a copy safe and do not edit it. Tell the solicitor that you have it so they can decide whether it is relevant to the evidence or to mitigation.
What is an average speed camera?
Average speed cameras record a vehicle at two or more points and work out the average speed between them. They are common on motorways, at roadworks and on some long stretches of road. The evidence works differently from a single spot reading, and the limit that applies across the section can matter. A solicitor can look at how the reading was produced.
Will I see the evidence before I plead?
If the case goes to court, the prosecution must provide the evidence it relies on, such as the device reading, photographs and officer statements. Single Justice Procedure notices include a summary of the evidence. If you plead not guilty, further material may be served before trial. A solicitor can review it and explain what it shows.
