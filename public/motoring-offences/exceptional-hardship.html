About exceptional hardship

Exceptional hardship is an argument made to the court that a totting up ban should not be imposed, or should be shorter, because of the effect on you and others. It needs careful preparation and evidence.

Exceptional hardship explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Exceptional hardship is an argument a driver can put to a magistrates' court when they are facing a totting up ban for reaching 12 or more penalty points. The driver asks the court not to impose the ban, or to impose a shorter one than the law would otherwise require, because a ban would cause hardship that is exceptional rather than ordinary.

Almost everyone who loses their licence suffers some hardship. They may struggle to get to work, rely on lifts or spend more on transport. The law accepts that this is a normal consequence of a ban. To succeed, the hardship has to go beyond what most people would face. Courts often pay particular attention to the effect on other people, such as family members, employees, patients or customers, who have done nothing wrong but would suffer because of the ban.

It is for the driver to prove exceptional hardship, on the balance of probabilities. That means the court must be satisfied that it is more likely than not. The argument is usually made by the driver giving evidence on oath, supported by documents and sometimes by witnesses. The prosecutor can ask questions, and the court decides whether the evidence is convincing.

Because the decision depends on how the court sees the evidence, preparation matters a great deal. Two people with similar circumstances can have different outcomes depending on how clearly the facts are set out and how well they are supported. That is one reason many drivers ask a specialist solicitor to prepare and present the argument.

When it applies

Exceptional hardship is only an answer to a totting up ban. It does not apply to an obligatory ban for an offence such as drink driving, where the only route to avoiding a ban is special reasons connected to the offence itself. It also cannot stop the DVLA revoking a new driver's licence after six points in the first two years. A solicitor can confirm which rules apply to you.

Leaflegal does not give legal advice. We introduce you to a regulated motoring solicitor who can look at your circumstances and explain whether an exceptional hardship argument might be open to you.

There is no fixed list of what counts as exceptional. Each case turns on its own facts, and different benches can take different views. However, some themes come up again and again in these hearings.

Common situations

  • A sole trader whose business would close, affecting staff and customers
  • A driver who is the only carer for a disabled or elderly relative
  • A health or social care worker who visits patients in their homes
  • A parent in a remote area where there is no public transport to school
  • A driver whose employer has confirmed in writing that the role requires a licence and cannot be adjusted

These are examples of situations that are often raised. They are not a list of arguments that will succeed. The court looks at the whole picture in each case.

Effect on other people

Hardship to innocent third parties tends to carry more weight than hardship to the driver alone. Examples include a disabled relative who relies on the driver for medical appointments, staff whose jobs depend on a small business continuing to run, or patients who rely on a visiting health professional.

Loss of employment

Losing a job is serious, but courts do not always regard it as exceptional on its own, because it is a common consequence of a ban. It may carry more weight where there is clear evidence the job would be lost, where finding other work would be particularly difficult, and where the loss would have knock on effects such as losing a home or being unable to support dependants.

Alternatives to driving

The court will usually ask whether there are other ways to manage. Could someone else drive? Is there public transport? Could duties be changed for the period of the ban? Could a taxi or a paid driver be used, and is that affordable? A strong argument usually deals with these questions directly, with evidence.

Things the court cannot consider

  • That the offence which took you to 12 points was minor
  • Hardship that is real but not exceptional
  • Circumstances the court has already accepted as a reason not to ban you within the previous three years

Honesty

The court expects evidence to be accurate and complete. Overstating the effect of a ban, or presenting documents that are not genuine, can seriously damage the argument and can lead to criminal proceedings in its own right. A solicitor will help you present your genuine circumstances clearly and fairly.

An exceptional hardship argument is only as strong as the evidence behind it. Courts are used to hearing these applications and tend to expect more than the driver's own word.

Your own evidence

The driver usually gives evidence from the witness box. You will explain your circumstances, how you use your car, what would happen if you were banned and why other options would not work. Your solicitor will usually prepare a statement with you beforehand so that the key points are clear.

Witnesses

Witnesses can be powerful, especially people who would be directly affected. An employer might explain what would happen to your role. A family member might explain their care needs. Witnesses usually need to attend court in person to give evidence and answer questions.

Documents

Documents help the court see that what you say is accurate. The right documents depend on the case.

  • A letter from your employer about your role and what would happen during a ban
  • Your employment contract, if it requires a licence
  • Medical letters about a relative you care for, or about your own health
  • Evidence of public transport times and costs from your home to work
  • Business accounts or contracts showing how the business depends on you
  • Evidence of your income and outgoings, such as bank statements or mortgage details

Timing

Gathering evidence takes time. Employers need to write letters, doctors need to provide reports and witnesses need to be available on the hearing date. Starting early gives the best chance of having everything in place. If the evidence is not ready, the court may agree to adjourn, but it does not have to.

Keeping it focused

More evidence is not always better. A small number of clear, relevant documents and well chosen witnesses usually helps the court more than a large pile of paper. Your solicitor can help you decide what is important and what can be left out.

Exceptional hardship is usually argued at a magistrates' court hearing after you have pleaded guilty to, or been convicted of, the offence that takes you to 12 points.

Attending court can be stressful, especially for people who have never been before. Knowing the order of events helps. Your solicitor will usually meet you at court before the hearing to go through the plan.

Before the hearing

Your solicitor will usually tell the court and the prosecution that an exceptional hardship argument will be made, so that enough time is set aside. They will prepare your statement, organise witnesses and put the documents into a bundle for the court.

Giving evidence

You will take an oath or affirm and then answer questions from your own solicitor or barrister. This is your chance to explain the hardship in your own words. The prosecutor can then ask you questions, which can include challenging parts of your account. The magistrates or district judge may ask questions too.

Witnesses and submissions

Any witnesses are called and go through the same process. Your solicitor or barrister then makes submissions, which means summarising the evidence and explaining why it shows exceptional hardship.

The decision

The court decides whether exceptional hardship has been shown. If it has, the court can decide not to ban you at all, or impose a ban shorter than the usual minimum. If it has not, the court imposes the totting up ban in the usual way. The court also deals with the fine for the offence.

If you started on the Single Justice Procedure

Exceptional hardship cannot realistically be argued on paper, because the court needs to hear evidence. If your case started with a Single Justice Procedure notice, you will usually need the case listed for a hearing in open court so that the argument can be made.

A successful argument means you keep your licence, or have a shorter ban than the minimum. It does not wipe the slate clean.

The points remain

The points stay on your record. You remain at 12 or more until the earlier offences fall outside the relevant period. Any further endorsable offence during that time is likely to bring you straight back before the court facing another totting up ban.

The three year rule

If you face another totting up ban within three years, the court cannot accept the same circumstances again as a reason not to ban you. You would need to show different exceptional hardship. The court will ask what was argued before, so keeping a record of the earlier hearing helps.

If it does not succeed

If the court is not persuaded, it imposes the ban. There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court within 21 days, where the argument can be heard again. An appeal needs proper grounds, and the Crown Court will look at all the evidence afresh. A solicitor can explain whether an appeal might be worth considering.

Planning ahead

Whatever the outcome, many people use the experience to think about how they manage driving in future, such as sharing driving at work or planning journeys differently. The solicitor can explain how long the points will stay relevant.

Exceptional hardship hearings involve more preparation than a straightforward guilty plea, and the fee usually reflects that. Legal aid is rarely available, so most people pay privately.

How firms usually charge

Many motoring firms offer a fixed fee for an exceptional hardship hearing, which often includes taking your instructions, preparing statements, reviewing documents and attending court. Some firms instruct a barrister for the hearing. Ask whether the barrister's fee is included. A trial on the underlying offence, if there is one, is usually charged separately.

Other costs

It is also worth asking whether the fee changes if the hearing is adjourned, or if the court needs a second day. Clear terms at the start avoid surprises later.

If you are convicted of the underlying offence, the court will order a fine, a victim surcharge and prosecution costs, whether or not you are banned. Witnesses may need time off work to attend. Medical or other reports may have a fee.

Weighing it up

For many people, the cost of preparing an argument properly is weighed against the cost of a ban to them, their family or their business. A solicitor can give you a realistic view of the strength of your circumstances before you commit. They cannot promise a result.

Exceptional hardship questions and answers

What counts as exceptional?
There is no fixed list. Courts look at the impact on others in particular.
Do I have to give evidence?
Usually, yes. A solicitor will prepare you.
Does it always work?
No. It depends on your circumstances and how well the argument is presented.
Which areas do you cover?
Motoring offences dealt with in the courts of England and Wales.
Is there legal aid for motoring offences?
Usually not, unless you are at risk of prison. We only introduce people looking for paid legal help.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm aims to contact you quickly, especially if you have a court date coming up. Please give a mobile number you can answer.
What is exceptional hardship?
It is an argument a driver can make to a court when facing a totting up ban for reaching 12 or more points. The driver asks the court not to ban them, or to impose a shorter ban, because a ban would cause hardship beyond the normal consequences of losing a licence. The driver must prove it with evidence.
What is the difference between hardship and exceptional hardship?
Most people who are banned suffer some hardship, such as difficulty getting to work or higher travel costs. The law treats that as normal. Exceptional hardship goes further. Courts often look for serious consequences, particularly for other people who rely on the driver, and for evidence that there is no realistic way to manage without driving.
Can exceptional hardship stop a drink driving ban?
No. Exceptional hardship only applies to totting up bans. A drink driving ban is obligatory, and the court can only decide not to ban, or to shorten the ban, if it finds special reasons connected to the offence itself, such as a genuine emergency. A solicitor can explain the difference and whether either applies.
Does it help new drivers?
Not with revocation. If you passed your test within the last two years and reach six points, the DVLA revokes your licence automatically, and an exceptional hardship argument cannot stop that. Exceptional hardship can still be relevant if a new driver also faces a totting up ban, which is less common. A solicitor can explain how the rules interact.
Who has to prove exceptional hardship?
The driver does. The court must be satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that a ban would cause exceptional hardship. That means it is more likely than not. The court will usually expect evidence from the driver and supporting documents, and sometimes evidence from other people who would be affected.
Does it always work?
No. Some arguments succeed and some do not. The outcome depends on the circumstances, the quality of the evidence and how the court sees the case. A solicitor can give a realistic view of the strengths and weaknesses of your circumstances, but no one can guarantee a result.
Can a business owner argue exceptional hardship?
Yes. Business owners often argue that a ban would affect the business and the people who work for it. The court will want evidence, such as accounts, contracts and details of staff, and will look at whether someone else could drive or whether the business could adapt for the period of the ban. The effect on employees can carry particular weight.
Can I argue it if I only have a short time before court?
It may still be possible, but less time means less opportunity to gather documents and arrange witnesses. If the evidence is not ready, a solicitor may ask the court to adjourn, which the court can agree to or refuse. Mentioning your hearing date as soon as you get in touch helps the firm judge what can realistically be done.
What evidence will I need?
It depends on your circumstances. Common examples include a letter from your employer, your employment contract, medical letters about a relative you care for, evidence of public transport options and costs, business records and evidence of your finances. Your solicitor will tell you which documents matter most in your case and how they should be presented.
Will I have to give evidence myself?
Usually yes. The court normally expects to hear from the driver in person, under oath or affirmation. You will explain your circumstances and be asked questions by your own representative, the prosecutor and sometimes the court. Your solicitor will help you prepare so that you know what to expect.
Can other people give evidence for me?
Yes. Witnesses who would be affected by a ban, such as an employer, a business partner or a family member, can give evidence. They usually need to attend court in person and can be asked questions by the prosecutor. Letters alone may carry less weight than a witness the court can hear from directly.
Is losing my job enough?
It can be part of an argument, but courts do not always treat job loss as exceptional on its own, because it is a common consequence of a ban. It may carry more weight with clear evidence the job would be lost, and where the loss would have serious effects on others, such as dependants. A solicitor can explain how courts tend to view this.
What if my employer could change my duties?
The court is likely to ask whether your employer could adjust your role during the ban, or whether a colleague could drive. If that is possible, it may weaken the argument. If it is not, evidence from your employer explaining why can help. Being open about alternatives usually helps the court trust the rest of your evidence.
Do I need medical evidence?
If your argument involves caring for someone with health needs, or your own health, medical evidence is often very important. A letter from a doctor or specialist explaining the condition and the need for transport can support what you say. Medical letters can take time, so it helps to ask for them early.
What if I exaggerate the hardship?
Overstating your circumstances is likely to backfire. The prosecutor may test your account with questions, and inconsistencies can damage the whole argument. Giving untrue evidence or relying on false documents is a serious matter that can lead to criminal proceedings. A solicitor will help you present your genuine situation clearly.
Should I write a statement?
Your solicitor will usually prepare a written statement with you, setting out your circumstances in a clear order. It helps you and the court focus on the important points. You will normally still give evidence in person, but a well prepared statement makes it easier to cover everything and to answer questions consistently.
Do letters need to be on headed paper?
Letters from employers, doctors and organisations usually carry more weight if they are on headed paper, signed and dated, and give the writer's role and contact details. That lets the court and prosecutor see who wrote them and check them if needed. Your solicitor can explain what each letter should cover so that it is useful to the court.
When is exceptional hardship argued?
It is argued after you have pleaded guilty to, or been convicted of, the offence that takes you to 12 points, and before the court decides on the ban. It usually needs a dedicated hearing so that there is enough time for evidence. Your solicitor will tell the court in advance that the argument is being made.
Can I argue it on a Single Justice Procedure form?
Not realistically. The court needs to hear evidence and the prosecutor needs the chance to ask questions. If your case started with a Single Justice Procedure notice, you will usually need it listed for a hearing in open court. A solicitor can explain how to request this.
How long does the hearing take?
It varies with the number of witnesses and how much evidence there is. Many hearings take an hour or two of court time, but you may wait for your case to be called. Your solicitor will give you an idea of how long to set aside and will tell your witnesses when to arrive.
Can the prosecution challenge my evidence?
Yes. The prosecutor can ask you and your witnesses questions to test what you say, such as whether there are alternatives to driving or whether the consequences are as serious as claimed. Your solicitor will help you prepare for the kinds of questions that tend to be asked, without telling you what to say.
What can the court decide?
If the court accepts exceptional hardship, it can decide not to ban you at all, or impose a ban shorter than the usual minimum. If it does not accept it, the court imposes the totting up ban in the usual way. Either way, the court also deals with the fine for the offence.
Can I have a barrister?
Yes. Some solicitors present the argument themselves and others instruct a barrister with experience of these hearings. Both are regulated. If a barrister is used, ask whether their fee is included in the price you have been given, and whether you will have a chance to meet them before the hearing.
What should I wear and how should I behave?
There is no dress code, but most people wear smart, clean clothes, as they would for an important meeting. Arrive early, switch off your phone and speak clearly when answering questions. If you do not understand a question, it is fine to say so. Your solicitor will explain what to expect on the day.
Do I keep my points if I succeed?
Yes. The points stay on your record, so you remain at 12 or more until the earlier offences fall outside the relevant period. Any further endorsable offence during that time is likely to bring you back to court facing another totting up ban, and the same reasons cannot be used again within three years.
Can I use the same reasons again?
Not within three years. If the court has already accepted certain circumstances as a reason not to ban you within the previous three years, it cannot accept them again. You would need to show different exceptional hardship. The court will ask about any earlier argument, so keeping a note of it helps.
Can I appeal if the court does not accept it?
There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court, normally within 21 days of sentence. The Crown Court hears the argument again. In some cases you can ask for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is decided. A solicitor can explain whether an appeal might be worth considering.
Will the court record why it did not ban me?
The court records the reasons it accepted as amounting to exceptional hardship. This matters because the same reasons cannot be used again within three years. If you face another totting up ban later, the court will be able to see what was relied on before.
Will I still get a fine?
Yes, usually. Exceptional hardship is about whether you are banned, not about the sentence for the offence that took you to 12 points. The court will still impose a fine for that offence, along with a victim surcharge and usually prosecution costs. The fine is normally based on your income.
How much does an exceptional hardship hearing cost?
Many firms offer a fixed fee that covers preparation and attending the hearing, and it is usually higher than a simple guilty plea fee because of the work involved. Barrister fees, reports and travel may be extra. Ask the firm what is included and get the fee confirmed in writing before any work begins.

How early should I get in touch?
Exceptional hardship arguments depend on evidence, and evidence takes time to gather. Employers, doctors and witnesses all need notice. Getting in touch with a solicitor as soon as you know you may reach 12 points gives the most time to prepare. If your hearing is soon, mention the date in your enquiry.
What should I have ready when the solicitor calls?
Have your driving record, the papers for the new offence and your court date. Make a list of the people who would be affected by a ban and how, and note whether anyone could drive for you or whether public transport would work. The solicitor will tell you which documents would be most useful.
Is legal aid available?
Legal aid is rarely available for exceptional hardship hearings, because it usually depends on a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately. If your circumstances mean legal aid might be possible, the solicitor can explain how to apply.
What does careless driving actually mean?
It means driving that falls below the standard expected of a competent and careful driver. The court does not need to find that you were reckless or that you knew you were driving badly. It looks at what a careful driver would have done in the same situation and compares that with what you did. It is a summary offence, which means it is dealt with in the magistrates' court.
Is inconsiderate driving the same offence?
It is part of the same section of the law and carries the same penalties. Inconsiderate driving is where your driving inconvenienced other people, such as pedestrians, cyclists or other drivers. Typical examples include driving through a puddle and soaking people at a bus stop, or tailgating to force another driver to move over. The prosecution has to show that someone was actually inconvenienced.
Can I be charged if nobody was hurt?
Yes. Careless driving does not require anyone to be injured or any damage to be caused. A police officer who sees poor driving can report the driver even where nothing happened as a result. That said, the harm caused or risked is relevant to how serious the court considers the offence to be, which affects the penalty.
The other driver was also at fault. Does that help me?
It may be relevant. The question for the court is whether your driving fell below the standard, not whether someone else was more to blame. However, the other driver's actions can explain why an incident happened and may show that your driving was reasonable in the circumstances. A solicitor will look at the evidence of both drivers' actions.
What if I was charged with dangerous driving instead?
Dangerous driving is a more serious offence with a compulsory ban. If you have been charged with it, the court can convict you of careless driving instead if it decides the driving was poor but not far below the standard. A solicitor dealing with a dangerous driving charge will usually consider whether this is a realistic outcome.
How long do the police have to bring a case?
There are time limits for starting a prosecution for careless driving, and there are rules about warning the driver within 14 days unless an accident happened that the driver knew about. Whether these rules were followed depends on the facts. If you think the case has been brought late or you were never warned, ask a solicitor to check.
What is a notice of intended prosecution?
It is a formal warning that the police are considering prosecuting a driver for certain offences, including careless driving. It is usually sent to the registered keeper within 14 days of the incident, or given verbally at the scene. Where there was an accident that the driver was aware of, no notice is usually needed. The notice is not a charge, and receiving one does not mean a case will definitely follow.
Can the police offer a course instead of court?
Sometimes. For less serious careless driving, the police may offer a driver awareness course or a fixed penalty with points. These are offered at the discretion of the police and depend on the circumstances, your history and local policy. There is no right to be offered one. If you are offered a course, the paperwork will explain the conditions and the deadline for accepting it.
