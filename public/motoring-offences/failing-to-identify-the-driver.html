About failing to identify the driver

When a vehicle is caught on camera, the registered keeper must say who was driving. Failing to do so is an offence carrying six points, more than many speeding offences.

Section 172 in brief

A section 172 notice asks the registered keeper to name the driver. Failing to identify the driver carries 6 points and a fine, often more than the original offence.

There are defences where the keeper used reasonable diligence and still could not identify the driver. A motoring solicitor can advise.

Failing to identify the driver explained

Pick a topic to read more.

When a vehicle is caught by a speed camera, a red light camera or a police officer and the driver was not stopped at the time, the police need to find out who was driving. They do this by sending a request to the registered keeper of the vehicle. The law that requires the keeper to answer is section 172 of the Road Traffic Act 1988, which is why these cases are often called section 172 cases.

Failing to give the information asked for is a criminal offence in its own right. It carries six penalty points and a fine. That is more points than many speeding offences, so a driver who fails to respond to a notice about a minor speeding matter can end up in a worse position than if they had dealt with the speeding itself.

Most section 172 cases are not about people trying to hide anything. Common situations include a notice going to an old address, post going missing, a car shared by several family members, a company vehicle used by several employees, a car that had just been sold, or a keeper who was in hospital or abroad when the notice arrived.

Because the offence is about responding to a legal requirement, the details matter. When the notice was sent, where it was sent, what it asked for, what you did and when you did it can all affect whether an offence was committed and whether there is a defence.

Typical situations

  • A court letter arrives about a notice you never saw
  • Several people insured on the car and nobody is sure who was driving
  • A pool or fleet vehicle used by different staff
  • A vehicle sold, scrapped or stolen around the date of the offence
  • A notice returned late because of illness, travel or a bereavement
  • A form returned but not completed or signed in the way required
  • A notice sent to a company where no one took responsibility for answering

Understanding what a section 172 notice is and what it asks for can help you see why these cases arise and why they are taken seriously.

What the notice is

The notice is a formal request, made by or on behalf of a chief officer of police, asking the keeper to give the name and address of the person driving at a particular time and place. It is often sent together with a notice of intended prosecution for the original offence, such as speeding.

Who has to answer

The registered keeper must give the information requested. Other people can also be required to give any information in their power that may lead to the driver being identified. This means a person named as the driver, or someone who lent the car to a friend, may also receive a request.

The time limit

The information must normally be provided within 28 days of the notice being served. The notice itself will say how to respond, whether by post or online, and what details are needed. If the time passes without a proper response, the offence may already have been committed.

What a proper answer looks like

The request is for the identity of the driver. Naming the driver correctly and in the way the notice asks is what the law requires. An answer that does not identify anyone, or that lists several possible drivers, will usually not meet the requirement unless there is a recognised defence.

Keeping your address up to date

Notices are sent to the address on the vehicle registration record held by the DVLA. If that address is out of date, notices may never reach you, and failing to tell the DVLA about a change of address is itself an offence. Keeping your V5C details accurate helps avoid many of the problems that lead to section 172 cases.

Why the law works this way

Camera enforcement only works if the police can find out who was driving. The section 172 duty puts the responsibility on the keeper, who is best placed to know who uses the vehicle. That is why the penalty is set high enough that ignoring a notice is never an easier route than dealing with it properly.

There are recognised defences to failing to identify the driver. Whether any of them apply depends on the facts, and only a solicitor who has looked at your case can say. This is a general summary of the areas a solicitor will usually consider.

A defence usually has to be supported by evidence. Courts are used to hearing that a keeper could not remember who was driving, so a defence that is backed up by documents, records and a clear account of what was done tends to be taken more seriously than a general explanation given for the first time at court.

Reasonable diligence

A keeper has a defence if they can show that they did not know who was driving and could not have found out with reasonable diligence. Courts expect real effort. That usually means checking diaries, work rotas, fuel receipts, phone records and asking everyone who had access to the vehicle, and being able to show the court what was done.

The notice was not received

The prosecution relies on the notice having been properly served. Where a notice was posted to the right address, the law usually presumes it arrived, but that presumption can sometimes be challenged with evidence. Whether this is possible depends on the circumstances, such as how the notice was sent and what evidence you have of not receiving it.

It was not reasonably practicable to respond in time

If it was not reasonably practicable to give the information within 28 days, and it was given as soon as it was reasonably practicable, that can be a defence. Examples might include a serious illness or a period abroad. Evidence of the circumstances is important.

Problems with the paperwork

Sometimes the notice was sent to the wrong person, did not contain the necessary information, or the response was actually sent but not processed. A solicitor will check the paperwork carefully, including the dates and any proof of posting or online submission.

What is not a defence

Not wanting to name a family member or colleague is not a defence. Nor is believing the original offence was not committed. Naming someone you know was not the driver, or arranging for someone else to take the points, is a far more serious matter that can lead to a charge of perverting the course of justice and a prison sentence. A solicitor can explain how to deal properly with a disputed original offence.

Businesses receive a large share of section 172 notices because company cars, vans and pool vehicles are often driven by several people. The law applies to companies in much the same way as it does to individuals.

The company's duty

Where a company is the registered keeper, it must identify the driver in response to a notice. Someone in the business needs to take responsibility for handling notices quickly and accurately. Notices that sit unopened in a post room or reach a former employee's desk are a common cause of prosecutions.

Fines rather than points

A company cannot receive penalty points, but it can be convicted and fined. In some circumstances, a director or manager can also be personally liable if the offence happened with their consent or connivance, or because of their neglect.

Keeping records

The reasonable diligence defence is much harder for a business to rely on if it does not keep records of who uses each vehicle. Vehicle logs, key sign out sheets, telematics data and fuel card records all help a company find out who was driving at a given time.

Leased and hire vehicles

Lease and hire companies usually pass liability on to the hirer or lessee by sending details of the hire agreement to the police. The hirer then receives the notice and must answer it. Businesses using leased vehicles should make sure the address held by the lease company is correct.

Getting help as a business

A solicitor can help a company respond to a notice it is unsure about, defend a prosecution and review its procedures. Some firms offer ongoing help to fleet operators handling a steady flow of notices.

Section 172 offences are summary offences, which means they are dealt with in the magistrates' court. The steps below give a general picture. A solicitor will explain what applies in your case.

1. The Single Justice Procedure notice

Many section 172 cases begin with a Single Justice Procedure notice. This explains the allegation and asks you to respond within 21 days by pleading guilty or not guilty. If you plead guilty, a single magistrate deals with the case on the papers, without a hearing. If you do not respond, the case may be decided in your absence.

2. Pleading not guilty

If you plead not guilty, the case is moved to a magistrates' court hearing. The court will set a trial date and ask what evidence and witnesses will be needed. A summons or postal requisition can also be used instead of the Single Justice Procedure.

3. Trial

At trial the prosecution must prove that a valid notice was served on you and that you did not give the information required. If you are relying on a defence, you will usually need to provide evidence to support it. Magistrates or a district judge decide the case.

4. Sentence

If convicted, you will receive six penalty points unless the court finds special reasons not to endorse, and a fine. The court can disqualify you, although a ban for this offence alone is not common. If the points take you to 12 or more within three years, a totting up ban will usually follow.

5. Cases decided without you

If you were convicted without knowing about the case, for example because the papers went to an old address, there may be a way to reopen it. Time limits apply, so speak to a solicitor as soon as you find out.

Talking to the prosecution

Before a trial, it is sometimes possible for a solicitor to raise points with the prosecution, such as evidence that a response was sent or that a notice was never properly served. In some cases this leads the prosecution to review whether to continue. There is no guarantee of this, and the court timetable still has to be followed.

Most section 172 cases are relatively short, but costs can still add up, especially if a trial is needed.

Court penalties

If you are convicted, the court will usually impose a fine based on your income, along with prosecution costs and a victim surcharge. The amounts vary and change over time. A company will usually face a fine as well, without points.

Solicitor fees

Many motoring firms offer fixed fees for advice on responding to a notice, representing you on a guilty plea, arguing special reasons or defending a trial. Advice at the early stage, before the deadline passes, is usually the least expensive. Ask what is included and whether there might be extra costs such as a barrister or travel.

Legal aid

Legal aid for section 172 cases is rarely available because the offence does not carry a prison sentence. Most clients pay privately. Check whether you have legal expenses cover with your motor insurance or another policy.

Recovering costs

If you pay privately and are found not guilty, you may be able to recover part of your legal costs from public funds, though usually not all of them.

Thinking about the whole cost

When weighing up whether to defend a case, people often think about more than the legal fee. Six points can affect insurance premiums for several years, can bring a driver closer to a totting up ban and can matter to employers who need staff to drive. A solicitor can explain the likely consequences so that you can make an informed decision about how to proceed.

What is a section 172 notice?
It is a formal request from the police asking the registered keeper of a vehicle to say who was driving at a particular time and place. It is named after section 172 of the Road Traffic Act 1988. It often arrives with a notice of intended prosecution for an offence such as speeding. You are legally required to answer it.
How long do I have to respond?
The information must normally be given within 28 days of the notice being served. The notice will explain how to respond and what details are needed. If the deadline has passed or is close, a solicitor can explain what this means for you. Late responses are sometimes accepted, but there is no guarantee.
Can I respond online?
Many police forces let you respond online, and the notice will usually give the web address and a reference number. Follow the instructions carefully and keep a copy or screenshot of your confirmation. If you respond by post, it is sensible to keep a copy of the form and proof of posting.
What if the car is shared by several people?
Sharing a car does not remove the duty to name the driver. The keeper is expected to find out who was driving, for example by checking diaries, fuel receipts and asking everyone who used the car. If, after real effort, you cannot find out, a solicitor can explain whether the reasonable diligence defence might apply.
I sold the car before the date on the notice. What now?
If you were no longer the keeper on the date of the alleged offence, you can usually say so in your response and give details of the buyer. Evidence such as the sale receipt and confirmation that you told the DVLA will help. If the DVLA record was not updated, a solicitor can explain how this affects your position.
What if the car was stolen or cloned?
If you believe the car was stolen at the time, or that your registration has been copied onto another vehicle, explain this in your response and provide whatever evidence you have, such as a crime reference number or photographs. A solicitor can help you present this clearly if the police do not accept it.
Do I have to sign the form?
The notice will tell you how to respond, which may include signing a declaration or completing an online submission. Follow those instructions exactly. If you are unsure whether a response you sent was valid, or the police say it was incomplete, a solicitor can look at what you sent.
I lent my car to a friend. What do I need to do?
As the keeper, you are expected to give the name and address of the person who was driving. If you lent the car to a friend, that usually means naming them. The police may then send them their own notice. If you are unsure who had the car at the time, think about what records or messages might help you find out, and speak to a solicitor if you are still unsure.
Is there a defence if I genuinely do not know who was driving?
There may be. The keeper has a defence if they can show they did not know and could not have found out with reasonable diligence. Courts look closely at what steps were actually taken. Simply saying you do not know is unlikely to be enough. A solicitor can tell you whether your efforts might meet the test.
I never received the notice. Is that a defence?
It may be. The law usually presumes a notice posted to the right address was received, but that can sometimes be challenged with evidence. If the notice went to an old address because you had not updated your details, the position is more difficult. A solicitor can look at how and where the notice was sent.
What if I was ill or abroad when it arrived?
If it was not reasonably practicable to respond within 28 days, and you responded as soon as it was reasonably practicable, that can be a defence. You will usually need evidence, such as medical records or travel documents. A solicitor can explain how to present this to the police or the court.
Can I name a few people who might have been driving?
Listing several possible drivers does not usually meet the requirement to identify the driver. However, a genuine explanation of who had access and what you did to find out may be relevant to the reasonable diligence defence. A solicitor can look at what you know and explain how the law treats it.
What if I think the original speeding offence did not happen?
Disputing the original offence is not a reason to refuse to name the driver. The two are separate. The proper course is usually to identify the driver and then challenge the original allegation through the court process. A solicitor can advise on both matters together.
What happens if someone gives false details?
Naming someone who was not driving, or agreeing for someone else to take the points, is treated very seriously. It can lead to a charge of perverting the course of justice, which can result in prison. Leaflegal and the firms we introduce do not help anyone do this. A solicitor can explain lawful ways to deal with a notice you are worried about.
I sent the form back but the police say they never got it. What can I do?
Gather any evidence that you responded, such as a copy of the form, proof of posting, an online confirmation, an email receipt or a note of when and where you posted it. A solicitor can use this to raise the issue with the prosecution or the court. If no evidence exists, the solicitor can still explain how the court is likely to approach the question.
What penalty will I get if convicted?
Failing to identify the driver carries six penalty points and a fine. The court can also disqualify you, though that is less common for this offence on its own. You will usually be ordered to pay prosecution costs and a victim surcharge. A company is fined but cannot receive points.
What is the Single Justice Procedure?
It is a way of dealing with some minor offences on paper. You receive a notice and have 21 days to plead guilty or not guilty. A guilty plea is dealt with by a single magistrate without a hearing. A not guilty plea sends the case to a court hearing. If you do not respond, the case can be decided without you.
Can six points lead to a ban?
Yes, if they bring your total to 12 or more within three years, which usually leads to a ban of at least six months. You can argue exceptional hardship to avoid or reduce that ban. New drivers who reach six points within two years of passing their test will have their licence revoked by the DVLA.
I was convicted without knowing. Can that be undone?
Sometimes. If you did not know about the proceedings, for example because papers went to an old address, you may be able to make a statutory declaration to reopen the case. Strict time limits apply from when you found out. A solicitor can explain the options and help you act quickly.
What are special reasons?
Special reasons are circumstances connected with the offence itself, rather than with you personally, which can persuade the court not to give you points. They are rare and depend on the facts. A solicitor will tell you whether anything in your case might amount to special reasons and what evidence would be needed.
Will a section 172 conviction affect my insurance?
Insurers usually ask about motoring convictions, and an MS90 conviction can affect premiums noticeably, sometimes more than a speeding conviction. You must answer insurers' questions honestly. A specialist broker may be able to help if you find cover difficult to obtain.
Will I also be prosecuted for the original offence?
If the driver has not been identified, the police usually cannot prosecute anyone for the original offence, such as speeding, because they do not know who to charge. That is why the section 172 offence carries more points than many original offences. If you are the driver and have named yourself, the original offence is dealt with in the usual way.
Does a company have to name the driver?
Yes. A company that is the registered keeper must identify the driver just as an individual must. The company should have a system so that notices reach the right person quickly and are answered within the deadline. A company that fails to respond can be prosecuted and fined.
Can a director be personally liable?
In some circumstances, yes. If a company commits the offence with the consent or connivance of a director or manager, or because of their neglect, that person can also be guilty. A solicitor can explain how this applies to your role and your business's procedures.
What records should a business keep?
Records showing who used each vehicle and when make it much easier to answer notices accurately. Examples include vehicle logs, key sign out sheets, telematics data and fuel card records. A solicitor can review your current approach and explain how it might be viewed if a defence of reasonable diligence were ever needed.
We lease our vans. Who receives the notice?
The lease company is often the registered keeper, but it usually passes the notice on to the business that leases the vehicle by providing details of the agreement to the police. Your business then has to answer it. Make sure the lease company has your correct address and that notices are dealt with promptly.
When is the best time to speak to a solicitor?
Many people find that advice is most useful before the 28 day deadline passes, while there is still time to respond properly. However, a solicitor can also help after a court notice has arrived or after a conviction you did not know about. The firm will look at your timeline and explain what can be done.
What should I have ready?
Gather the notice and any envelope, the dates you received or saw it, any response you sent and proof of posting or online confirmation. Include details of everyone who had access to the vehicle and what you have done to find out who was driving. Any court papers are important too.
Is Leaflegal a law firm?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service and does not give legal advice. We pass your enquiry to one regulated law firm that handles motoring cases. Any advice comes from that firm. You are free to decide whether to instruct them after speaking to them.
Can the solicitor contact the police for me?
Yes. A solicitor can write to the police or the prosecution on your behalf, for example to explain why a response was late, to provide evidence that a form was sent or to raise a problem with how the notice was served. Whether this changes the outcome depends on the facts, but it can sometimes resolve matters before a trial is needed.
What does driving without insurance cover?
It covers using a vehicle on a road or other public place without a policy that covers that driver, that vehicle and that type of use. It also covers causing or permitting someone else to do so. So an owner who lends their car to someone who is not insured can face the same charge as the driver.
I thought I was insured. Is that a defence?
Usually not on its own, because the offence is one of strict liability. However, a genuine and reasonable belief that you were insured may support an argument for special reasons, which can allow the court not to give points. If you were an employee driving a work vehicle, a specific defence may apply. A solicitor will look at the facts.
Who has to prove I was insured?
In most cases it is for the driver to prove that there was a valid policy covering that use. That is why your policy documents, certificate and schedule are so important. If you say you were insured, gather everything you can and give it to your solicitor as early as possible.
What is the employee defence?
It is a statutory defence for some employees. It can apply where the vehicle did not belong to you and was not hired or lent to you, you were driving it in the course of your employment, and you did not know and had no reason to believe it was uninsured. Each part must be shown, and a solicitor can check whether your situation fits.
Can the vehicle owner be prosecuted too?
Yes. A person who causes or permits someone else to use a vehicle without insurance can be prosecuted, and the penalties are the same as for the driver. This often arises where a car is lent to a friend or family member, or where an employer allows staff to drive a vehicle that is not properly insured.
How long after the incident can I be prosecuted?
Insurance offences have different time limits from many other minor motoring offences. This can allow a prosecution to start some time after the incident, particularly where it took a while for the authorities to obtain the evidence. A solicitor can check whether your case was brought within the time allowed.
Does it matter that I was only driving a short distance?
The offence can be committed over any distance, even moving a car a few metres on a public road. However, in some cases the distance driven and the reason for driving, such as a genuine emergency, may be relevant to whether special reasons exist or to the penalty. A solicitor can explain how courts tend to view the circumstances in your case.
Does my policy let me drive other cars?
Some policies include driving other cars cover, but many do not, and where it is included it is often limited to third party cover for certain drivers. It may not apply to cars you own or that are registered to you. Check your certificate and policy wording. A solicitor can look at the exact terms if you have been charged.
