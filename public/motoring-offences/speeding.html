Speeding explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Speeding is the most common motoring offence in England and Wales. Most drivers who are caught never see the inside of a courtroom. They are offered a speed awareness course or a fixed penalty, pay a fine and either avoid points or take three on their licence. For some people, though, a speeding allegation is much more serious. A high speed can lead to a court hearing and a ban. A modest speed can lead to a totting up ban if the driver already has points.

Speeding means driving above the limit that applies to the road or to the vehicle. Some vehicles, such as heavy goods vehicles, coaches and vehicles towing trailers, have lower limits of their own on certain roads. Temporary limits, such as those in roadworks or set by variable signs on smart motorways, are enforced in the same way as permanent ones.

Speed is usually measured by a fixed or average speed camera, a mobile camera van, a handheld laser or radar device, or a police vehicle fitted with calibrated equipment. The evidence is normally a photograph, a video, or a device reading supported by an officer's statement.

The outcome often depends less on the speed alone than on the whole picture. The road, the time of day, the weather, the driver's record and the number of points already on the licence can all make a difference. Two drivers caught at the same speed in the same place can face quite different consequences.

When people look for a solicitor

  • The speed was high enough that a ban is possible
  • The driver already has points and is close to 12
  • The driver passed their test within the last two years and is close to six points
  • The driver's job depends on holding a licence
  • Something in the paperwork or the timings looks wrong
  • The driver wants help presenting mitigation to the court

Leaflegal does not give legal advice. We introduce you to a regulated motoring solicitor who can read the paperwork and explain the likely outcome and your options.

Speeding cases are driven by paperwork and dates. Many of the questions a solicitor asks at the start are about when things happened and when letters arrived.

Notice of intended prosecution

For speeding, the driver must usually be warned of a possible prosecution. This happens either at the roadside, by a summons within a set time, or by a notice of intended prosecution sent to the registered keeper within 14 days of the offence. There are exceptions, for example where there was an accident. If the vehicle is owned by a company or a hire firm, the timing rules apply to the registered keeper, and later notices to the named driver can arrive after the 14 days.

Request to name the driver

The notice usually comes with a request for information under section 172 of the Road Traffic Act. The registered keeper must say who was driving, normally within 28 days. Failing to respond without a good reason is a separate offence that carries six points, which is more than a typical speeding offence. If you genuinely do not know who was driving, a solicitor can explain what the law expects.

Time limit to prosecute

For speeding, the prosecution usually has to start court proceedings within six months of the offence. This is one of the dates a solicitor will check, along with the dates on the notice.

Keeping track

It helps to keep every letter and envelope, write down the date each one arrived and keep a copy of anything you send back. If you have moved house or the vehicle was recently bought or sold, mention it to the solicitor, because it can affect who received what and when.

If a notice or Single Justice Procedure deadline is close, tell the solicitor as soon as you get in touch. Missing a deadline can mean losing the option of a course or fixed penalty, or the court deciding the case without hearing from you.

Once the driver has been named, the police decide how to deal with the offence. For lower speeds there are usually two options that avoid court.

Speed awareness course

The police may offer a speed awareness course instead of prosecution. It is usually available where the speed was not far above the limit and the driver has not done a similar course recently. The driver pays a fee to the course provider. Completing the course means no points and no conviction. Whether it is offered is at the discretion of the police, and you cannot insist on one.

Fixed penalty

If a course is not offered, the police may offer a fixed penalty. This is a set fine and three points, accepted without going to court. The driver usually has to accept it and send the required details within a time limit. A fixed penalty is not offered where the speed is too high, or where the points would take the driver to 12 or more.

Choosing to go to court

Drivers can usually turn down a fixed penalty and ask for the case to be decided by a court. People sometimes do this where they dispute the evidence. It means the court will decide the outcome, and if convicted the fine and points may be higher than the fixed penalty, plus court costs and a surcharge.

When the case goes straight to court

Where the speed is high, the driver is close to 12 points, or the driver did not accept a fixed penalty in time, the case is sent to court. This is often by a Single Justice Procedure notice, although some cases are listed at a magistrates' court hearing.

Before you accept

Accepting a fixed penalty means accepting the points, and those points count towards totting up. Before accepting, many drivers check how many points they already have and the dates of the earlier offences. If the new points would bring you close to 12, or close to six as a new driver, that is often the point at which people seek advice.

Speeding is dealt with in the magistrates' court. How the case runs depends on how it started and what the driver decides to do.

Single Justice Procedure

Many speeding cases begin with a Single Justice Procedure notice. It sets out the allegation and the evidence, and gives you a deadline to reply. You can plead guilty and include a written statement of mitigation, plead not guilty, or ask for a hearing in open court. A single magistrate then decides guilty plea cases on the papers. If a ban is being considered, the court will usually give you the chance to attend before deciding.

Before a hearing, it helps to think about what you would want the court to know. Courts often ask about your job, how far you travel, whether others rely on you driving and your previous record. A solicitor can help you put this information in order, with documents where they are useful, so that it can be presented clearly.

Hearings in open court

If you plead not guilty, or you want to address the court in person, the case is listed at a magistrates' court. If you plead not guilty, a trial date is set. Trials involve the prosecution evidence, often from a camera operator or an officer, and your own evidence if you give it.

Evidence the court relies on

For speeding, the law says a person cannot be convicted only on one witness's opinion of their speed. There is usually a device reading or other corroboration. Devices used for enforcement must be of an approved type and used correctly. A solicitor can look at the evidence served and explain whether it raises any genuine questions.

Special reasons

In rare cases, there may be special reasons not to add points or impose a ban, such as a genuine medical emergency. The court looks at these strictly, and they need to be supported by evidence. A solicitor can explain whether they might apply.

When sentencing for speeding, the court uses the Sentencing Council guideline. The guideline sorts cases into bands according to how far the speed was over the limit. The higher the band, the higher the fine and points, and at the top end the court will consider a short ban instead of points.

What the court can impose

  • Between three and six points
  • A discretionary ban instead of points, usually for higher speeds
  • A fine linked to your weekly income
  • A victim surcharge and prosecution costs

Factors the court may consider

The court can take into account things that make the offence more or less serious, such as poor weather, a busy area, a school nearby, carrying passengers or towing, and whether the driver has previous convictions. It also listens to personal mitigation, such as a good driving record or the effect of a ban on others.

Totting up

If the points imposed would take you to 12 or more within three years, the court must usually impose a totting up ban of at least six months, unless it accepts an argument of exceptional hardship. This is often more serious than the speeding offence itself.

New drivers

If you passed your test within the last two years and reach six points, the DVLA will usually revoke your licence. You then need to apply for a provisional licence and pass both tests again. The court cannot stop this happening by reducing the points below what the law requires.

When the court adjourns

If a ban is being considered and you are not at court, the court may adjourn the case to give you the chance to attend. It can also adjourn so that an exceptional hardship argument can be prepared. In some adjourned cases, the court has power to impose an interim ban until the sentencing hearing.

Most people deal with a straightforward fixed penalty or speed awareness course on their own. Legal costs become relevant where a ban is possible, where the driver is close to 12 points, or where the evidence is disputed.

How firms usually charge

Motoring firms often offer a fixed fee for reviewing paperwork and advising, and separate fixed fees for a guilty plea hearing or a trial. Where a ban is likely, they may also offer a fee for presenting mitigation, or for an exceptional hardship argument if totting up is involved. Expert evidence about a speed device, if needed, is usually an extra cost.

Fines and court costs

If you are convicted, the court sets the fine based on the guideline and your income, and usually adds a victim surcharge and prosecution costs. These are paid to the court and are separate from what you pay your solicitor.

Is it worth paying for help?

That depends on what is at stake. For many people the cost of a solicitor only makes sense where a ban or a large fine is possible, or where the driver's job or family depends on driving. The firm can talk you through the likely outcome before you decide.

Speeding questions and answers

Can I be banned for speeding?
Yes, either for a very high speed or by reaching 12 points.
What is a Single Justice Procedure notice?
A way of dealing with some minor offences on paper. Get advice before responding if you are near a ban.
Is it worth getting a solicitor?
If a ban is possible or your job depends on driving, specialist advice often makes a difference.
Which areas do you cover?
Motoring offences dealt with in the courts of England and Wales.
Is there legal aid for motoring offences?
Usually not, unless you are at risk of prison. We only introduce people looking for paid legal help.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm aims to contact you quickly, especially if you have a court date coming up. Please give a mobile number you can answer.
What is a notice of intended prosecution?
It is a formal warning that you may be prosecuted for an offence such as speeding. It is usually sent to the registered keeper within 14 days of the offence, unless you were warned at the time or there was an accident. It normally comes with a request to name the driver. A solicitor can check whether it was served correctly.
How long do I have to name the driver?
The request usually gives 28 days to reply. Failing to name the driver without a good reason is a separate offence that carries six points, more than a typical speeding offence. If you are not sure who was driving, or the car is used by several people, it is worth getting advice before the deadline passes.
What if the notice arrived after 14 days?
The 14 days are counted from the offence to the date the notice is treated as served on the registered keeper. If the registered keeper is a company or hire firm, later notices to the named driver can lawfully arrive after 14 days. There are also exceptions, such as accidents. A solicitor can look at the dates and explain whether they matter.
What if I was not the registered keeper?
The notice goes to the registered keeper shown on DVLA records. If you sold the car, moved house without updating your details, or drive a company or hire vehicle, the paperwork can take an unusual route. Keep everything you receive and note the dates. A solicitor can explain how the rules apply to your situation.
What if I cannot remember who was driving?
The law expects the keeper to use reasonable diligence to find out who was driving. If, after genuine efforts, you still cannot say, there may be a defence to the offence of failing to name the driver. It is narrow and depends on the steps taken. A solicitor can explain what is expected and what evidence may be needed.
Can I ask to see the photo?
Some police forces will provide a photograph if you ask, often to help identify the driver. They are not always required to, and many camera photos are taken from behind. Asking for a photo does not usually extend the deadline to name the driver, so it is important to keep track of the date.
What if the car is a company car?
Where a company is the registered keeper, the company must name the driver. The notice to the driver then follows. Companies that do not keep proper records of who uses their vehicles can find it hard to respond, and the company itself can be prosecuted for failing to name the driver. Many fleet managers ask a solicitor to review their process.
How many points will I get for speeding?
Speeding carries between three and six points. A fixed penalty is normally three points. If the case goes to court, the number of points depends on how far over the limit you were, along with other factors, using the Sentencing Council guideline. For higher speeds the court can impose a short ban instead of points.
Can I be banned for speeding?
Yes, in two main ways. The court can impose a discretionary ban for the speeding offence itself, usually where the speed was well above the limit. Or the points for the offence can take you to 12 or more within three years, which normally means a totting up ban of at least six months.
Who decides whether I get a speed awareness course?
The police decide, following national guidance. A course is usually offered where the speed was not much above the limit and you have not done a similar course recently. You cannot insist on one, and it is not available once a case has gone to court. If you are offered a course, you pay the provider directly.
Does a speed awareness course go on my record?
Completing a course means no points and no conviction. The police keep a record that you attended, which is used to decide whether you can be offered another course. Some insurers ask whether you have attended a course, so it is worth answering their questions accurately.
How is the fine worked out?
If the case goes to court, the fine is based on the seriousness of the offence and your weekly income, following the Sentencing Council guideline. You will be asked for details of your finances. If you do not provide them, the court can make assumptions about your income. The court also adds a victim surcharge and usually prosecution costs.
What happens if I am a new driver?
If you passed your test within the last two years and reach six or more points, the DVLA will usually revoke your licence. Two speeding offences can be enough. You would then need to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again. The court cannot prevent this if the points are imposed.
Will speeding affect my insurance?
A speeding conviction or points usually needs to be declared to insurers when they ask, and it may increase premiums, particularly if there is more than one. A speed awareness course is not a conviction, but some insurers ask about courses too. Answering insurers' questions accurately protects your cover if you need to make a claim.
How long do speeding points last?
Speeding points stay on your driving record for four years from the date of the offence. For totting up purposes, the court looks at offences committed within three years of each other. So points can still show on your record for a while after they stop counting towards a totting up ban. You can check your record on GOV.UK.
What is a Single Justice Procedure notice?
It is a way of dealing with some offences on paper. A single magistrate decides the case using the written evidence and your reply, without a hearing in open court. You have a deadline to respond and can plead guilty with mitigation, plead not guilty or ask for a hearing. If a ban is being considered, you will usually be given the chance to attend.
Do I have to go to court for speeding?
Not always. Many cases are dealt with by fixed penalty or by post under the Single Justice Procedure. You may need to attend if you plead not guilty, if a ban is being considered, or if the court has told you to attend. A solicitor can tell you whether you need to be there and can represent you.
What happens if I plead not guilty?
The case is listed for trial at a magistrates' court. The prosecution must prove that you were driving and that you were over the limit, usually with device evidence and officer statements. You can give evidence and call witnesses. A trial takes more time and preparation, so a solicitor will usually review the evidence carefully before a plea is entered.
Can I be convicted on an officer's opinion alone?
No. The law says a person cannot be convicted of speeding solely on the opinion of one witness about their speed. There is usually evidence from a camera or device, or more than one source. A solicitor can look at the evidence served and explain whether it meets the requirements.
What are special reasons in a speeding case?
Special reasons are circumstances connected to the offence that allow the court not to add points or impose a ban. A genuine medical emergency is one example sometimes raised. The court looks at these strictly and needs supporting evidence. They are not about personal hardship. A solicitor can explain whether such an argument might apply.
Can I keep driving until the court decides?
In most cases, yes. A ban starts from the day the court imposes it. The court can sometimes impose an interim ban if a case is adjourned before sentence. Until then you can usually drive, as long as your licence is otherwise valid and you remain insured.
What if I am close to 12 points?
If the points for the new speeding offence would take you to 12 or more within three years, the case will usually go to court and a totting up ban of at least six months is likely. The court can decide not to ban, or to ban for a shorter time, if it accepts that a ban would cause exceptional hardship. A solicitor can explain this argument.
Can speed cameras be challenged?
Speed devices used for enforcement must be of an approved type and used in line with the manufacturer's instructions and police guidance. Challenges to device evidence are possible but need a proper basis and often expert evidence. A solicitor will look at what has been served and explain whether there is anything genuinely worth exploring.
Do speed limit signs have to be in place?
Speed limits generally need to be signed in line with the regulations, although some limits, such as the usual limit on roads with street lights, do not need repeater signs. Where signage is relevant, a solicitor can look at the location and the rules that apply. It is a technical area and depends on the road.
Is dashcam footage useful?
It can be, depending on what it shows. Footage may help establish the conditions, the speed of other traffic, or what was happening at the time. Keep a copy safe and do not edit it. Tell the solicitor that you have it so they can decide whether it is relevant to the evidence or to mitigation.
What is an average speed camera?
Average speed cameras record a vehicle at two or more points and work out the average speed between them. They are common on motorways, at roadworks and on some long stretches of road. The evidence works differently from a single spot reading, and the limit that applies across the section can matter. A solicitor can look at how the reading was produced.
Will I see the evidence before I plead?
If the case goes to court, the prosecution must provide the evidence it relies on, such as the device reading, photographs and officer statements. Single Justice Procedure notices include a summary of the evidence. If you plead not guilty, further material may be served before trial. A solicitor can review it and explain what it shows.
Is it worth getting a solicitor for speeding?
For a simple fixed penalty, many people manage on their own. Help is more often sought where a ban is possible, the driver is close to 12 points, the driver is a new driver, or the paperwork looks wrong. The firm can explain the likely outcome so you can weigh up the cost against what is at stake.
How much does a speeding solicitor cost?
Many firms offer fixed fees, such as a fee for reviewing the paperwork, a fee for a guilty plea hearing and a higher fee for a trial. If totting up is involved, there may be a separate fee for an exceptional hardship argument. Ask what is included and get it in writing.

What should I have ready?
It helps to have the notice of intended prosecution, any later letters, the Single Justice Procedure notice if you have one, and your driving record. Note the dates you received each letter. Think about how you use your car and how a ban would affect you and others, in case it becomes relevant.
How quickly will someone contact me?
Firms aim to get in touch quickly, usually within one working day and often sooner. If you have a deadline on a notice or a court date coming up, mention it in your enquiry so the firm can prioritise. Please give a mobile number you can answer during the day.
What is totting up?
Totting up is the system that leads to a driving ban when you build up 12 or more penalty points from offences committed within three years of each other. The court must usually impose a ban of at least six months. It applies to all drivers, although new drivers face a separate, lower limit of six points in their first two years.
Which points count towards 12?
The court counts points for offences committed within three years of the date of the new offence, together with the points for the new offence itself. It is the offence dates that matter, not when you were convicted. Points that led to an earlier totting up ban do not count again. A solicitor can check your record.
Why do I have points showing that do not count?
Most points stay on your driving record for four years, but only offences within three years of each other count towards totting up. Drink and drug driving related endorsements show for eleven years. So your record may show points that are no longer relevant for totting up purposes.
If my case is delayed, will old points drop off?
Usually not. The three year window is measured between the dates the offences were committed, not the date of the court hearing. A delay in the court process does not normally change which points count. Your solicitor can work out exactly which points apply to the new offence.
What if I get two offences on the same day?
Where offences are committed on the same occasion and dealt with together, the court usually only adds the points for the most serious offence, unless it decides there is a reason not to. Offences on separate occasions are counted separately. A solicitor can look at the dates and how the offences were handled.
How do I check my points?
You can check your record on the GOV.UK view driving licence service using your licence number, National Insurance number and postcode. It shows your endorsements, offence dates and previous bans. It is a good idea to check before replying to a notice or speaking to a solicitor.
Does a speed awareness course add points?
No. A speed awareness course is offered instead of prosecution, so completing it means no conviction and no points. It does not count towards totting up. Whether a course is offered is a decision for the police, and it is not usually available once a case has gone to court or if you have done a similar course recently.
Do points from a fixed penalty count?
Yes. Points accepted through a fixed penalty are endorsed on your licence in the same way as points imposed by a court, and they count towards totting up. That is why a fixed penalty is not usually offered if it would take you to 12 points. In that situation, the case goes to court so that a ban can be considered.
