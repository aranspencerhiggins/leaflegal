Drug driving explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Drug driving cases are newer and often more technical than drink driving cases. Since 2015, it has been an offence in England and Wales to drive, attempt to drive or be in charge of a motor vehicle with certain specified drugs in your blood above a set limit. As with drink driving, the prosecution does not need to show that your driving was affected. If the level of a specified drug in your blood is over its limit, the offence is made out.

The specified drugs fall into two broad groups. The first is a list of illegal drugs, such as cannabis and cocaine, which have very low limits set at a level that rules out accidental exposure. The second is a list of medicines, such as some strong painkillers and anti anxiety medicines, which have higher limits set to reflect normal medical use. Amphetamine has its own limit. The rules for medicines are explained further in the Medicines tab.

Alongside this offence there is an older one of driving while unfit through drugs. That offence does not depend on a specific level. Instead, the prosecution must show that your ability to drive properly was impaired, often using evidence from officers, a roadside impairment test or a doctor. It can apply to any drug, including medicines and substances that are not on the specified list.

Why the outcome matters

A conviction for drug driving carries an obligatory driving ban of at least 12 months, rising to at least three years if you have a relevant previous conviction for drink or drug driving within ten years. The court will also usually impose a fine, and can impose a community order or, in serious cases, a custodial sentence. The conviction stays on your driving record for eleven years.

Common situations

  • A driver stopped for a routine check who tests positive on a roadside swab
  • A patient taking prescribed painkillers or anti anxiety medicine
  • A driver who used cannabis days earlier and believed it had worn off
  • A person involved in an accident where blood was taken at hospital
  • A driver found with both alcohol and drugs in their system

Some people also worry about the wider effects, such as their job, their professional registration, travel or their family. A specialist motoring solicitor can explain how the criminal case is likely to go. Leaflegal does not give legal advice, and we introduce you to a regulated firm that can look at your situation.

Drug driving cases rely heavily on scientific evidence. Understanding how the samples were taken and analysed is a large part of what a solicitor will look at.

Roadside testing

The police can carry out a preliminary drug test at the roadside or at a police station. This is usually a saliva swab on a device that screens for some common drugs, such as cannabis and cocaine. Officers may also carry out a preliminary impairment test, which involves simple physical tasks such as walking in a line or standing on one leg. These tests help officers decide whether to arrest, but they are not usually the evidence of the level of the drug.

The blood sample

After arrest, you are usually asked to give a blood sample at the police station. It is taken by a doctor, nurse or paramedic. Refusing without a reasonable excuse is a separate offence with similar penalties. The sample is normally divided, and you should be offered part of it in a sealed container so that it can be analysed independently if you choose.

Analysis

The police sample is sent to an accredited laboratory, which produces a report showing which drugs were found and at what level. This can take weeks and sometimes months. In the meantime, you are usually released under investigation or on bail and are not charged until the result comes back.

What a solicitor may check

  • Whether the procedure for requesting and taking the sample was followed
  • How the sample was labelled, stored and handed on
  • The analyst's report and the method used
  • Whether the level is close to the limit, allowing for measurement uncertainty
  • Whether an independent analysis of your part of the sample could help
  • Whether the charge should be the specified limit offence or the impairment offence

Whether anything in this list matters in a particular case depends on the evidence. A solicitor will tell you what they find after reviewing the papers.

Many people who face a drug driving allegation have never taken an illegal drug. They take medicine prescribed by a doctor, sometimes for years, and are surprised to find that it can be relevant to driving.

Specified medicines

Some prescription medicines are on the specified list with their own limits. Examples include certain benzodiazepines, such as diazepam, and some opioid painkillers, such as morphine and methadone. The limits are set higher than for illegal drugs, to take into account normal therapeutic use. Many people taking these medicines as directed will be under the limit, but not everyone.

The medical defence

The law provides a defence for someone over the limit for a specified drug if the drug was lawfully prescribed or supplied for medical or dental purposes and was taken in line with the advice of the prescriber and the instructions that came with it. The defence does not apply if the advice included not driving, or waiting a certain time after taking it, and that advice was not followed. It is for the driver to raise the defence, and evidence such as prescriptions and medical records is usually needed.

Prescribed cannabis based medicines

Some cannabis based medicines can now be lawfully prescribed by specialist doctors. If a person with such a prescription is found over the limit for the relevant substance, the same medical defence may be relevant. Whether it applies depends on the exact medicine, the prescription and how it was taken.

Impairment still matters

Even where a medicine is taken exactly as prescribed, it is still an offence to drive while your ability to drive is impaired. The medical defence applies to the specified limit offence, not to the separate impairment offence. Some people also have a duty to tell the DVLA about medical conditions that affect driving, which is a separate issue from any prosecution.

A solicitor can look at your prescriptions, medical records and the analyst's report and explain whether a medical defence may be available to you.

Drug driving is dealt with in the magistrates' court. Because of the time needed for blood analysis, the case often reaches court several months after the date you were stopped.

The time between being stopped and receiving court papers can feel long and uncertain. Many people use it to get their documents together, such as prescriptions, medical letters and their driving record, and to speak to a solicitor so that they understand what the evidence is likely to show.

Postal requisition

Once the result is back, you are usually sent a postal requisition by the Crown Prosecution Service or the police. It sets out the charge and tells you which court to attend and when. It may arrive with a summary of the evidence, including the level found in your blood.

First hearing

At the first hearing you will be asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you that day or adjourn for a report. If you plead not guilty, the court sets a trial date and gives directions about the evidence each side needs to provide, including any expert reports.

Expert evidence

Contested drug driving cases often involve experts. The prosecution may rely on the laboratory analyst. The defence may want its own scientist to review the analysis, the uncertainty of measurement, or the likely effect of a medicine. A solicitor will explain whether expert evidence is needed and what it would cost before it is arranged.

Trial

At a trial, the prosecution must prove its case. If the medical defence is raised, the court will hear evidence about the prescription and how the medicine was taken. The magistrates or district judge then decide whether the offence is proved, and if so, go on to sentence.

Hospital cases

If you were taken to hospital after an accident, the procedure for requesting a sample is different, and medical staff must be told before a sample is requested. These cases can be more complex, and a solicitor will look closely at how the sample was obtained.

The Sentencing Council publishes a guideline for drug driving. Unlike drink driving, the ban is not tied to a simple table of levels, because different drugs affect people in different ways and the level in blood does not always show how impaired someone was.

What the court looks at

  • Whether more than one drug, or drugs and alcohol, were found
  • The standard of driving and whether there was an accident
  • Whether passengers were carried, especially children
  • Whether the vehicle was being driven for hire or reward
  • Previous convictions
  • Personal mitigation and an early guilty plea

The ban and other penalties

The minimum ban is 12 months, or three years for a repeat within ten years. The court can impose a longer ban where the circumstances are more serious. It will also usually impose a fine linked to your income, and may make a community order. A custodial sentence is possible in the most serious cases.

No rehabilitation course

The rehabilitation course that can shorten a drink driving ban is not usually available for drug driving. Drivers convicted of drug driving should expect to serve the full ban the court imposes, unless it is changed on appeal.

Getting the licence back

At the end of the ban you apply to the DVLA to get your licence back. In some cases, particularly where there is evidence of misuse or dependency, the DVLA may make medical enquiries before returning it.

The solicitor can explain how the court is likely to approach the factors in your case and what kind of personal information, such as work or caring responsibilities, may be relevant to mitigation.

Legal aid is rarely available for drug driving, so most people pay privately. Fees depend on the stage the case reaches and whether expert evidence is needed.

How firms usually charge

A guilty plea hearing is often offered at a fixed fee that covers reviewing the evidence, preparing mitigation and attending court. A trial or a hearing about the medical defence is usually charged at a higher fixed fee or by the hour. Reports from forensic scientists or toxicologists are an extra cost and can be significant, so the firm should explain the likely amount and why it is needed before you commit.

Costs if convicted

On conviction, the court will usually order a fine, a victim surcharge and a contribution towards the prosecution's costs. These are separate from your legal fees.

There can be, depending on the medicine and whether you took it as directed. A solicitor will advise.

Motoring offences dealt with in the courts of England and Wales.

How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm aims to contact you quickly, especially if you have a court date coming up. Please give a mobile number you can answer.
What is drug driving?
Drug driving usually means driving, attempting to drive or being in charge of a vehicle with a specified drug in your blood above its legal limit. There is also an older offence of driving while unfit through drugs, which depends on showing impairment rather than a set level. Both are criminal offences dealt with in the magistrates' court, and both usually lead to a ban.
Which drugs are covered?
The specified limit offence covers a list of drugs set in regulations. It includes illegal drugs, such as cannabis, cocaine, ketamine and MDMA, with very low limits, and some medicines, such as diazepam, morphine and methadone, with higher limits. Amphetamine has a separate limit. The impairment offence can apply to any drug that affects your ability to drive.
Do the police have to prove my driving was affected?
Not for the specified limit offence. If the level of a specified drug in your blood is over the limit, the offence is made out even if your driving seemed normal. For the separate impairment offence, the prosecution does need to show that your ability to drive properly was impaired. Your solicitor will explain which offence you face.
Can I be charged if I was not driving?
Yes. Being in charge of a vehicle with a specified drug above the limit is an offence, even if the vehicle was parked. The penalties for being in charge are different from those for driving, and there can be a defence if there was no likelihood of you driving while over the limit. A solicitor can look at the facts.
Is drug driving treated the same as drink driving?
In many ways, yes. Both carry an obligatory minimum ban of 12 months and stay on your record for eleven years. The differences are mainly in the evidence and sentencing. Drug driving depends on blood analysis, the sentencing guideline is not linked to a simple table of levels, and the rehabilitation course that can shorten a drink driving ban is not usually available.
Is drug driving a criminal offence?
Yes. It is a criminal offence and a conviction appears on your criminal record as well as your driving record. It can affect insurance, some jobs and travel to certain countries. Separately from the criminal case, if you are found to have been in possession of an illegal drug, that could be dealt with as a different offence.
How long do drugs stay in the blood?
It varies a great deal between drugs and between people, depending on things like the dose, how often the drug is used, body weight and metabolism. Some substances can remain in the blood for longer than people expect, even after they no longer feel any effect. There is no reliable rule of thumb, which is why many cases need careful scientific review.
What is the roadside drug test?
It is a preliminary test, usually a saliva swab on a handheld device, that screens for some common drugs such as cannabis and cocaine. A positive result usually leads to arrest and a blood test at the police station. The roadside result on its own is not normally the evidence of the level used to prosecute.
Can I refuse a blood test?
Refusing to give a blood sample without a reasonable excuse is an offence in itself, with penalties similar to drug driving. A reasonable excuse is usually medical, such as a genuine phobia of needles supported by evidence. Whether an excuse is reasonable is decided by the court. A solicitor can explain how this is assessed.
Why is my case taking so long?
Blood samples must be analysed by an accredited laboratory, and this can take weeks or months. You are usually released while you wait and are not charged until the results are back. The delay can be stressful, but it also gives time to gather documents, such as prescriptions, and to speak to a solicitor before any court date.
Can I get my own sample tested?
When blood is taken, you should be offered part of the sample. You can ask an independent laboratory to analyse it, usually through your solicitor, who can arrange secure collection and storage. Independent analysis has a cost and is not useful in every case. Your solicitor can explain whether it is likely to help in yours.
What if the level is only just over the limit?
Laboratory results come with a margin of uncertainty, and reports usually take this into account. Where a level is close to the limit, a solicitor may want to look closely at how the analysis was done and what the uncertainty is. Whether this makes a difference depends on the evidence in the particular case.
Could passive exposure explain a positive result?
People sometimes say they were near others using drugs rather than taking them themselves. The limits for illegal drugs were set with accidental exposure in mind, so this explanation is not straightforward. Whether it has any relevance would usually need expert evidence. A solicitor can talk it through after seeing the analyst's report.
What is a field impairment test?
It is a set of simple physical tasks an officer may ask you to carry out, such as walking heel to toe, standing on one leg or following a moving object with your eyes. The officer records how you perform. It is mainly used to decide whether to arrest and can be relevant evidence in the separate offence of driving while unfit through drugs.
Can I be prosecuted for taking my prescribed medicine?
It is possible if your medicine is on the specified list and your blood level is over its limit, or if your driving was impaired. However, there is a medical defence to the specified limit offence where the medicine was lawfully prescribed and taken in line with the advice given. A solicitor will look at the prescription and how you took it.
What do I need to show for the medical defence?
Generally, you need to show that the drug was lawfully prescribed or supplied for medical or dental purposes, and that you took it in line with the prescriber's advice and the instructions that came with it. Evidence such as the prescription, the patient leaflet and medical records is usually needed. Your solicitor will explain how the defence is raised.
Does the medical defence cover prescribed cannabis?
It can be relevant where a cannabis based medicine was lawfully prescribed by a specialist and taken as directed. Each case depends on the exact product, the prescription, the advice given and whether your driving was affected. A solicitor can look at the paperwork and explain whether the defence could apply in your situation.
What if my doctor did not mention driving?
The medical defence turns on whether you followed the advice and instructions you were given, including the leaflet supplied with the medicine. If nobody advised against driving, that may be relevant. However, the separate impairment offence can still apply if your driving was affected. A solicitor will look at what advice you actually received.
Do I need to tell the DVLA about my medication?
Taking medicine is not in itself something you have to report. You do have a duty to tell the DVLA about certain medical conditions that can affect driving. The GOV.UK website lists notifiable conditions. This is separate from any criminal case, and your doctor or the DVLA can tell you what applies to you.
What if I take several medicines?
Taking more than one medicine is common, and the analyst's report will show which specified drugs were found and at what level. The court can treat the presence of more than one drug as making an offence more serious, but the circumstances matter. Your prescriptions and medical records help a solicitor understand the full picture and explain how the evidence may be viewed.
Will I definitely be banned?
A conviction for drug driving carries an obligatory ban of at least 12 months, or at least three years if you have a relevant previous conviction within ten years. The court can only decide not to ban if special reasons are found, which is rare. If the medical defence succeeds, there is no conviction and so no ban.
How long will the ban be?
The court uses the Sentencing Council guideline. It looks at factors such as whether more than one drug was found, whether alcohol was also involved, the standard of driving and your previous record. The minimum is 12 months, and more serious cases attract longer bans. A solicitor can give a realistic range once they see the evidence.
Can my ban be reduced with a course?
The drink drive rehabilitation course is not usually available for drug driving. That means people convicted of drug driving should generally expect to serve the full ban. The court can, however, take into account personal mitigation and an early guilty plea when deciding the length of the ban within the guideline range.
What happens at the first hearing?
You will usually be asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you that day after hearing the facts and your mitigation, or adjourn for a report. If you plead not guilty, the court sets a trial date and timetable for evidence. A solicitor can attend with you and explain what is happening.
Could I go to prison?
A custodial sentence is possible but is usually kept for the most serious cases, such as those involving very poor driving, an accident or repeat offences. Many people receive a ban and a fine, and some receive a community order. A solicitor will look at the guideline and explain where your case is likely to sit.
Can I drive while I wait for the result?
Usually, yes. Unless a court has banned you or your bail conditions say otherwise, you can normally continue to drive while the blood sample is analysed and until the court decides the case. You still have to stay within the law, and a further allegation while you wait would be treated seriously. A solicitor can explain anything specific to your situation.
Can I appeal a drug driving conviction?
There is usually a right of appeal from the magistrates' court to the Crown Court against conviction, sentence or both. The time limit is short, normally 21 days from sentence. In some cases you can ask for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is heard. A solicitor can look at what happened and explain whether an appeal is realistic.
How much does a drug driving solicitor cost?
Many firms offer fixed fees for a guilty plea hearing and a higher fee or hourly rate for a trial or a medical defence hearing. Expert reports are usually an extra cost. Fees vary, so ask the firm what is included and get it confirmed in writing before any work starts.
Is legal aid available?
Legal aid is not usually available for drug driving, because it depends mainly on whether there is a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately. If the solicitor thinks legal aid might be possible in your case, they can explain how to apply.

What should I gather before speaking to a solicitor?
Useful items include any paperwork from the police station, your postal requisition and court date if you have them, details of any prescriptions with the patient leaflets, the names of your doctors and your driving record. A short note of what happened on the day, written while you remember it, can also help.
Why might an expert be needed?
Drug driving evidence is scientific. An expert may be asked to review how the laboratory carried out the analysis, explain measurement uncertainty, or give an opinion on how a medicine is likely to have affected you. Not every case needs one. The solicitor will explain whether an expert could help and what it is likely to cost before arranging anything.
What is a notice of intended prosecution?
It is a formal warning that you may be prosecuted for an offence such as speeding. It is usually sent to the registered keeper within 14 days of the offence, unless you were warned at the time or there was an accident. It normally comes with a request to name the driver. A solicitor can check whether it was served correctly.
How long do I have to name the driver?
The request usually gives 28 days to reply. Failing to name the driver without a good reason is a separate offence that carries six points, more than a typical speeding offence. If you are not sure who was driving, or the car is used by several people, it is worth getting advice before the deadline passes.
What if the notice arrived after 14 days?
The 14 days are counted from the offence to the date the notice is treated as served on the registered keeper. If the registered keeper is a company or hire firm, later notices to the named driver can lawfully arrive after 14 days. There are also exceptions, such as accidents. A solicitor can look at the dates and explain whether they matter.
What if I was not the registered keeper?
The notice goes to the registered keeper shown on DVLA records. If you sold the car, moved house without updating your details, or drive a company or hire vehicle, the paperwork can take an unusual route. Keep everything you receive and note the dates. A solicitor can explain how the rules apply to your situation.
What if I cannot remember who was driving?
The law expects the keeper to use reasonable diligence to find out who was driving. If, after genuine efforts, you still cannot say, there may be a defence to the offence of failing to name the driver. It is narrow and depends on the steps taken. A solicitor can explain what is expected and what evidence may be needed.
Can I ask to see the photo?
Some police forces will provide a photograph if you ask, often to help identify the driver. They are not always required to, and many camera photos are taken from behind. Asking for a photo does not usually extend the deadline to name the driver, so it is important to keep track of the date.
What if the car is a company car?
Where a company is the registered keeper, the company must name the driver. The notice to the driver then follows. Companies that do not keep proper records of who uses their vehicles can find it hard to respond, and the company itself can be prosecuted for failing to name the driver. Many fleet managers ask a solicitor to review their process.
How many points will I get for speeding?
Speeding carries between three and six points. A fixed penalty is normally three points. If the case goes to court, the number of points depends on how far over the limit you were, along with other factors, using the Sentencing Council guideline. For higher speeds the court can impose a short ban instead of points.
