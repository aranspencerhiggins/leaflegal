About drink driving

Drink driving carries a minimum driving ban of 12 months for most first offences, as well as a possible fine or other sentence. A conviction can affect your job, your insurance and travel abroad.

A motoring solicitor will look at the evidence, including how the procedure was followed, explain whether there is a defence and, if you plead guilty, put forward mitigation to keep the sentence as low as possible. They may also explain the drink drive rehabilitation course.

Drink driving solicitors in brief

A drink driving conviction normally brings a driving ban of at least 12 months, and can affect your job and insurance for years. Drink driving solicitors check the procedure and evidence, advise on special reasons that could avoid a ban and represent you at court.

Drink driving explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Drink driving is one of the most common reasons people end up in a magistrates' court for the first time. It means driving or attempting to drive a motor vehicle on a road or other public place after drinking so much alcohol that the level in your breath, blood or urine is over the legal limit. The offence is complete once the level is over the limit. The prosecution does not have to show that your driving was affected.

In England and Wales the limits are 35 microgrammes of alcohol in 100 millilitres of breath, 80 milligrammes in 100 millilitres of blood, or 107 milligrammes in 100 millilitres of urine. Scotland has lower limits, so it is important to know which part of the UK the case is in. The limit applies to the reading taken as evidence at the police station or hospital, not the roadside screening test.

A conviction carries an obligatory driving ban of at least 12 months for most people. If you have had a previous conviction for certain drink or drug driving offences in the last ten years, the minimum is three years. On top of the ban, the court will usually impose a fine, and in more serious cases it can impose a community order or a custodial sentence. The conviction stays on your driving record for eleven years.

Related offences

There are several offences that sit close to drink driving and are often confused with it. Each has its own rules and its own possible outcomes.

  • Being in charge of a vehicle while over the limit, for example sitting in a parked car with the keys
  • Failing to provide a specimen of breath, blood or urine for analysis without a reasonable excuse
  • Failing to provide a preliminary breath test at the roadside
  • Driving while unfit through drink, where the focus is on impairment rather than a measured level
  • Causing death or serious injury by careless driving while over the limit, which is far more serious and dealt with differently

Much of a drink driving case is about what happened between the moment you were stopped and the moment a reading was produced. The law sets out a detailed procedure for how specimens are taken, and a solicitor will usually look at each stage carefully.

At the roadside

The police can ask a driver to take a preliminary breath test in certain situations, such as after an accident, where they suspect alcohol, or after a moving traffic offence. This is a screening test on a handheld device. It decides whether you are arrested and taken to a police station, but it is not usually the reading used to prosecute.

At the police station

At the station you are usually asked to provide two specimens of breath into an approved evidential machine. The lower of the two readings is the one relied on. The machine prints a record, and officers fill in a form that sets out each step. You have the right to free legal advice at the police station, although the police are not always required to delay the breath procedure while you wait for it.

Blood or urine instead

In some situations the police ask for blood or urine instead of breath. This can happen if the machine is not working properly, if one is not available, or if there is a medical reason why breath cannot be given. A blood sample is taken by a healthcare professional. You should be offered part of the sample so that it can be analysed independently if you wish. The analysis can take some weeks.

Charge or release

If the evidential reading is over the limit, you will usually be charged and bailed to appear at a magistrates' court on a set date, often within a few weeks. Where blood or urine was taken, you are more likely to be released while the sample is analysed and then sent a postal requisition telling you when to attend court.

A solicitor will look at the custody record, the printouts, the forms completed by officers and any body worn camera footage. Whether anything in the procedure matters to your case is something only they can assess after seeing the papers.

Drink driving is a summary offence, which means it is dealt with in the magistrates' court. The case is usually heard by a district judge or a bench of magistrates. Most cases are listed for a first hearing a few weeks after charge.

Entering a plea

At the first hearing you are asked whether you plead guilty or not guilty. Many people plead guilty, especially where the reading is clear and the procedure was followed. Pleading guilty at the first opportunity usually means a reduction in the sentence. If there is a real dispute about the evidence or the procedure, a not guilty plea leads to a trial on a later date.

Sentence on the day

If you plead guilty, the court will often sentence you at the same hearing. The prosecutor reads out the facts and the reading, and your solicitor or barrister can then put forward mitigation. In higher reading cases, or where a community order is being considered, the court may ask the Probation Service for a report and adjourn for a few weeks.

Special reasons

A small number of cases involve special reasons. These are circumstances connected with the offence, not the person, which can allow the court not to impose the ban even though the offence is admitted. Examples sometimes raised include a drink that was spiked without your knowledge, a genuine emergency, or driving a very short distance in circumstances where no danger was likely. These arguments need evidence and are decided at a separate hearing. Courts look at them closely, and a solicitor will explain whether one could realistically apply.

Trial

If you plead not guilty, the prosecution has to prove the case. Officers may give evidence and be cross examined. Expert evidence can be relevant in some cases, for example where it is said that alcohol was drunk after driving. Trials take longer to prepare and cost more, so a solicitor will want to look at the evidence thoroughly before a plea is entered.

The court follows sentencing guidelines published by the Sentencing Council. For drink driving the main factor is the level of alcohol in the evidential reading. The higher the reading, the longer the ban and the more serious the other penalties tend to be.

Factors the court looks at

  • The evidential reading
  • The quality of the driving and any accident
  • Whether there were passengers, especially children
  • Whether the vehicle was being used for hire or reward
  • Previous convictions, especially for driving offences
  • Personal mitigation, such as remorse, good character or caring responsibilities
  • Whether a guilty plea was entered early

The rehabilitation course

Where the ban is 12 months or more, the court can offer a drink drive rehabilitation course. If you complete it by the date the court sets, the ban is reduced. The course is run by approved providers and the driver pays for it. It is offered by the court, so it is worth asking the solicitor whether the court is likely to make the offer.

High risk offenders

Some drivers are treated by the DVLA as high risk offenders. This usually applies to readings of two and a half times the limit or more, to people convicted of failing to provide a specimen, and to people with two drink driving convictions within ten years. Before getting their licence back, high risk offenders need to pass a medical assessment arranged through the DVLA.

The effect of the guilty plea

A guilty plea at the first reasonable opportunity reduces the overall sentence, including the fine. It does not usually reduce the ban below the minimum the law requires. The solicitor can explain how this might work out in your case.

Most people pay privately for help with a drink driving case. Legal aid is only granted in limited situations, usually where there is a real risk of a prison sentence.

Typical fee structure

Many motoring firms offer a fixed fee for a guilty plea hearing, which usually covers reviewing the papers, preparing mitigation and attending court. A special reasons hearing or a trial is normally charged at a higher fixed fee or by the hour, because more preparation is involved. Expert evidence, such as a report from a forensic scientist, is usually an extra cost.

Other costs to plan for

Some people choose to speak to a solicitor only for a short review of the evidence before deciding whether to instruct them for the hearing. Many firms are happy to talk about this. Knowing what the review covers, and what it costs, helps you plan.

If you are convicted, the court will usually order a fine linked to your income, a victim surcharge and a contribution to the prosecution's costs. You will also want to think about the course fee if the rehabilitation course is offered, the cost of getting around during the ban, and the likely effect on your insurance once you are back on the road.

Ask the firm what their fee includes, whether a barrister may be used and whether travel to a distant court is extra. The fee should be confirmed in writing.

Every drink driving case is different, but solicitors see some situations again and again.

The morning after

People are often caught the morning after an evening of drinking. Alcohol leaves the body slowly and at different rates for different people, so feeling fine is not a reliable guide. The law treats a morning after case the same as any other, although the circumstances can be relevant to mitigation.

Drinking after driving

Sometimes a driver says they drank alcohol after they stopped driving but before they were tested. The law assumes the reading reflects the level at the time of driving, and it is for the driver to show otherwise. This usually needs expert evidence about how alcohol is absorbed. A solicitor can explain how the court approaches this.

Being in charge

Sitting in a car while over the limit, even without driving, can be an offence if you are in charge of the vehicle. There is a defence if you can show there was no likelihood of you driving while still over the limit. Being in charge carries points or a discretionary ban rather than an obligatory one.

Medical problems with the test

Some people cannot give a breath specimen because of a medical condition, such as a respiratory problem. If you were charged with failing to provide, your medical history can be relevant to whether you had a reasonable excuse. Medical records and expert evidence may be needed.

Employment

For many people the biggest worry is work. A ban can affect anyone who drives for a living, and some professions have their own reporting rules. A solicitor can explain the criminal side, and your employer's policies or your professional body can tell you about any separate duties.

Drink driving questions and answers

Can a drink driving solicitor stop a ban?
A ban is usually mandatory, but a solicitor can check whether the evidence and procedure were correct and whether special reasons apply, which can avoid or reduce a ban.
Will I definitely be banned?
Most people convicted of drink driving receive a ban of at least 12 months. A solicitor will explain whether any exceptions or defences could apply.
Should I plead guilty?
Get advice before your first hearing. A solicitor will review the evidence first.
Can I get a shorter ban?
Many courts offer a drink drive rehabilitation course, which can reduce the ban if you complete it.
Which areas do you cover?
Motoring offences dealt with in the courts of England and Wales.
Is there legal aid for motoring offences?
Usually not, unless you are at risk of prison. We only introduce people looking for paid legal help.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm aims to contact you quickly, especially if you have a court date coming up. Please give a mobile number you can answer.
What is the drink driving limit in England and Wales?
The limits are 35 microgrammes of alcohol in 100 millilitres of breath, 80 milligrammes in 100 millilitres of blood and 107 milligrammes in 100 millilitres of urine. The limits are lower in Scotland. There is no safe number of drinks that guarantees you will be under the limit, because the level depends on many things, including your weight, what you have eaten and how quickly your body processes alcohol. A solicitor can explain how the limits apply to your reading.
Can I be charged if I was not actually driving?
You can be charged with being in charge of a vehicle while over the limit, even if you were not driving. This can apply to someone sitting in a parked car with the keys. There is a defence if you can show there was no likelihood of you driving while you were still over the limit. A solicitor can explain how this works on your facts.
What does failing to provide a specimen mean?
It is an offence to fail, without a reasonable excuse, to provide a specimen of breath, blood or urine when properly asked. If you were driving or attempting to drive, it carries an obligatory ban like drink driving itself, and the DVLA treats it seriously. A reasonable excuse is usually medical. A solicitor will look at the circumstances and any medical evidence.
Is drink driving a criminal offence?
Yes. Drink driving is a criminal offence, and a conviction is a criminal conviction as well as an endorsement on your licence. It stays on your driving record for eleven years. It may need to be disclosed to insurers and some employers, and it can be relevant to travel to some countries. How long it must be disclosed in other contexts depends on the rehabilitation rules.
What if I was caught the morning after?
Morning after cases are treated in the same way as any other drink driving case. If the evidential reading is over the limit, the offence is made out, even if you felt sober. The circumstances, such as the time since your last drink, can be relevant to mitigation. A solicitor will explain how the court is likely to see it.
Is it different if I drove a very short distance?
Driving a short distance does not stop it being an offence. In rare cases, a very short distance in circumstances where there was little chance of danger can be raised as a special reason not to impose a ban. Courts look at this strictly and consider things like how far you drove, why, and the road and traffic conditions. A solicitor can say whether it is realistic.
Which reading is used against me?
If you gave breath at the police station, the lower of the two evidential readings is the one used. The roadside screening reading is not usually the one relied on in court. If blood or urine was taken instead, the analysed level from that sample is used. Your solicitor will see the printout or the analyst's certificate as part of the evidence.
Why did the police take blood instead of breath?
The police can ask for blood or urine if the breath machine is unreliable or unavailable, or if there is a medical reason you cannot give breath. In some situations, such as a hospital case, the procedure is different again. The reasons and the steps followed are recorded, and a solicitor will look at them as part of reviewing the case.
Can I get my blood sample tested independently?
When blood is taken, you should be offered part of the sample in a sealed container. You can arrange for it to be analysed by an independent laboratory. This is usually done through a solicitor, because the sample needs to be stored and handled correctly. Whether an independent analysis would be useful is something the solicitor can discuss with you.
Can a breath machine be wrong?
Evidential breath machines must be of an approved type and are checked as part of the procedure, but challenges to the reliability of a reading are sometimes made. These need specific evidence, often expert evidence, and are not straightforward. A solicitor can look at the printout, the procedure and any records to see whether there is anything worth exploring.
Should I have asked for a solicitor at the police station?
You have the right to free legal advice at the police station. The police do not always have to wait for a solicitor before carrying out the breath procedure, because the reading could change with time. If you did not get advice at the station, you can still speak to a solicitor about the case before your first court hearing.
What if I drank after I stopped driving?
The law assumes that the alcohol level at the time of the test reflects the level when you were driving. If you drank after driving, it is for you to prove that the later drink took you over the limit. This usually needs expert evidence and careful calculations. A solicitor will explain what evidence would be needed and how the court looks at this argument.
Will I definitely be banned?
For most people, a conviction for drink driving means an obligatory ban of at least 12 months, or three years if there is a relevant previous conviction within ten years. The court can only decide not to ban if special reasons are found, which is rare. A solicitor will explain what is likely in your case after seeing the evidence.
How long will my ban be?
The length depends mainly on the evidential reading, together with other factors such as the standard of driving and any previous convictions. The Sentencing Council guidelines link higher readings to longer bans. The court also considers personal mitigation. A solicitor can give you a realistic idea of the range once they know the reading and your circumstances.
Could I go to prison for drink driving?
A custodial sentence is possible, but it is usually reserved for the most serious cases, such as very high readings, very bad driving, or repeat offences. Many people receive a fine and a ban. Some receive a community order. The solicitor will look at the guidelines and explain where your case is likely to sit.
Can I reduce my ban with a course?
If you are banned for 12 months or more, the court can offer you a drink drive rehabilitation course. If you complete it by the deadline the court sets, your ban is reduced. You pay for the course yourself. The court decides whether to offer it, and the solicitor can tell you whether it is likely to be offered in your case.
What happens at the first court hearing?
You will usually be asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you that day after hearing the facts and your mitigation. If a report is needed, the case is adjourned. If you plead not guilty, a trial date is set. A solicitor can attend with you and explain each step as it happens.
Does a guilty plea reduce the ban?
An early guilty plea reduces the overall sentence, such as the fine or the length of a community order. It cannot take the ban below the legal minimum. Within the guideline range, a plea may affect the length of the ban the court chooses. The solicitor can explain how credit for a plea is likely to work in your case.
What are special reasons?
Special reasons are circumstances connected to the offence that allow a court not to impose an obligatory ban, or to impose a shorter one. Examples raised in court include a spiked drink, a genuine medical emergency or a very short drive. They are not about personal hardship. They need evidence and a separate hearing, and courts are careful about accepting them.
Can I keep driving before my court date?
In most drink driving cases, yes. The ban normally starts on the day the court imposes it, not on the day you were stopped or charged. If you were bailed with conditions, check whether any of them affect driving. Many people use the time before the hearing to arrange other ways of getting to work and meeting family commitments.
How do I get my licence back?
When the ban ends, you usually need to apply to the DVLA for your licence to be returned. If you are classed as a high risk offender, you will need to pass a medical assessment arranged through the DVLA before the licence is returned. The DVLA sends information about this towards the end of the ban.
What is a high risk offender?
It is a category used by the DVLA for some drink driving offenders. It usually covers people with a reading of two and a half times the limit or more, people convicted of failing to provide a specimen, and people with two drink driving convictions within ten years. They must pass a medical assessment before their licence is returned.
Will my insurance go up after the ban?
Almost certainly. Insurers treat drink driving convictions seriously, and many will ask about them for several years. Some insurers decline cover, and specialist insurers may charge much more. Being honest with insurers when they ask is important, because failing to disclose a conviction can cause problems if you make a claim.
Can I drive abroad while banned in the UK?
A UK driving ban means you hold no valid licence during the ban, and an International Driving Permit relies on a valid licence. Different countries also have their own rules about recognising foreign bans. It is not something to assume. Check with the authorities of the country concerned and speak to a solicitor if you need to know more.
Can I appeal a drink driving ban?
There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court against conviction, sentence or both, within 21 days of sentence. In some cases you can apply for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is decided. Appeals need to have proper grounds. A solicitor can look at what happened and tell you whether an appeal is realistic.
Will my employer find out?
The court does not usually contact your employer. However, if you drive for work, your employer may check your licence or ask you to declare convictions, and some contracts require you to tell them. Certain professions also have reporting duties to a regulator. Your contract, staff handbook or professional body can tell you what applies to you.
How much does a drink driving solicitor cost?
Many firms offer a fixed fee for a guilty plea hearing, and a higher fee or hourly rate for a special reasons hearing or a trial. Expert evidence is usually extra. Fees vary between firms and areas. The firm will explain what is included and confirm it in writing before you agree to anything.
Is legal aid available?
Legal aid is not usually available for drink driving, because it depends mainly on whether there is a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately. If your case is serious enough that legal aid might be available, the solicitor can tell you and explain how to apply.

What should I have ready when the solicitor calls?
It helps to have your charge sheet or postal requisition, your court date, any paperwork from the police station, your driving record and an idea of how a ban would affect you and others. If you have medical conditions that may be relevant, mention them. The solicitor will tell you what else they need.
What will the solicitor do for me?
A motoring solicitor will usually read the police evidence, check that the procedure was followed, explain the likely sentence range and talk through whether there is anything to challenge. If you plead guilty, they prepare and present mitigation. If there is a special reasons argument or a trial, they prepare the evidence and represent you, sometimes with a barrister.
What is drug driving?
Drug driving usually means driving, attempting to drive or being in charge of a vehicle with a specified drug in your blood above its legal limit. There is also an older offence of driving while unfit through drugs, which depends on showing impairment rather than a set level. Both are criminal offences dealt with in the magistrates' court, and both usually lead to a ban.
Which drugs are covered?
The specified limit offence covers a list of drugs set in regulations. It includes illegal drugs, such as cannabis, cocaine, ketamine and MDMA, with very low limits, and some medicines, such as diazepam, morphine and methadone, with higher limits. Amphetamine has a separate limit. The impairment offence can apply to any drug that affects your ability to drive.
Do the police have to prove my driving was affected?
Not for the specified limit offence. If the level of a specified drug in your blood is over the limit, the offence is made out even if your driving seemed normal. For the separate impairment offence, the prosecution does need to show that your ability to drive properly was impaired. Your solicitor will explain which offence you face.
Can I be charged if I was not driving?
Yes. Being in charge of a vehicle with a specified drug above the limit is an offence, even if the vehicle was parked. The penalties for being in charge are different from those for driving, and there can be a defence if there was no likelihood of you driving while over the limit. A solicitor can look at the facts.
Is drug driving treated the same as drink driving?
In many ways, yes. Both carry an obligatory minimum ban of 12 months and stay on your record for eleven years. The differences are mainly in the evidence and sentencing. Drug driving depends on blood analysis, the sentencing guideline is not linked to a simple table of levels, and the rehabilitation course that can shorten a drink driving ban is not usually available.
Is drug driving a criminal offence?
Yes. It is a criminal offence and a conviction appears on your criminal record as well as your driving record. It can affect insurance, some jobs and travel to certain countries. Separately from the criminal case, if you are found to have been in possession of an illegal drug, that could be dealt with as a different offence.
How long do drugs stay in the blood?
It varies a great deal between drugs and between people, depending on things like the dose, how often the drug is used, body weight and metabolism. Some substances can remain in the blood for longer than people expect, even after they no longer feel any effect. There is no reliable rule of thumb, which is why many cases need careful scientific review.
What is the roadside drug test?
It is a preliminary test, usually a saliva swab on a handheld device, that screens for some common drugs such as cannabis and cocaine. A positive result usually leads to arrest and a blood test at the police station. The roadside result on its own is not normally the evidence of the level used to prosecute.
