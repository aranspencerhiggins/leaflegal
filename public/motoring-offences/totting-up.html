Totting up ban in brief

A totting up ban usually follows when you reach 12 or more points within three years, and the minimum ban is six months. The main way to avoid it is to show exceptional hardship.

A totting up solicitor prepares your evidence and witnesses and presents the argument in court. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm.

Totting up bans explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A totting up ban is a driving ban that comes from the build up of penalty points over time, rather than from one serious offence. It often catches people by surprise. A driver with nine points can face a ban of six months or more for a relatively minor offence, such as speeding a few miles per hour over the limit or using a mobile phone, because the new points take them to 12.

The rule is set out in law. If a driver is convicted of an endorsable offence and the points for it, added to other points that count, reach 12 or more, the court must usually disqualify them. The court has very little room to do anything else. The main exception is where the driver can show that a ban would cause exceptional hardship, which is a specific legal argument with its own rules.

Totting up cases are heard in the magistrates' court. Many start as a Single Justice Procedure notice or a postal requisition for the new offence. The court then looks at the driver's record, sees that the new points would take them to 12 or more, and lists the case so that a ban can be considered.

The law treats the build up of points as a sign that a driver presents a risk, even if each offence on its own seemed minor. That is why the court has so little discretion. It is also why the ban is often far more serious, in practical terms, than the fine for the new offence that triggered it.

Who tends to be affected

  • People who drive long distances for work, such as sales staff and engineers
  • Delivery, taxi and private hire drivers
  • Business owners who rely on driving to visit clients or sites
  • Carers who drive relatives to appointments
  • Parents in rural areas with limited public transport
  • Drivers who have picked up camera offences over a short period

Because the consequences can be so significant, many drivers facing a totting up ban look for specialist help before the hearing. Leaflegal does not give legal advice. We introduce you to a regulated motoring solicitor who can check your record and explain what the court is likely to do.

Working out whether totting up applies is not always as simple as adding up the numbers on your licence. The rules about which points count are specific, and mistakes are common.

The examples below describe the general rules. A solicitor will apply them to the exact dates and offences on your record, because small differences in dates can change the outcome.

The three year window

The court counts points from offences that were committed within three years of the date of the new offence. What matters is the date each offence was committed, not the date of conviction or the date you received the points. This means delays in a case do not usually change whether earlier points count.

Points that show but do not count

Most points stay on your driving record for four years, and drink and drug driving related endorsements stay for eleven years. Points can therefore still appear on your record after they have stopped counting for totting up. Equally, points that look old may still count if the offence dates are close enough together.

After a previous totting up ban

If you have already been banned under the totting up rules, the points that led to that ban do not count again towards a future totting up ban. They remain on your record for the usual period, but the slate is cleared for this purpose. A short ban for a single offence does not have the same effect.

Several offences at once

Where a court deals with more than one offence committed on the same occasion, it will usually only add the points for the most serious one, unless it decides otherwise. This can make a difference to the total. A solicitor will look at how your offences were dealt with.

Checking your record

You can see your endorsements, offence dates and any previous bans on the GOV.UK view driving licence service. Having this to hand when you speak to a solicitor lets them work out quickly whether totting up applies.

The law sets a minimum period for a totting up ban. The court can impose a longer ban, but it cannot go below the minimum unless it finds exceptional hardship.

The minimum periods

  • At least six months if you have no previous bans in the relevant period
  • At least one year if you have had one ban of 56 days or more in the three years before the new offence
  • At least two years if you have had two or more such bans in that period

These are minimums, and in most cases the court imposes the minimum unless there are features that make the case more serious. Your solicitor can explain what to expect after looking at your record.

The sentence for the new offence

The ban is not the only part of the sentence. The court still deals with the new offence itself, which usually means a fine, a victim surcharge and prosecution costs. When a totting up ban is imposed, the court does not normally also impose a separate short ban for the same offence.

When the ban starts

A ban usually starts on the day the court imposes it. If the court imposed an interim ban earlier in the case, that time counts towards the total. You must not drive at all during the ban. Driving while disqualified is a serious offence in its own right.

No extended retest

A totting up ban does not normally require you to take a driving test again. When the ban ends you apply to the DVLA to get your licence back. Drivers in their first two years after passing are treated differently, because six points can lead to the DVLA revoking the licence.

Totting up cases usually involve a hearing at a magistrates' court, because the court will not normally ban someone without giving them a chance to be heard.

Getting to court

If your case began with a Single Justice Procedure notice and you plead guilty, the magistrate will see from your record that a ban is likely. You will usually be sent a notice inviting you to attend a hearing before the ban is decided. If your case began with a postal requisition or charge, the date will already be set.

Pleading to the new offence

The first question is whether you plead guilty or not guilty to the new offence. If you plead not guilty and are acquitted, no points are added and totting up does not arise. If you plead guilty or are convicted, the court moves on to sentence, including the question of a ban.

At the sentencing hearing

The prosecutor tells the court about the offence and your record. The court confirms that the points take you to 12 or more. If you are not arguing exceptional hardship, the court will usually impose the minimum ban and deal with the fine. If you are arguing it, you will give evidence and may call witnesses before the court decides.

Adjournments

Sometimes a case is adjourned so that an exceptional hardship argument can be prepared, so that you can attend, or so that the court can get more information. During an adjournment, the court may have power to impose an interim ban. Your solicitor will explain the likely timetable and whether an adjournment is likely.

What the court cannot consider

When deciding whether to ban, the court is not allowed to take into account that the new offence was minor, hardship that is not exceptional, or circumstances it has already accepted as a reason not to ban within the previous three years. This is why the argument against a ban is narrow and needs careful preparation.

Every case is different, and a solicitor will look at your own circumstances before saying what may be possible. In general terms, drivers facing a totting up ban usually find themselves in one of the following positions.

None of these routes is right for everyone, and none guarantees a particular result. A solicitor will want to understand your record, the new offence and your personal circumstances before giving a view.

Contesting the new offence

If there is a genuine issue with the evidence for the new offence, such as who was driving or whether the offence happened at all, the driver can plead not guilty. The court decides at a trial. If the driver is acquitted, no points are added.

Accepting the ban

Some drivers decide that a ban, while difficult, is manageable. They plead guilty and the court imposes the ban. A solicitor can still help by presenting mitigation on the fine and by explaining the practical steps, such as getting the licence back.

Arguing exceptional hardship

A driver who can show that a ban would cause exceptional hardship can ask the court not to impose the ban, or to impose a shorter one. This needs evidence and a well prepared argument. If the court accepts it, the points stay on the licence, and the same reasons cannot be used again within three years.

Checking the record

In some cases the points have been counted wrongly, or an earlier offence falls outside the three year window. A solicitor will check the dates before anything else. This is about making sure the law is applied correctly, not about avoiding the consequences of an offence.

Whatever the position, a solicitor will explain the likely outcome of each route so that you can make an informed choice.

Most people facing a totting up ban pay privately. Legal aid is rarely available for motoring cases, because it depends on a real risk of prison or other special factors.

How firms usually charge

Firms often charge a fixed fee for a straightforward guilty plea and totting up hearing, and a higher fixed fee where an exceptional hardship argument is being run. The higher fee reflects the work involved in gathering evidence, preparing witnesses and attending a longer hearing. A trial on the underlying offence is usually charged separately.

Other costs

It can help to think about the practical costs of a ban alongside the legal fees. Travel, lost work and arrangements for family can add up over six months or more. Knowing this helps you weigh up the options when you talk to the firm.

If you are convicted, the court will usually order a fine, a victim surcharge and prosecution costs. You may also face practical costs during a ban, such as taxis, public transport, or paying someone to drive for you, and higher insurance premiums afterwards.

Totting up bans questions and answers

How long is a totting up ban?
Usually at least six months, longer if you have had a previous ban.
What is exceptional hardship?
Hardship beyond the normal consequences of a ban, often affecting other people such as family, employees or patients.
Can I argue it more than once?
The court can’t accept the same reasons again within three years.
Which areas do you cover?
Motoring offences dealt with in the courts of England and Wales.
Is there legal aid for motoring offences?
Usually not, unless you are at risk of prison. We only introduce people looking for paid legal help.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm aims to contact you quickly, especially if you have a court date coming up. Please give a mobile number you can answer.
What is totting up?
Totting up is the system that leads to a driving ban when you build up 12 or more penalty points from offences committed within three years of each other. The court must usually impose a ban of at least six months. It applies to all drivers, although new drivers face a separate, lower limit of six points in their first two years.
Which points count towards 12?
The court counts points for offences committed within three years of the date of the new offence, together with the points for the new offence itself. It is the offence dates that matter, not when you were convicted. Points that led to an earlier totting up ban do not count again. A solicitor can check your record.
Why do I have points showing that do not count?
Most points stay on your driving record for four years, but only offences within three years of each other count towards totting up. Drink and drug driving related endorsements show for eleven years. So your record may show points that are no longer relevant for totting up purposes.
If my case is delayed, will old points drop off?
Usually not. The three year window is measured between the dates the offences were committed, not the date of the court hearing. A delay in the court process does not normally change which points count. Your solicitor can work out exactly which points apply to the new offence.
What if I get two offences on the same day?
Where offences are committed on the same occasion and dealt with together, the court usually only adds the points for the most serious offence, unless it decides there is a reason not to. Offences on separate occasions are counted separately. A solicitor can look at the dates and how the offences were handled.
How do I check my points?
You can check your record on the GOV.UK view driving licence service using your licence number, National Insurance number and postcode. It shows your endorsements, offence dates and previous bans. It is a good idea to check before replying to a notice or speaking to a solicitor.
Does a speed awareness course add points?
No. A speed awareness course is offered instead of prosecution, so completing it means no conviction and no points. It does not count towards totting up. Whether a course is offered is a decision for the police, and it is not usually available once a case has gone to court or if you have done a similar course recently.
Do points from a fixed penalty count?
Yes. Points accepted through a fixed penalty are endorsed on your licence in the same way as points imposed by a court, and they count towards totting up. That is why a fixed penalty is not usually offered if it would take you to 12 points. In that situation, the case goes to court so that a ban can be considered.
How long is a totting up ban?
The minimum is six months. It rises to at least one year if you have had one ban of 56 days or more in the three years before the new offence, and at least two years if you have had two or more. The court can impose a longer ban, but usually imposes the minimum unless there are aggravating features.
Is a ban automatic once I reach 12 points?
Not automatic, but close to it. The law says the court must disqualify unless there are mitigating circumstances, which in practice means exceptional hardship. The court cannot take into account ordinary hardship or the fact that the new offence was minor. A solicitor can explain whether an exceptional hardship argument might be available.
Do I have to retake my test after a totting up ban?
No, not normally. At the end of the ban you apply to the DVLA to get your licence back. Retests are ordered for some serious offences, and new drivers who reach six points within two years face revocation and must pass again, but a standard totting up ban does not require a new test.
Can I drive while I wait for court?
In most cases, yes. A ban starts on the day the court imposes it. If the case is adjourned, the court may sometimes impose an interim ban before sentence. Until then, you can usually drive as long as your licence is valid and you are insured.
What happens if I drive while banned?
Driving while disqualified is a serious criminal offence. It can lead to a further ban, a community order or a custodial sentence, and your insurance will be invalid. It is treated far more seriously than the offence that led to the ban. Whatever the pressures, the ban must be respected for its whole length.
Will a totting up ban affect my job?
It can, especially if you drive for work or your contract requires a valid licence. Some employers can adjust duties during a ban, others cannot. Your contract or employer's policies will set out what happens. The effect on your job may also be relevant if you are considering an exceptional hardship argument.
Is a totting up ban the same as a ban for drink driving?
No. A drink driving ban is an obligatory ban for that offence and is not about points. A totting up ban comes from reaching 12 points across several offences. The rules for each are different. For example, exceptional hardship is an argument against a totting up ban, while drink driving bans can only be avoided in rare cases through special reasons.
Does the type of vehicle I drive make a difference?
A totting up ban applies to your whole licence, so you cannot drive any vehicle during the ban, including vans, lorries or motorcycles if you hold those categories. Professional drivers with vocational licences may also face separate action from the traffic commissioner. Your solicitor can explain the criminal side, and your employer or licensing body can explain anything else.
Why do I have to go to court?
Courts do not normally ban someone without giving them the chance to attend and be heard. If your case started on paper, you will usually receive a notice inviting you to a hearing once the court sees that a ban is likely. Attending gives you the chance to put forward mitigation or an exceptional hardship argument. A solicitor can attend with you.
What happens at the totting up hearing?
The prosecutor sets out the offence and your record. The court confirms the points take you to 12 or more. If you are arguing exceptional hardship, you give evidence and may call witnesses, and the prosecutor can ask questions. The court then decides whether to ban and for how long, and deals with the fine.
Can I plead not guilty to the new offence?
Yes, if you dispute it. The court will then hold a trial. If you are found not guilty, no points are added and totting up does not apply. If you are convicted, the court goes on to consider a ban. A solicitor will review the evidence and explain the options before you decide how to plead.
Could the court give me a short ban instead?
Where a totting up ban applies, the court will usually deal with the case that way rather than imposing a short ban for the offence itself. The approach is set out in sentencing guidance. A solicitor can explain how the court is likely to treat your offence. The answer depends on the offence, the points it carries and the guidance that applies at the time of sentence.
Can I appeal a totting up ban?
There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court, normally within 21 days of sentence. You can sometimes apply for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is heard. An appeal needs proper grounds, and the Crown Court looks at the case afresh. A solicitor can tell you whether an appeal is realistic.
Do I need to bring anything to court?
It usually helps to bring the court papers, your driving licence details, and any documents that support what you want the court to know, such as letters from an employer. If you are arguing exceptional hardship, your solicitor will tell you what evidence and which witnesses are needed. Bringing details of your income helps the court set the fine.
Do my points disappear after a totting up ban?
The points that led to the ban stay on your driving record for the usual period, but they are cleared for totting up purposes. They will not count towards another totting up ban in future. Points from any later offences start a new total. A solicitor can explain how this works on your own record.
How do I get my licence back?
Near the end of the ban, you apply to the DVLA to get your licence back if it was not returned automatically. Check the DVLA guidance on GOV.UK for the current process. You must not drive until the ban has ended. If you were a new driver whose licence was revoked, the rules are different.
What happens if I was not banned because of exceptional hardship?
You keep your licence, but the points stay on it. That means any further endorsable offence within the relevant period could lead to another totting up ban, and the same reasons cannot be relied on again within three years. Many drivers in this position take particular care for the following years.
Will my insurance be affected?
Insurers usually ask about bans and convictions, and a totting up ban is likely to affect your premiums for several years. Some insurers decline cover after a ban. Answering their questions accurately is important, because failing to disclose can affect a claim.
Can I get a ban reduced partway through?
For some longer bans, a driver can apply to the court to have the ban removed early after a set part of it has passed. This does not usually apply to shorter bans, such as a six month totting up ban. The rules depend on the length of the ban. A solicitor can explain whether an application might be possible in your case.
How much does help with a totting up case cost?
Many firms charge a fixed fee for a guilty plea and totting up hearing, and a higher fee where an exceptional hardship argument is needed. A trial on the new offence is usually separate. Fees vary, so ask the firm what is included and get it confirmed in writing before work starts.

What should I have ready?
Have your driving record, the papers for the new offence, your court date, and any notices about previous bans. Think about who would be affected by a ban and how, such as an employer, staff, family members or people you care for. The solicitor will tell you what further evidence might be useful. Bring details of any earlier hardship arguments too, because the court will ask about them.
Is legal aid available for totting up cases?
Legal aid is rarely available for totting up cases, because it is usually only granted where there is a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately. If the solicitor thinks your case might qualify for legal aid, they can explain how to apply.
How quickly will a firm contact me?
Firms aim to contact you quickly, usually within one working day and often sooner. Totting up cases often have a court date already set, so mention it in your enquiry. An exceptional hardship argument needs time to prepare, so early contact gives the solicitor more room to gather the evidence the court will expect.
What is exceptional hardship?
It is an argument a driver can make to a court when facing a totting up ban for reaching 12 or more points. The driver asks the court not to ban them, or to impose a shorter ban, because a ban would cause hardship beyond the normal consequences of losing a licence. The driver must prove it with evidence.
What is the difference between hardship and exceptional hardship?
Most people who are banned suffer some hardship, such as difficulty getting to work or higher travel costs. The law treats that as normal. Exceptional hardship goes further. Courts often look for serious consequences, particularly for other people who rely on the driver, and for evidence that there is no realistic way to manage without driving.
Can exceptional hardship stop a drink driving ban?
No. Exceptional hardship only applies to totting up bans. A drink driving ban is obligatory, and the court can only decide not to ban, or to shorten the ban, if it finds special reasons connected to the offence itself, such as a genuine emergency. A solicitor can explain the difference and whether either applies.
Does it help new drivers?
Not with revocation. If you passed your test within the last two years and reach six points, the DVLA revokes your licence automatically, and an exceptional hardship argument cannot stop that. Exceptional hardship can still be relevant if a new driver also faces a totting up ban, which is less common. A solicitor can explain how the rules interact.
Who has to prove exceptional hardship?
The driver does. The court must be satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that a ban would cause exceptional hardship. That means it is more likely than not. The court will usually expect evidence from the driver and supporting documents, and sometimes evidence from other people who would be affected.
Does it always work?
No. Some arguments succeed and some do not. The outcome depends on the circumstances, the quality of the evidence and how the court sees the case. A solicitor can give a realistic view of the strengths and weaknesses of your circumstances, but no one can guarantee a result.
Can a business owner argue exceptional hardship?
Yes. Business owners often argue that a ban would affect the business and the people who work for it. The court will want evidence, such as accounts, contracts and details of staff, and will look at whether someone else could drive or whether the business could adapt for the period of the ban. The effect on employees can carry particular weight.
Can I argue it if I only have a short time before court?
It may still be possible, but less time means less opportunity to gather documents and arrange witnesses. If the evidence is not ready, a solicitor may ask the court to adjourn, which the court can agree to or refuse. Mentioning your hearing date as soon as you get in touch helps the firm judge what can realistically be done.
