Dangerous driving explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Dangerous driving is one of the most serious driving offences that does not involve someone being killed or injured. It is set out in section 2 of the Road Traffic Act 1988. A conviction brings a compulsory driving ban and a compulsory extended retest, and the court can impose a prison sentence.

Allegations of dangerous driving come from many different situations. Some follow a police pursuit. Some arise from racing on public roads, very high speeds, overtaking into oncoming traffic or driving while badly distracted by a phone. Others involve a vehicle that was in a dangerous condition, such as an overloaded van, a trailer that was not properly secured or a car with serious defects the driver should have known about.

Because the stakes are high, these cases are often defended with care from the very beginning. The police investigation may involve collision investigators, examination of the vehicle, phone downloads and footage from several sources. The way the case is handled at the police station and in the weeks that follow can matter a great deal.

Dangerous driving is also an offence where the person charged often feels strongly that the police account is unfair or exaggerated. A short clip of footage can look very different once the full recording, the road layout and the actions of other drivers are taken into account. A solicitor will want to see all of the material, not just the part the prosecution relies on.

When people usually get in touch

  • After being arrested or asked to attend a police interview
  • After being released under investigation or on bail
  • When a postal requisition or charge arrives
  • Before a first hearing at the magistrates' court
  • When a case has been sent to the Crown Court
  • When the driver accepts some fault but believes the driving was careless rather than dangerous

The legal test for dangerous driving is different from the test for careless driving, and understanding the difference helps explain how these cases are argued.

Far below the standard

A person drives dangerously if the way they drive falls far below what would be expected of a competent and careful driver, and it would be obvious to a competent and careful driver that driving in that way would be dangerous. Danger here means a risk of injury to people or serious damage to property. The test looks at the driving objectively. It does not depend on what the driver thought or intended.

Dangerous condition of the vehicle

A person can also drive dangerously if it would be obvious to a competent and careful driver that driving the vehicle in its current state would be dangerous. This covers things like a load that could fall off, badly worn tyres or brakes that clearly do not work. The court looks at what the driver knew or should obviously have noticed.

The line with careless driving

The gap between driving below the standard and driving far below it is where many dangerous driving cases are fought. The same piece of driving can be described in very different ways. Whether a court sees it as dangerous or careless can depend on speed, road conditions, visibility, the length of time the driving lasted and who else was around.

More serious related offences

Where dangerous driving causes death or serious injury, separate offences apply. These are heard in the Crown Court and carry much longer maximum sentences. If anyone was hurt in your case, tell the firm at the outset, because the investigation and the advice you need will be different.

Other charges

Dangerous driving charges often come with others from the same incident. Examples include failing to stop for police, failing to stop after an accident, driving while disqualified, driving without insurance or drink and drug driving. A solicitor will look at all the allegations together.

What the court takes into account

When deciding whether driving was dangerous, the court looks at all the circumstances. That can include the speed compared with the limit and the conditions, the volume of traffic, whether there were pedestrians or cyclists about, visibility, the condition of the road and how long the driving lasted. A brief misjudgement is treated differently from a sustained course of bad driving, although even a short moment can be dangerous in the wrong place.

What happens before any charge can shape the whole case. Many people facing a dangerous driving allegation first meet the police at the roadside or after being traced through their number plate.

Arrest or voluntary interview

Some drivers are arrested and interviewed while in custody. Others are invited to attend a voluntary interview at a later date. In both cases the interview is under caution, which means it is recorded and can be used in evidence.

Free legal advice

Anyone being interviewed by the police has the right to free and independent legal advice. This can be from the duty solicitor or from a solicitor of your choice. The advice is free whatever your income. A solicitor can find out what the police say happened before the interview starts and advise on how to approach it.

Investigation

The police may examine the vehicle, gather footage from dashcams, CCTV and police cameras, take statements and download data from phones or the car itself. In serious cases a collision investigator may reconstruct what happened. This can take weeks or months.

Bail or release under investigation

After interview, the police may charge you, release you on bail with conditions, or release you under investigation while enquiries continue. The Crown Prosecution Service decides whether to charge in most serious cases. Your solicitor can keep in touch with the police during this time.

The charge

If you are charged, you will be given a date for a first hearing at the magistrates' court. Some cases instead begin with a postal requisition. Either way, the time between charge and the first hearing is a useful window to get advice and start gathering evidence.

Dangerous driving is an either way offence. That means it can be heard in the magistrates' court or the Crown Court. The steps below give a general picture of how cases usually move through the system.

1. First hearing

Every case starts in the magistrates' court. You are asked to indicate a plea. The prosecution should provide initial details of its case beforehand so that you and your solicitor can see the main evidence.

2. Allocation

If you indicate a not guilty plea, the magistrates decide whether the case is suitable for them to hear or should go to the Crown Court. If they agree to keep it, you can still choose to have a trial by jury in the Crown Court. If you plead guilty, the magistrates can sentence you or send you to the Crown Court for sentence if they think their own powers are not enough.

3. Crown Court preparation

Cases sent to the Crown Court have a plea and trial preparation hearing, where you enter a formal plea and the judge sets a timetable. The prosecution then serves its full evidence, and the defence sets out the issues in dispute. Expert evidence, such as a defence collision report, is often prepared at this stage.

4. Trial

In the Crown Court, a jury decides whether you are guilty and the judge deals with the law and the sentence. In the magistrates' court, magistrates or a district judge decide both. Witnesses are called and can be cross examined, and you can give evidence yourself.

5. Alternative verdict

If the court is not sure the driving was dangerous but is sure it was careless, it can convict of careless driving instead. Sometimes the prosecution will accept a guilty plea to careless driving. Whether that is realistic depends entirely on the evidence.

6. Sentence

Before sentence, the court may ask the probation service for a pre-sentence report. Your solicitor or barrister then presents mitigation. The court follows the sentencing guidelines for dangerous driving.

Bail during proceedings

Most people charged with dangerous driving are released on bail while the case is ongoing. The court can attach conditions, such as living at a particular address or, in some cases, not driving. Breaking bail conditions is taken seriously, so make sure you understand any conditions that apply to you.

Sentences for dangerous driving vary widely because the offence covers a wide range of driving. The court considers how dangerous the driving was, how long it lasted, any harm caused and your personal circumstances.

Disqualification

A conviction for dangerous driving carries a compulsory ban of at least 12 months, and often longer. The court can only avoid a ban if it finds special reasons, which are rare and relate to the offence rather than to you personally. If a prison sentence is imposed, the ban is extended so that it does not simply run out while you are in custody.

Extended retest

After the ban ends you must pass an extended driving test before you can hold a full licence again. The extended test is longer and more demanding than the standard practical test.

Custody and community orders

The court can impose a prison sentence of up to two years. It can also suspend a prison sentence, or make a community order with requirements such as unpaid work, a curfew or a rehabilitation activity. The sentencing guidelines help the court decide which type of sentence fits the case.

Fines and other orders

The court can impose a fine as well as or instead of other penalties, and will usually order you to pay prosecution costs and a victim surcharge. In some cases it can order that the vehicle used is taken away from you.

Credit for a guilty plea

A guilty plea usually reduces the sentence. The biggest reduction is given for a plea at the first opportunity. This reduction applies to prison sentences and fines, but it cannot reduce the length of the compulsory minimum ban.

Personal mitigation

Personal mitigation is the information about you that the court weighs alongside the facts of the offence. It can include a clean driving record, good character, genuine remorse, steps you have taken since the incident, health issues and responsibilities for others. Your solicitor or barrister will gather this material and present it in a way the court can rely on, such as references and documents.

Dangerous driving cases can involve a lot of work, so it is worth understanding how funding works before you start.

Legal aid

Advice at the police station is free for everyone. For court proceedings, legal aid depends on a means test and on whether the interests of justice require it. Because dangerous driving can lead to prison, the interests of justice test is more likely to be met than in less serious motoring cases. In the Crown Court you may be asked to contribute towards the cost depending on your income and savings.

Private fees

Some people are not eligible for legal aid, and others choose to pay privately so that they can instruct a particular firm. Private fees often depend on the stage the case reaches. A guilty plea in the magistrates' court will cost much less than a contested trial in the Crown Court with expert evidence and a barrister.

Experts and barristers

Many dangerous driving cases need a collision investigator, a vehicle examiner or another expert. Crown Court cases usually involve a barrister for hearings and trial. Ask the firm how these costs are handled and whether they are included in any quote.

If you are acquitted

If you pay privately and are found not guilty, you may be able to recover some of your costs from public funds. The amount is usually limited to set rates and may not cover everything you paid.

Getting a clear quote

Ask the firm to set out in writing what each stage will cost, what is included and what would be extra. Ask too what happens to the fee if the case changes direction, for example if the prosecution accepts a plea to careless driving or the case moves from the magistrates' court to the Crown Court.

Dangerous driving questions and answers

Is dangerous driving a serious charge?
Yes. It carries a ban, an extended retest and possibly prison.
Can the charge be reduced?
Sometimes a court accepts careless driving instead. A solicitor will advise.
Will I need a barrister?
Crown Court cases often involve a barrister as well as your solicitor.
Which areas do you cover?
Motoring offences dealt with in the courts of England and Wales.
Is there legal aid for motoring offences?
Usually not, unless you are at risk of prison. We only introduce people looking for paid legal help.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm aims to contact you quickly, especially if you have a court date coming up. Please give a mobile number you can answer.
What is the difference between dangerous and careless driving?
Careless driving is driving below the standard of a competent and careful driver. Dangerous driving is driving far below that standard, where it would be obvious to a careful driver that the driving was dangerous. The difference is one of degree, and many contested cases turn on which side of that line the driving falls. The penalties for dangerous driving are much heavier.
Can I be convicted if I did not think I was driving dangerously?
Yes. The test is objective. It asks what would have been obvious to a competent and careful driver, not what you believed at the time. However, the circumstances you were in, such as the road, the weather and what other drivers were doing, are part of the picture the court looks at.
Can a vehicle defect lead to a dangerous driving charge?
It can. If it would have been obvious to a competent and careful driver that driving the vehicle in its condition was dangerous, that can amount to dangerous driving. Examples include an insecure load or serious defects with brakes or tyres. If the defect was hidden and not something you could reasonably have known about, that is likely to be important.
Is a police chase always dangerous driving?
Not automatically. The prosecution must still prove that the driving itself fell far below the expected standard. Failing to stop for the police is a separate offence. In practice, driving during a pursuit is often closely examined using police camera footage, and the court looks at the speed, the roads and the risk to other people.
Can the charge be reduced to careless driving?
Sometimes. The court can convict of careless driving as an alternative if it is not sure the driving was dangerous. The prosecution may also accept a plea to careless driving in some cases. Whether this is realistic depends on the evidence, and a solicitor can tell you once they have reviewed it.
What if someone was injured?
If dangerous driving causes serious injury or death, different and more serious offences apply. These are always heard in the Crown Court and carry much longer sentences. Tell the firm straight away if anyone was hurt, even if the police have not yet said what charge they are considering.
Do I have to receive a warning before being prosecuted?
For dangerous driving, the police usually have to warn the driver that a prosecution is being considered, either at the scene or by a notice of intended prosecution sent within 14 days. There is an exception where an accident happened that the driver was aware of. Whether the rules were followed depends on the facts, and a solicitor can check this when reviewing the case.
Should I have a solicitor at my police interview?
Leaflegal cannot advise you on that. What we can say is that everyone interviewed under caution has a right to free legal advice, whatever their income. A solicitor can find out what the police allege before the interview and talk through how to approach it. The firm we introduce you to can explain this in more detail.
What does released under investigation mean?
It means the police have released you without charge and without bail conditions while they continue their enquiries. There is no fixed date for a decision. You may later be charged, interviewed again or told no further action will be taken. Your solicitor can contact the officer in the case for updates.
Will my case go to the Crown Court?
Not necessarily. Dangerous driving can be heard in either court. The magistrates decide whether the case is suitable for them, and you can choose a jury trial in the Crown Court if you plead not guilty. Magistrates can also send a case to the Crown Court for sentence. A solicitor will explain the pros and cons of each court for your case.
What happens at the first hearing?
The first hearing takes place at the magistrates' court. You confirm your details and indicate whether you intend to plead guilty or not guilty. The court then decides where the case should be heard. If the case stays in the magistrates' court and you plead guilty, sentence may happen that day or later after a report.
Will I need a barrister?
Crown Court cases usually involve a barrister, who represents you at hearings and trial, working with your solicitor. Some solicitors have higher rights of audience and can appear in the Crown Court themselves. In the magistrates' court, a solicitor normally represents you. Your firm will explain who will be doing what.
How long will the case take?
It depends on the length of the police investigation, whether you plead guilty, which court hears the case and how busy that court is. A guilty plea in the magistrates' court may be dealt with fairly quickly. Crown Court trials can take many months to come to court. Your solicitor can give you an idea once the case is underway.
Can I be banned before the case finishes?
In some circumstances the court can impose an interim disqualification, for example when a case is sent to the Crown Court or adjourned before sentence. If this happens, the time spent banned is usually taken into account when the final ban is decided. Your solicitor can explain whether this is likely to apply.
Will I definitely be banned?
A conviction for dangerous driving carries a compulsory ban of at least 12 months unless the court finds special reasons. Special reasons are rare and must relate to the circumstances of the offence, not to the effect a ban would have on you. In practice, most people convicted of dangerous driving should expect a ban, often longer than the minimum.
What is the extended retest?
After a dangerous driving ban ends, you must pass an extended driving test to get your full licence back. You apply for a provisional licence and take the test, which is longer than the standard practical test. Until you pass, you can only drive under the conditions that apply to learner drivers.
Could I go to prison?
The maximum sentence is two years in prison. Whether custody is likely depends on how serious the driving was, any harm caused, your record and your personal circumstances. Many cases end with a suspended sentence or community order. A solicitor can explain where your case may fall under the sentencing guidelines once they have seen the evidence.
What is a pre-sentence report?
It is a report prepared by the probation service to help the court decide the right sentence. A probation officer speaks to you about the offence, your background and your circumstances, and suggests what kind of sentence might be suitable. The court does not have to follow the suggestion, but it often carries weight.
Does pleading guilty make a difference?
A guilty plea usually leads to a reduced sentence, with the largest reduction for a plea at the first stage of proceedings. The reduction applies to prison terms and fines but cannot cut a compulsory ban below its minimum. Whether to plead guilty is your decision, made after advice from your solicitor on the strength of the evidence.
Can they take my car?
The court has the power to deprive an offender of property used to commit an offence, which can include a vehicle. This is not automatic and the court considers the effect on you and others, such as family members who rely on the car or a finance company that owns it. Your solicitor can address this at sentence.
How long will the conviction affect my licence?
A dangerous driving conviction usually stays on your driving record for four years from the date of conviction. You will need to declare it to insurers, who may ask about convictions for longer than that. Premiums after a dangerous driving conviction are often much higher, and some insurers may decline cover. A specialist broker may be able to help once you are able to drive again.
Can I get legal aid?
Possibly. Police station advice is free for everyone. For court, legal aid depends on a means test and an interests of justice test. Because dangerous driving can lead to prison, the interests of justice test is often met. In the Crown Court you may have to pay a contribution depending on your finances. The firm can check your eligibility.
How much does a private defence cost?
It depends on the court, whether you plead guilty and whether experts and a barrister are needed. Many firms quote by stage so you know the cost of each part. A contested Crown Court trial is significantly more expensive than a magistrates' court plea. Ask for a written estimate and what would change it.
Who pays for expert reports?
If you are legally aided, expert fees can often be covered with the approval of the Legal Aid Agency. If you are paying privately, expert fees are usually an extra cost on top of the solicitor's fee. Your solicitor will explain why an expert is needed and what it is likely to involve before instructing one.
Can I get my costs back if I win?
If you are acquitted after paying privately, you may be able to recover some of your legal costs from public funds through a defendant's costs order. The amount is usually limited to legal aid rates, so it may not cover everything you paid. Your solicitor can explain what you might be able to claim.
What should I gather for my solicitor?
Bring your charge sheet or requisition, any bail papers, letters from the police and any dashcam footage or photographs you have. Write down your own account while it is fresh, including the route, speed, weather and anyone you remember seeing. Your solicitor will tell you what else may be useful.
Should I post about the incident online?
Anything you post can be found and used as evidence. People sometimes comment on social media without realising it may later be put to them in court. Leaflegal cannot advise you, but a solicitor can explain the risks of discussing the case with anyone other than your legal team.
Will my employer find out?
The police do not usually contact your employer, but court hearings are public and a ban may affect your ability to work. Some jobs require you to report a charge or conviction. Your solicitor can help you think about how the possible outcomes affect your work and what evidence about your job may help at sentence.
Is Leaflegal a law firm?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service. We do not give legal advice and we are not a law firm. We introduce you to one regulated firm that handles serious motoring cases, and any advice comes from them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.
Can the solicitor help if I have already been charged?
Yes. Many people first look for a solicitor after charge or once a court date arrives. The firm will ask for your paperwork, review the initial details of the prosecution case and talk you through the next hearing. The sooner they are involved, the more time there is to secure evidence and prepare, but it is rarely too late to get advice.
What is a section 172 notice?
It is a formal request from the police asking the registered keeper of a vehicle to say who was driving at a particular time and place. It is named after section 172 of the Road Traffic Act 1988. It often arrives with a notice of intended prosecution for an offence such as speeding. You are legally required to answer it.
How long do I have to respond?
The information must normally be given within 28 days of the notice being served. The notice will explain how to respond and what details are needed. If the deadline has passed or is close, a solicitor can explain what this means for you. Late responses are sometimes accepted, but there is no guarantee.
Can I respond online?
Many police forces let you respond online, and the notice will usually give the web address and a reference number. Follow the instructions carefully and keep a copy or screenshot of your confirmation. If you respond by post, it is sensible to keep a copy of the form and proof of posting.
What if the car is shared by several people?
Sharing a car does not remove the duty to name the driver. The keeper is expected to find out who was driving, for example by checking diaries, fuel receipts and asking everyone who used the car. If, after real effort, you cannot find out, a solicitor can explain whether the reasonable diligence defence might apply.
I sold the car before the date on the notice. What now?
If you were no longer the keeper on the date of the alleged offence, you can usually say so in your response and give details of the buyer. Evidence such as the sale receipt and confirmation that you told the DVLA will help. If the DVLA record was not updated, a solicitor can explain how this affects your position.
What if the car was stolen or cloned?
If you believe the car was stolen at the time, or that your registration has been copied onto another vehicle, explain this in your response and provide whatever evidence you have, such as a crime reference number or photographs. A solicitor can help you present this clearly if the police do not accept it.
Do I have to sign the form?
The notice will tell you how to respond, which may include signing a declaration or completing an online submission. Follow those instructions exactly. If you are unsure whether a response you sent was valid, or the police say it was incomplete, a solicitor can look at what you sent.
I lent my car to a friend. What do I need to do?
As the keeper, you are expected to give the name and address of the person who was driving. If you lent the car to a friend, that usually means naming them. The police may then send them their own notice. If you are unsure who had the car at the time, think about what records or messages might help you find out, and speak to a solicitor if you are still unsure.
