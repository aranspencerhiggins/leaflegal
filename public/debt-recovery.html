Debt recovery explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Debt recovery is the work of getting money back from someone who owes it to you and has not paid. On Leaflegal, debt recovery means one thing only. It is about a business that is owed money by another business, in England and Wales. That might be a limited company, a limited liability partnership, a partnership or a sole trader who bought goods or services from you in the course of their own business.

We do not introduce anyone collecting debts from consumers. If the person who owes you money bought something for their own personal use, such as a householder paying for a new kitchen, that is consumer debt and it is outside what we do. Consumer debt collection has its own rules and regulators, and it is not part of this service.

Late payment is one of the most common problems a business faces. A single unpaid invoice can tie up cash that is needed for wages, suppliers and tax. A few unpaid invoices at once can put a healthy business under real strain. Most business owners would rather keep a good relationship with a customer, but there comes a point where a firmer approach is needed.

A debt recovery solicitor helps a business move from polite reminders to formal steps. Those steps usually build on each other, from a formal letter, to a court claim, to enforcing a judgment, or in some cases to insolvency routes such as a statutory demand or a winding up petition. Each step has its own rules, costs and risks.

Debts between businesses tend to fall into a few familiar patterns. Some customers are simply slow and pay eventually once pressed. Some are short of cash and want time. Some raise a complaint about the goods or work, sometimes genuine and sometimes as a reason to delay. A smaller number have no intention of paying at all. The right approach depends a great deal on which of these you are dealing with, and an experienced solicitor will usually spot the pattern quickly.

Debt recovery covers several related matters. Some businesses need help with only one of them. Others move through several in turn as a debt progresses. These are the matters we introduce for.

Unpaid invoices

This is where most debts start. A customer has not paid an invoice, or has paid part of it, and reminders have not worked. A solicitor will check what is owed under the contract and suggest the most sensible route to payment.

Letters before action

A formal letter from a solicitor sets out what is owed and warns that court proceedings will follow if the debt is not paid. It is often the first formal step, and courts expect to see that the debtor has been given a fair chance to pay before a claim is issued.

Court claims

If the letter does not produce payment, a solicitor can issue a claim. Many debt claims are not defended, and the creditor can ask for judgment without a hearing. Defended claims go through the full court process.

Enforcement

A judgment is an order to pay, but it does not guarantee that the money arrives. Enforcement covers the tools available to turn a judgment into cash, such as enforcement agents, charging orders over property and orders that freeze bank accounts.

Statutory demands and winding up petitions

These are insolvency tools. A statutory demand is a formal demand for an undisputed debt. If a company does not pay, the creditor may be able to petition the court to wind it up. These are powerful steps that should only be used in the right cases.

Late payment interest and compensation

Businesses owed money by other businesses can often add interest and a fixed sum of compensation to late invoices, either under their contract or under late payment law. A solicitor will work out what can be claimed and add it to the demand.

How the matters fit together

Not every debt goes through every stage. Many are settled after a letter. Some go straight from a letter to a claim and are paid once judgment is entered. Others need enforcement, and a few are better suited to an insolvency route from the outset. A solicitor will explain which path makes most sense for the amount owed, the strength of your evidence and what is known about the debtor's finances.

If you are not sure which of these matters fits your situation, that is fine. Choose the one that seems closest when you fill in the form, and the firm will help you work out the right starting point.

Debt recovery is a practical area of law. What most businesses want is a firm that acts quickly, explains costs clearly and keeps them updated without fuss. Here are some things to think about when you speak to a firm.

Commercial experience

Business debts are different from personal debts. A firm that regularly acts for businesses will understand contract terms, retention of title clauses, personal guarantees and the insolvency tools that only apply to companies. Every firm we introduce handles commercial debt recovery.

Clear pricing

Many firms offer fixed fees for early steps such as a letter before action or an undefended claim. Ask what is included, what would cost more, and whether court fees are included. Ask how the firm charges if the debt is disputed, because defended work usually costs more.

Speed and communication

Time matters in debt recovery. The longer a debt sits, the harder it can be to recover, especially if the debtor is in financial difficulty. Ask how quickly the firm can send a letter, and how often it will update you.

Volume and repeat work

If your business often has slow payers, ask whether the firm offers a service for ongoing debts. Some firms have online portals for sending new debts and tracking progress. Others offer credit control support or will review your terms and conditions.

Honest advice about value

A good solicitor will tell you when a debt is not worth chasing, for example because the debtor has no assets or is already insolvent. That advice can save you money. Be wary of anyone who promises recovery, because no firm can guarantee that a debtor will pay.

Checking the debtor first

Ask whether the firm can carry out basic checks on the debtor before you spend money on a claim. Public records at Companies House, the Register of Judgments and the Gazette can show whether a company is already in trouble, has other unpaid judgments against it or has been struck off. A few minutes of checking can change the whole plan.

Remember that you are choosing someone to act for your business, possibly against a customer you have dealt with for years. It helps to feel comfortable with how the firm will speak to that customer on your behalf, and to agree at the start how firm or how conciliatory you want the tone to be.

The cost of recovering a business debt depends on how far the matter goes. A debt paid after a solicitor's letter costs far less than one that goes to a defended trial. There are usually three kinds of cost to think about.

It is sensible to agree a budget with the firm at the outset and to review it at each stage. Before moving from a letter to a claim, or from a claim to enforcement, a good solicitor will explain the likely extra cost against the realistic chance of getting paid, so that you can make an informed decision about whether to carry on.

The firm's fees

Many firms charge fixed fees for straightforward steps such as a letter before action, issuing a claim and requesting judgment. Defended claims, insolvency work and complex enforcement are more often charged by the hour or in fixed stages. Some firms offer other arrangements for certain debts. Ask the firm to explain its pricing in writing before any work starts.

Court and other fees

Issuing a claim, applying for judgment, enforcing a judgment and presenting a winding up petition all carry court fees. Winding up petitions also need a deposit for the Official Receiver. These fees are set by the government and change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask the firm for the current figures.

What you might get back

In many debt claims, the winning side can recover some of its costs from the other side. For lower value claims, the costs that can be recovered are usually limited to fixed amounts, so you may not get all of your legal spend back. Between businesses, late payment law often allows a creditor to add interest, a fixed sum of compensation and in some cases reasonable recovery costs. A solicitor will explain what may be recoverable in your case.

How long it takes to recover a business debt varies a great deal. Some debts are paid within days of a solicitor's letter. Others take many months, especially if the debtor defends the claim or has no obvious assets. These are typical stages, but your solicitor will explain what to expect in your case.

Letter before action

A letter usually gives the debtor a set period to pay or respond. For a company or other business debtor this is often around 14 days. Where the debtor is an individual, including a sole trader, a specific pre-action protocol applies and usually gives them 30 days.

Court claim

Once a claim is served, the debtor has a short period to respond. If they do nothing, the creditor can usually ask for judgment in default without a hearing. If they defend, the case is allocated to a track and timetabled, and a defended claim can take many months to reach a final hearing.

Enforcement

After judgment, the debtor is usually given time to pay. If they do not, enforcement can begin. Some methods work quickly, while others, such as charging orders, can take longer but give security over property.

Limitation

In England and Wales, a claim for an unpaid contract debt must usually be started within six years of the date the money became due. The time can restart if the debtor acknowledges the debt in writing or makes a payment. Do not leave a debt too long, as evidence and the debtor's assets can disappear well before the deadline.

Insolvency routes

A statutory demand gives a company 21 days to pay or secure the debt before a winding up petition can usually be presented. The petition is then served, advertised and heard by the court. The whole process takes several weeks at the least, though many debtors pay well before the hearing date.

Having the right papers to hand makes the first conversation with a solicitor much quicker and helps them give you a clear view. You do not need everything, but the more you have, the better.

  • The full legal name and registered number of the debtor, if it is a company
  • The debtor's trading address and registered office
  • Copies of the unpaid invoices
  • Your contract, order confirmation or signed terms and conditions
  • Evidence that the goods were delivered or the work was done
  • Emails, letters and notes of calls about the debt
  • Any complaints the debtor has raised about the goods or work
  • A statement of account showing what has been paid and what is outstanding
  • Any personal guarantee signed by a director
  • Details of any assets you know the debtor owns

Check who your customer really is

A common problem is invoicing the wrong entity. A business might trade under one name but contract through a company with another name. Checking the details on Companies House early helps make sure any claim is brought against the right party.

Be ready to talk about disputes

If the debtor has complained about the quality of the goods or work, tell the solicitor straight away. A genuine dispute changes which routes are open and how much a recovery might cost. It is far better for the solicitor to know at the start.

Keep a simple timeline

A short note of key dates is very useful. Include when the order was placed, when the goods were delivered or the work finished, when each invoice was sent and when it fell due, and when you chased. This helps the solicitor work out interest, check the limitation period and draft the letter accurately.

Is a debt recovery solicitor better than a debt collection agency?
A debt collection agency can chase payment, but only a solicitor can issue court proceedings for you, advise on the strength of your claim and take steps such as a statutory demand or a winding up petition. Many businesses go straight to a debt recovery solicitor for larger or disputed debts.

What counts as a business debtor?
A business debtor is any organisation or person who bought goods or services from you for the purposes of their own business. That includes limited companies, limited liability partnerships, partnerships and sole traders acting in the course of their trade. A solicitor will check the status of your debtor, because some routes, such as winding up, only apply to companies.

Can you help if the debtor is in Scotland or Northern Ireland?
This service covers debts pursued under the law of England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems and court procedures. If your debtor is based outside England and Wales, mention it on the form, and the firm will tell you whether they can help or whether you need a firm elsewhere.
When should I involve a solicitor?
Many businesses involve a solicitor once their own reminders have not worked and the debt is clearly overdue. Waiting too long can make recovery harder, especially if the debtor is struggling. A solicitor can explain the options early and help you decide whether a formal letter is the right next step.
Is my debt too small to chase?
There is no fixed minimum, but the cost of recovery has to make sense against the amount owed. For smaller debts, a fixed fee letter or a straightforward claim may be proportionate. A solicitor will give you an honest view of whether it is worth pursuing, taking into account interest and compensation that may be added.
What if the debtor says the work was faulty?
A genuine dispute about the quality of the goods or work changes things. Some routes, such as a statutory demand, are not suitable for disputed debts. A solicitor will look at the complaint, your contract and the evidence, and explain which options remain open and what the risks are.
What if I don't have a written contract?
A contract does not have to be written to be binding. Orders by email, purchase orders, quotes that were accepted and a history of dealings can all show what was agreed. A solicitor will look at whatever records you have and explain how they support your claim.
Can a solicitor help with several debts at once?
Yes. Many firms handle multiple debts for the same business, and some offer a service designed for regular debt recovery with online tracking. If you have several overdue accounts, mention this when you enquire so the firm can explain its options.
What if the debtor has stopped trading?
If the debtor has closed down, entered administration or gone into liquidation, the options change. You may need to submit a claim to the insolvency practitioner rather than sue. A solicitor can check the debtor's status and explain what is realistic.
What is a personal guarantee?
A personal guarantee is a promise, usually by a director, to pay the company's debts if the company does not. If your customer signed one when opening a credit account, it may give you a second person to pursue. A solicitor will check whether the guarantee was properly signed and what it covers before relying on it.
What is retention of title?
A retention of title clause in your terms says that goods remain yours until they are paid for. If it was properly included in the contract, it may let you recover unpaid goods that the debtor still holds, which can be valuable if the debtor becomes insolvent. A solicitor will check whether your clause is likely to work.
What is a letter before action?
It is a formal letter that sets out what is owed and why, and warns that court proceedings will follow if the debt is not paid by a deadline. Courts expect parties to try to resolve matters before issuing a claim, and many debts are paid once a solicitor's letter arrives.
How does a court claim work?
The solicitor issues a claim, usually online, setting out the debt, interest and costs. The debtor has a short period to respond. If they do not respond, judgment can usually be entered without a hearing. If they defend, the court sets a timetable and the case may go to a hearing.
What happens after judgment?
The debtor is ordered to pay, usually within a set time. If they do not, the creditor can use enforcement tools such as enforcement agents, charging orders, third party debt orders or an order requiring the debtor to give information about its finances. A solicitor will suggest the method most likely to work.
What is a statutory demand?
A statutory demand is a formal demand for payment of an undisputed debt, made under insolvency law. If a company owes more than £750 and does not pay or secure the debt within 21 days, the creditor may be able to petition to wind it up. It should only be used where the debt is clear.
What is a winding up petition?
It is an application to the court to put a company into compulsory liquidation because it cannot pay its debts. It is a serious step that affects all of the company's creditors. A solicitor will explain whether it is suitable for your debt and what it involves.
How long do I have to bring a claim?
In England and Wales, a claim for a contract debt must usually be started within six years of the date payment fell due. Written acknowledgement of the debt or a part payment can restart the clock. It is still best to act long before the deadline, while evidence and assets are available.
How much does debt recovery cost?
It depends on how far the matter goes. Many firms charge fixed fees for letters and undefended claims, and charge by the hour or in stages for defended or complex work. Court fees are extra and change from time to time. Ask the firm for a written quote before work starts.
Can I get my legal costs back?
Some costs can usually be recovered from the debtor if you win, but for many debt claims the recoverable amount is limited to fixed sums. Between businesses, late payment law may also allow you to claim a fixed sum of compensation and reasonable recovery costs. A solicitor will explain what may be recoverable.
Can I add interest to the debt?
Often, yes. Your contract may set a rate of interest for late payment. If it does not, businesses owed money by other businesses can usually claim statutory interest under late payment law. A solicitor will check your terms and calculate the interest for you.
What if the debtor cannot pay?
A judgment is only worth something if the debtor has money or assets. A solicitor can carry out checks before you spend money on a claim and explain whether recovery looks realistic. Sometimes a payment plan or a negotiated settlement is the most practical outcome.

Should I check the debtor before suing?
It is usually wise. Checks on Companies House, the Register of Judgments and the Gazette can show whether a debtor is already in difficulty or has other unpaid judgments. If a company has no assets or is about to enter insolvency, a claim may not be worth the cost. A solicitor can carry out or arrange these checks.
What will the solicitor ask me?
Expect questions about who the debtor is, what was supplied, what the contract says about payment, how much is owed and whether there is any dispute. Having your invoices, contract and correspondence ready will help the solicitor give you a clear picture quickly.
Will chasing the debt damage the relationship?
It can, but a well judged approach often keeps the door open. Many solicitors begin with a firm but professional letter and are happy to discuss payment plans. Your solicitor can explain how to balance getting paid with the commercial relationship you want to keep.
Can the solicitor review my terms and conditions?
Many firms offer this. Good terms can make future debts easier to recover, for example by setting clear payment dates, interest for late payment and retention of title. Ask the firm whether it offers a review and how it charges for it.

More questions people ask

Do I need a court judgment first?
No. A statutory demand can be served without first obtaining a judgment, which is one reason some creditors use it. However, it is only suitable for debts that are clearly due and not genuinely disputed. If there is any real dispute, a court claim is usually the safer route.
What is the minimum debt for a statutory demand?
For a winding up petition against a company, the debt must be more than £750. For a bankruptcy petition against an individual, including a sole trader, the minimum is much higher and is set by law. That figure has changed before, so your solicitor will confirm the current amount.
Is a statutory demand issued by the court?
No. It is prepared by the creditor or its solicitor and served directly on the debtor. The court only becomes involved if the debtor applies to set it aside, applies to stop a petition, or if the creditor goes on to present a petition. This is why there is no court fee for serving a demand.
Can I use a statutory demand for a claim for damages?
Usually not. A statutory demand must be for a liquidated sum, meaning a fixed amount that is due, such as an unpaid invoice. A claim for damages that still needs to be valued by the court is not suitable. Your solicitor will check whether your debt fits.
Is a statutory demand the same as a letter before action?
No. A letter before action is a step before an ordinary court claim and gives the debtor a chance to respond. A statutory demand is an insolvency tool that can lead to winding up or bankruptcy. Some creditors send a letter before action first, and then consider a statutory demand if the debt is undisputed and remains unpaid.
Can a statutory demand be used on a debt owed by a sole trader?
Yes. A sole trader is an individual, so a statutory demand served on them can lead to a bankruptcy petition if the debt meets the bankruptcy threshold and is not paid. Different rules on service and challenges apply to individuals. Your solicitor will explain how this works and whether it is proportionate.
