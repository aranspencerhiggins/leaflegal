More questions people ask

What counts as a motoring offence?
A motoring offence is any criminal offence connected with driving, riding or keeping a vehicle. Common examples are speeding, drink and drug driving, using a mobile phone, careless driving, driving without insurance and failing to name the driver. Most are dealt with in the magistrates' court, and many lead to penalty points or a driving ban as well as a fine.
Do I need a solicitor for a motoring offence?
There is no rule that you must have one, and many people deal with a simple fixed penalty on their own. People often look for a solicitor when a ban is possible, when they think there is a problem with the evidence, or when their job depends on driving. A solicitor will look at your own situation and explain the options.

Which courts deal with motoring cases?
Almost all motoring cases in England and Wales begin in the magistrates' court. Many are decided by a single magistrate on the papers under the Single Justice Procedure. A small number of serious cases, such as dangerous driving or causing death by driving, can go to the Crown Court. Appeals from the magistrates' court usually go to the Crown Court.
Is a motoring conviction a criminal record?
Yes. A conviction for an endorsable motoring offence is a criminal conviction, although many people do not think of it that way. It will appear on your driving record and may need to be disclosed to insurers and some employers. How long it must be disclosed depends on the sentence and the rules on rehabilitation.
Will a conviction affect my insurance?
Usually yes. Insurers often ask about motoring convictions and points over the last few years, and premiums tend to rise after a conviction, especially for drink driving or a ban. Not telling an insurer when asked can cause problems if you later make a claim. Your insurer can tell you what they need to know.
Can a business be prosecuted for a motoring offence?
Yes, in some situations. A company that owns or keeps a vehicle must respond to a request to name the driver, and failing to do so is an offence. Businesses can also face prosecution over vehicle condition, loads or drivers' hours. Directors and fleet managers often ask a solicitor to review how their notices are handled.
What is a notice of intended prosecution?
It is a warning that you may be prosecuted for certain offences, such as speeding. For many offences it must be given at the time or sent to the registered keeper within a set number of days. It often comes with a request to name the driver. A solicitor can check whether the notice was served correctly.
What is a Single Justice Procedure notice?
It is a way of dealing with some lower level offences on paper. A single magistrate decides the case using the written evidence and your reply, without a hearing in open court. You are given a deadline to respond. If you plead not guilty or want to explain something in person, you can ask for a court hearing.
What happens if I ignore court papers?
Ignoring court papers rarely makes a case go away. The court may decide the case without you, which can mean a conviction, a fine, points or a ban that you only find out about later. In some cases a warrant can be issued. If you have missed a deadline, a solicitor can explain what options may still be open.
How long does a motoring case take?
It varies a lot. A guilty plea under the Single Justice Procedure can be finished within weeks of the reply. A case that goes to trial in the magistrates' court can take several months. Cases that need blood analysis or expert evidence often take longer. Your solicitor can give you a better idea once they see the papers.
Do I have to go to court?
It depends on the offence and how the case started. Some cases can be dealt with by post. Others require you to attend, especially where a ban is likely or where the court has said you must be there. Your solicitor can tell you whether you need to attend and can usually represent you when you do.
Can I appeal a magistrates' court decision?
There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court against conviction, sentence or both. The time limit is short, normally 21 days from the sentence. In some cases you can ask for a driving ban to be suspended until the appeal is heard. A solicitor can explain whether an appeal is realistic.
Can the police just send me a fixed penalty?
For many minor offences, yes. A fixed penalty lets you accept a set fine and, for endorsable offences, a set number of points without going to court. It is not always offered, for example where the offence is too serious or you would reach 12 points. You can usually choose to have the matter decided by a court instead.
What is a postal requisition?
A postal requisition is a formal document sent by post that tells you what you are charged with and when and where you must attend court. It is commonly used for drink and drug driving cases after the police have released you, and for other offences where a court hearing is needed. It usually comes with the prosecution's case summary.
How many points lead to a ban?
Under the totting up system, reaching 12 or more points from offences committed within three years of each other usually leads to a ban of at least six months. Drivers within two years of passing their test face a lower limit of six points, which normally means their licence is revoked by the DVLA rather than a court ban.
How long do points stay on my licence?
For most offences, points stay on your driving record for four years from the date of the offence or conviction, depending on the offence. Drink and drug driving related endorsements stay for eleven years. Only points from offences within three years of each other count towards a totting up ban, even if older ones still show on your record.
Can I keep driving until my court date?
In most cases, yes. A ban usually starts from the day the court imposes it, not from the date of the offence or the charge. There are some exceptions, such as where a court imposes an interim ban before sentence. Your solicitor can tell you whether anything affects your own case.
What is the difference between special reasons and exceptional hardship?
Special reasons are about the circumstances of the offence itself, such as a genuine emergency, and can allow a court not to add points or not to impose an obligatory ban. Exceptional hardship is about the effect a totting up ban would have on you and others. They are different arguments with different rules.
Will I need to retake my test?
Not after most bans. You usually get your licence back when the ban ends, although for some offences you need to apply to the DVLA. The court must order an extended retest for certain serious offences, such as dangerous driving, and can order a retest in other cases. New drivers who reach six points also need to pass again.
What is a drink drive rehabilitation course?
It is a course a court can offer when it bans someone for a drink driving related offence for 12 months or more. If the driver completes it within the time set by the court, their ban is reduced. The driver pays for the course. It is not usually available for drug driving, and the court decides whether to offer it.
How do I check how many points I have?
You can view your driving record online through the GOV.UK driving licence service, using your licence number and a few personal details. It shows current endorsements, offence dates and any bans. It is worth checking before you reply to a notice or speak to a solicitor, because people often misremember dates and the exact number of points they hold.
How much does a motoring solicitor cost?
It depends on the offence, how complex it is and whether there is a trial. Many firms offer fixed fees for each stage, such as a guilty plea or an exceptional hardship hearing. Trials and cases needing expert evidence cost more. The firm will explain its fees and what they include before you agree to anything.
Is legal aid available for motoring offences?
Legal aid is rarely granted for motoring offences, because it usually depends on a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately. If your case is serious enough that legal aid might be available, a solicitor can tell you and help you apply.
Can I get my costs back if I am found not guilty?
Sometimes. A court can order that some of your legal costs are paid from public funds if you are acquitted. The amount is usually limited to set rates and is often well below what you paid privately. Ask the firm to explain how this works before you decide how to proceed.

What if my offence happened in Scotland?
Scotland has its own courts and procedures, which are different from those in England and Wales. The firms we introduce deal with cases in the courts of England and Wales. If your case is in Scotland or Northern Ireland, you would usually need a solicitor who practises there.
What is a licensable activity?
Under the Licensing Act 2003 the main licensable activities are selling alcohol by retail, supplying alcohol in a members club, providing regulated entertainment and providing late night refreshment, which is hot food or hot drink between 11pm and 5am. Some entertainment has been deregulated in certain circumstances, so not every event needs a licence. A licensing solicitor can explain how the rules apply to what you plan to do.
Who is the licensing authority?
For premises and personal licences, the licensing authority is usually the local district, borough, city or unitary council where the premises is, or where the applicant lives for a personal licence. For taxis and private hire, it is the council that issues the licence, or Transport for London for London. Each authority publishes its own licensing policy, which it must take into account when making decisions.
What are responsible authorities?
These are the public bodies that receive copies of premises licence applications and can make representations. They usually include the police, the fire and rescue authority, environmental health, planning, trading standards, the body responsible for child protection, the public health team, the licensing authority itself and Home Office Immigration Enforcement. Any of them can also apply for a review.
What is a statement of licensing policy?
Every licensing authority must publish a statement of its licensing policy and review it regularly. The policy explains how the council approaches applications, what it expects from operators and any local issues, such as areas with special rules on cumulative impact. Your solicitor will look at the relevant policy when preparing an application or responding to an objection.
Is planning permission the same as a licence?
No. Planning permission and licensing are separate regimes. A premises can hold a licence that allows later hours than the planning permission, or the other way round. Both usually need to be in place, and breaching either can cause problems. A solicitor can explain how the two interact for your premises and whether planning advice is also needed.
Does licensing cover gambling?
Gambling premises are licensed under a separate law, the Gambling Act 2005, with councils and the Gambling Commission both involved. Some licensing firms also handle gambling work. If your matter involves gaming machines in a pub or a betting or bingo premises, mention it on the form so the firm knows what you need.
Do I need a licence to play background music?
Not always. Recorded music and some live music in licensed premises have been deregulated within certain hours and audience sizes, and background music is generally not regulated entertainment. Separate copyright licences for playing music are a different matter and are not covered by the Licensing Act. A licensing solicitor can check whether your plans need to be on your premises licence.
