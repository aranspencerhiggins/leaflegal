More questions people ask

What is a licensable activity?
Under the Licensing Act 2003 the main licensable activities are selling alcohol by retail, supplying alcohol in a members club, providing regulated entertainment and providing late night refreshment, which is hot food or hot drink between 11pm and 5am. Some entertainment has been deregulated in certain circumstances, so not every event needs a licence. A licensing solicitor can explain how the rules apply to what you plan to do.
Who is the licensing authority?
For premises and personal licences, the licensing authority is usually the local district, borough, city or unitary council where the premises is, or where the applicant lives for a personal licence. For taxis and private hire, it is the council that issues the licence, or Transport for London for London. Each authority publishes its own licensing policy, which it must take into account when making decisions.
What are responsible authorities?
These are the public bodies that receive copies of premises licence applications and can make representations. They usually include the police, the fire and rescue authority, environmental health, planning, trading standards, the body responsible for child protection, the public health team, the licensing authority itself and Home Office Immigration Enforcement. Any of them can also apply for a review.
What is a statement of licensing policy?
Every licensing authority must publish a statement of its licensing policy and review it regularly. The policy explains how the council approaches applications, what it expects from operators and any local issues, such as areas with special rules on cumulative impact. Your solicitor will look at the relevant policy when preparing an application or responding to an objection.
Is planning permission the same as a licence?
No. Planning permission and licensing are separate regimes. A premises can hold a licence that allows later hours than the planning permission, or the other way round. Both usually need to be in place, and breaching either can cause problems. A solicitor can explain how the two interact for your premises and whether planning advice is also needed.
Does licensing cover gambling?
Gambling premises are licensed under a separate law, the Gambling Act 2005, with councils and the Gambling Commission both involved. Some licensing firms also handle gambling work. If your matter involves gaming machines in a pub or a betting or bingo premises, mention it on the form so the firm knows what you need.
Do I need a licence to play background music?
Not always. Recorded music and some live music in licensed premises have been deregulated within certain hours and audience sizes, and background music is generally not regulated entertainment. Separate copyright licences for playing music are a different matter and are not covered by the Licensing Act. A licensing solicitor can check whether your plans need to be on your premises licence.
What is a designated premises supervisor?
Premises licensed to sell alcohol usually need to name a designated premises supervisor, often called the DPS. This person must hold a personal licence and is usually the manager responsible for day to day running. The police see the DPS as their main point of contact. Some community premises can apply to remove this requirement.
Do all staff need a personal licence?
No. Every sale of alcohol must be made or authorised by a personal licence holder, but staff do not each need their own licence. In practice this means a personal licence holder, usually the DPS, authorises others to make sales. Many operators keep written authorisations and train staff on age checks and refusing sales.
What are licence conditions?
Conditions are the rules attached to a premises licence. Some are mandatory and apply to all alcohol licences, such as having an age verification policy. Others come from your own operating schedule or are added by the council, such as door staff at certain times or limits on outside drinking. Breaching a condition can be a criminal offence.
What is a temporary event notice?
A temporary event notice lets someone carry on licensable activities for a short event without a premises licence, subject to limits on numbers, length and how many notices can be given. It is given to the council and the police, who can object. There are standard and late notices with different deadlines. A solicitor can check whether a notice suits your plans.
Can I run a venue as a members club?
Genuine members clubs can apply for a club premises certificate instead of a premises licence. The club has to meet certain conditions about membership and how it is run. The rules are quite specific, so it is worth checking with a solicitor whether your organisation qualifies and which option suits it best.
What happens if a premises licence lapses?
A licence can lapse in some situations, such as the death, incapacity or insolvency of the holder. There are short time limits for steps that can keep it alive, such as an interim authority notice or a transfer. If you think a licence has lapsed or is about to, speak to a licensing solicitor quickly.
What is a licensing hearing?
A hearing takes place before a licensing sub-committee, usually three councillors, when an application or review cannot be agreed. The applicant, the responsible authorities and anyone who made representations can speak. Hearings are less formal than court, but they follow set rules. Many people choose to have a solicitor speak for them.
Can residents object to my licence?
Yes. Anyone can make a representation about a new application, a full variation or a review, as long as it relates to the licensing objectives and is not frivolous or vexatious. Representations are often about noise, anti-social behaviour or litter. A solicitor may be able to resolve concerns by agreeing conditions before a hearing.
What can the council do at a review?
At a review the council can take no action, issue a warning, change the conditions, remove a licensable activity, remove the designated premises supervisor, suspend the licence for up to three months or revoke it. The decision must be appropriate to promote the licensing objectives. Most decisions do not take effect until the appeal period ends.
Can I appeal a licensing decision?
Most licensing decisions can be appealed to the magistrates' court. The time limit is short, often 21 days from being told of the decision. The court looks at the matter again and can make a different decision. Appeals carry a risk of paying the other side's costs, so a solicitor will explain the risks before you decide.
What if the police close my premises?
The police and councils have powers to close premises quickly in some situations, such as serious disorder or nuisance, using closure notices and orders. A closure order can also lead to a licence review. These matters move very fast and often involve a court hearing within days. Contact a licensing solicitor as soon as possible.
Can I be prosecuted for licensing offences?
Yes. Offences include carrying on licensable activities without authorisation, breaching conditions, selling alcohol to children and allowing disorderly conduct. Some are dealt with by a fine, and sales to children can lead to a review. A solicitor can help with an investigation or prosecution and with any knock on effect on the licence.
What is cumulative impact?
Some councils have adopted a cumulative impact assessment for areas with a high number of licensed premises, often busy town centres. In those areas, new applications and some variations are more likely to be refused if objections are made, unless the applicant can show they will not add to existing problems. A solicitor can tell you whether your premises sits in such an area.
How much do licensing solicitors charge?
It depends on the type of matter. Routine applications such as transfers, DPS changes and minor variations are often done for a fixed fee. New licences, contested applications, reviews and appeals may be charged hourly or in stages, with a separate fee for attending a hearing. Ask the firm for a written quote before you agree to anything.
How much are council licence fees?
Premises licence fees under the Licensing Act 2003 are set nationally and usually depend on the rateable value of the premises, with an annual fee once granted. Taxi and private hire fees are set locally. All of these change from time to time, so check the current figures with your council or on GOV.UK.
Will I have to pay the council's costs if I lose?
Costs are not usually awarded at a council licensing hearing. On an appeal to the magistrates' court, the court can order one side to pay the other's costs, and councils often ask for theirs if the appeal fails. Your solicitor will explain the likely costs risk before you decide whether to appeal.

What is a leasehold flat?
When you own a leasehold flat, you own the right to live in it and sell it for the number of years set out in the lease. The freeholder owns the building and the land. Your lease sets out your rights and duties, such as paying ground rent and service charge, keeping the inside in repair and following rules about the building. When the lease ends, the flat goes back to the freeholder unless the lease has been extended.
Why does the length of my lease matter?
As a lease gets shorter, the flat usually becomes less valuable and harder to sell or mortgage. Many lenders have a minimum term they want left on the lease when the mortgage ends. Under the long standing rules, the cost of extending also tends to rise more quickly once the lease drops below 80 years. A solicitor can explain how this affects your flat.
How do I find out how many years are left?
Your lease states when it started and how long it was granted for. Subtract the years that have passed since the start date. If you do not have a copy of your lease, it can usually be obtained from HM Land Registry. A solicitor can check this for you as part of their first review, and it is worth doing as the start date is not always the date the lease was signed.
What is the difference between a lease extension and buying the freehold?
A lease extension gives you a new, longer lease of your own flat, but the freeholder still owns the building. Buying the freehold, usually with other flat owners, means you take over ownership of the building itself. Freehold ownership brings control over how the building is managed, along with the responsibilities that come with it.
Do these rights apply to houses?
Owners of leasehold houses have separate rights under the Leasehold Reform Act 1967, which can include extending the lease or buying the freehold of the house. The rules and valuation methods differ from those for flats. A solicitor who handles leasehold work can explain the position for a house.
What is the Right to Manage?
The Right to Manage lets qualifying flat owners take over the management of their building from the freeholder through a company they set up, without buying the freehold. It does not extend anyone's lease or change who owns the building. It is a separate process with its own rules, and a solicitor can explain how it compares with buying the freehold.
