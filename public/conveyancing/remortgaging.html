About remortgaging

When you remortgage to a new lender, a conveyancer acts for the lender to make sure the new mortgage is properly secured on your property. They check the title, pay off your existing mortgage and register the new charge at HM Land Registry.

Some lenders offer free legal work with a remortgage. If yours doesn’t, or the property or your circumstances are more complicated, you will need to instruct a firm yourself.

A good conveyancer keeps you updated without you having to chase, explains anything unusual in the paperwork in plain English and works with your estate agent and mortgage broker to keep the chain moving. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they work and what they need from you to get started.

Remortgage solicitor in brief

A remortgage solicitor handles the legal side when you move your mortgage to a new lender, raise extra money or change whose name is on the title. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm that handles remortgage conveyancing quickly and to your lender’s requirements.

Some lenders offer a free legal package, but you can choose your own remortgage solicitor, especially if there is a transfer of equity, a lease issue or a second charge to deal with. Remortgage legal fees are usually a fixed fee plus a few small disbursements.

Remortgaging explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Remortgaging means replacing your existing mortgage with a new one, usually from a different lender, without moving home. People remortgage when a fixed or tracker deal is coming to an end, to find a better rate, to borrow more for home improvements or other plans, to change the length of the loan or to take someone off or put someone on the mortgage.

Because the new lender is lending against your home, it needs to be sure that the property is good security and that its mortgage will rank first on the title once the old one is paid off. That is the legal work. A conveyancer checks the title, confirms the property meets the lender's requirements, pays off the old lender on completion and registers the new mortgage at HM Land Registry.

Compared with buying or selling, a remortgage is usually simpler. There is no seller, no chain and no contract to negotiate. But it still has a deadline that matters, which is the end of your current deal. If the remortgage is not completed in time, you may move onto your current lender's standard variable rate for a while, which is often higher.

The legal side of a remortgage is only one part of the picture. Your broker or lender deals with the choice of product, your affordability and the valuation. Your conveyancer deals with the title, the money and the registration. The two sides work in parallel, and a delay on one usually holds up the other, so it helps to keep both informed about any change in your plans or circumstances.

When a conveyancer is not needed

If you stay with your current lender and simply take a new deal, known as a product transfer, there is usually no legal work, because the existing mortgage stays registered against your home. A conveyancer is normally only needed when a new lender is involved, when the amount or terms change in a way that needs a new mortgage deed, or when the ownership changes at the same time.

Who this page is for

  • Homeowners switching to a new lender at the end of a deal
  • Owners borrowing more to fund home improvements or other plans
  • Couples removing or adding someone as part of the remortgage
  • Owners with a leasehold, shared ownership or equity loan property
  • Owners whose lender does not offer free legal work, or whose case is too complex for it

A remortgage follows a fairly predictable pattern. The legal work usually begins once your mortgage application is under way or your offer has been issued.

1. Mortgage application

You apply to the new lender, often through a broker. The lender values the property, assesses your income and outgoings and issues a mortgage offer. The offer will say which firm is acting for the lender, or ask you to choose one from its panel.

2. Instructing the firm

The conveyancer carries out identity checks and receives instructions from the lender. They ask you for details of your current mortgage and any other loans secured on the property.

3. Checking the title

The conveyancer obtains up to date copies of the register and title plan from HM Land Registry. They check that you are the registered owner, look for any other charges, restrictions or notices, and confirm that nothing on the title breaches the lender's requirements.

4. Searches or insurance

Lenders do not always require full searches on a remortgage. Many accept a special insurance policy instead, which covers the lender against matters a search would have revealed. Your conveyancer will follow what the lender asks for.

5. Redemption figure

The conveyancer asks your existing lender for a redemption statement showing how much is needed to pay off the mortgage on a particular date, including any early repayment charge.

6. Signing the mortgage deed

You sign the new mortgage deed, usually in front of an independent witness or electronically if the firm and lender allow it. Your conveyancer then reports to the lender that everything is in order and asks for the funds.

7. Completion

The new lender sends the money to your conveyancer, who pays off the old mortgage. Any extra borrowing is sent to you, less fees and any amount you have asked to be used elsewhere.

8. Registration

The conveyancer applies to HM Land Registry to remove the old mortgage from the title and register the new one.

What you will be asked for

  • Photo ID and proof of address for each borrower
  • Your current lender's name and your mortgage account number
  • Details of any other loans secured on your home
  • Your mortgage offer, or the reference for it
  • For leasehold, your landlord or managing agent's details
  • Bank details for any extra money being released, confirmed by phone

Many lenders offer a free legal service with their remortgage products. The lender chooses the firm and pays its standard fee, which can make a remortgage cheaper overall. Others offer cashback instead, which you can put towards your own conveyancer's fees.

What free legal work usually covers

Free legal packages are designed for straightforward remortgages. They typically cover checking the title, redeeming your existing mortgage, completing the new mortgage and registering it. They often include the cost of any insurance used in place of searches.

What it may not cover

  • Adding or removing someone from the ownership
  • Leasehold properties needing notices to the landlord or managing agent
  • Shared ownership or equity loan consents
  • Unregistered property needing first registration
  • Problems on the title, such as a defective lease or missing rights
  • Second charges or other secured loans that need dealing with
  • Properties owned by a company or trust

If your remortgage involves any of these, the lender's firm may charge you for the extra work, or the lender may require you to instruct your own firm.

Using your own firm

Some people prefer to choose their own conveyancer even when free legal work is available. Reasons include wanting a named person to deal with, needing help with something outside the standard package, or wanting the same firm to handle a transfer of equity at the same time. Your own firm will need to be on the lender's panel and will act for the lender as well as for you.

If you go with free legal work, it is still worth reading what the lender's firm sends you and replying promptly. These firms handle large volumes of work through set processes, and a missing answer or unsigned form can sit unnoticed until someone chases it.

A mortgage broker can explain the difference in total cost between a deal with free legal work and one with cashback or no legal incentive.

Most remortgages are routine, but some properties and situations need extra steps. These can add time, so it helps to raise them early.

Leasehold property

Your conveyancer will check that the lease is acceptable to the new lender, including how long it has left and the ground rent terms. After completion, many leases require notice of the new mortgage to be given to the landlord or managing agent, often with a fee. If the lease is getting short, some lenders may not lend until it has been extended.

Equity loans

If you bought with a government backed equity loan, you usually need the scheme administrator's consent to remortgage, and your new lender must agree to rank behind or alongside it as the rules require. The consent process has its own forms and timescales, so it is wise to start it early.

Shared ownership

Shared ownership leases usually require the housing provider's consent to a new mortgage. If you are borrowing more to buy extra shares, known as staircasing, the conveyancing is more involved because the lease and ownership shares change at the same time.

Changing the owners

If someone is being added or removed at the same time, the remortgage and the transfer of equity are usually done together. The new lender will need to approve the new owners, and the transfer deed and new mortgage will be registered at the same time.

Unregistered property

If your home has never been registered at HM Land Registry, the new lender will want it registered. Your conveyancer will need the original deeds and will apply for first registration, which takes longer than a standard remortgage.

Debt consolidation and second charges

If you are using the remortgage to pay off other debts, the lender may require some of those debts to be repaid directly on completion. If you have an existing secured loan, it may need to be cleared or the other lender's consent obtained.

Remortgage costs are usually lower than buying costs, but they vary depending on whether you use free legal work and how complex the property is.

Legal costs

If you use the lender's free legal service, the lender pays the standard fee. If you instruct your own firm, expect a fixed fee for a standard remortgage, with extra charges for things like leasehold, equity loans, shared ownership, a transfer of equity or a second charge. Disbursements usually include HM Land Registry fees, title copies, identity checks and bank transfer fees.

Lender and broker costs

Outside the legal fee, there may be an arrangement fee for the new mortgage, a valuation fee, a broker fee and an early repayment charge or exit fee from your current lender. These can make a big difference to whether a remortgage saves money overall, so it is worth asking a broker to compare the full picture.

Property tax

A straightforward remortgage does not usually involve property tax, because no one is buying the property. If ownership changes at the same time and money or debt passes between owners, tax may apply. Your conveyancer will check this if a transfer of equity is involved.

Remortgages are usually quicker than purchases, but a few issues come up often enough to be worth knowing about.

Leaving it too late

Many people start looking for a new deal only a few weeks before the old one ends. If there is any problem with the title, the valuation or the lender's checks, the remortgage may not complete in time. Lenders often allow you to secure a new deal some months in advance, so speaking to a broker early helps.

Valuation shortfalls

If the lender values the property lower than expected, it may offer less or put you on a different rate. Your broker can help find alternatives.

Title issues

Remortgages sometimes uncover problems that were missed or arose after purchase, such as a restriction from a previous loan, an old charge that was never removed, or a name on the register that no longer matches. These can usually be fixed, but may need extra paperwork.

Signing and witnessing

Mortgage deeds must be signed and witnessed correctly. If the witness is a family member, or the deed is dated or completed wrongly, the lender may reject it and ask for it to be signed again, causing delay. Follow your conveyancer's instructions closely.

Occupiers

Lenders usually ask whether anyone aged 17 or over lives in the property who is not on the mortgage. Those people are often asked to sign a form agreeing that the lender's mortgage takes priority over any rights they might have. This can cause awkward conversations, so it helps to know in advance.

Changes in circumstances

Lenders make their offer based on the information you gave when you applied. If something important changes before completion, such as your job, your income, a new debt or someone moving in, you may need to tell the lender. Some changes can lead the lender to review or withdraw its offer. Your broker can explain what needs to be reported, and your conveyancer will confirm the position just before completion.

Most conveyancing firms quote a fixed legal fee for the transaction, plus disbursements. Disbursements are costs the firm pays on your behalf, such as searches, Land Registry fees, identity checks and bank transfer fees. Remortgaging questions and answers

Do I need a solicitor to remortgage?
Yes, your new lender needs a conveyancer to check the title, pay off the old mortgage and register the new one. Some lenders arrange this for free, but you can choose your own.
What are typical remortgage legal fees?
Usually a modest fixed fee plus VAT and small disbursements such as Land Registry fees and searches or search insurance. The firm confirms the figure in writing.
Do I need a solicitor to remortgage?
If you are moving to a new lender, the lender will need its legal work done. Some lenders arrange this for free. Otherwise you instruct a conveyancer.
How long does a remortgage take?
Usually a few weeks from instruction, depending on the lender and any issues with the title. Starting early helps if your current deal is ending.
Can I add or remove someone at the same time?
Yes. This is a transfer of equity, and many firms handle it alongside the remortgage.
Does Leaflegal do the conveyancing?
No. Leaflegal is a free introduction service. The firm we introduce you to does the legal work and is responsible for it.
Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
What is the difference between a remortgage and a product transfer?
A product transfer is when you take a new deal with your existing lender. The mortgage stays the same legal charge, so usually no conveyancer is involved. A remortgage is when you move to a new lender, or sometimes borrow more in a way that needs a new deed. That requires legal work to pay off the old loan and register the new one.
Who does the conveyancer act for in a remortgage?
The conveyancer acts for the new lender, making sure its mortgage is properly secured, and usually also for you. If the lender's own panel firm is doing free legal work, it may act mainly for the lender. If you choose your own firm, it acts for you and the lender, and must be on the lender's panel.
Do I need searches for a remortgage?
Not always. Many lenders accept a remortgage search insurance policy instead of full searches, which is quicker and cheaper. Some lenders, or some properties, still require searches. Your conveyancer will follow the lender's instructions and tell you if searches are needed.
Can I remortgage to release money from my home?
Yes, many people borrow more when they remortgage, sometimes called equity release in the general sense, though that term also describes specialist lifetime mortgages for older homeowners. Your lender will decide how much you can borrow. Your conveyancer handles the legal side and pays you the extra money on completion.
Is a lifetime mortgage the same as a remortgage?
No. A lifetime mortgage is a specialist form of equity release usually for older homeowners, with its own rules and protections. Lenders normally require you to have independent legal advice from a solicitor before it completes. It is quite different from a standard remortgage and needs specialist advice.
Can I remortgage a property I own outright?
Yes. If you own your home without a mortgage and want to borrow against it, the process is similar but there is no existing lender to pay off. Your conveyancer checks the title, completes the new mortgage and registers it, then sends you the money.
Will I get the extra money on the day of completion?
If you are borrowing more than you owe, your conveyancer receives the full advance from the new lender, pays off the old mortgage and any debts the lender requires to be cleared, deducts agreed fees and sends you the rest. This usually happens on completion day or the next working day, to an account you have confirmed by phone.
How long does a remortgage take?
A straightforward remortgage often takes a few weeks from the mortgage offer to completion. It can take longer if there are title problems, consents to obtain, a leasehold notice process or an equity loan involved. Starting well before your current deal ends gives room for any delays.
When should I start looking for a new deal?
Many lenders let you secure a new rate several months before your current deal ends. Speaking to a broker or lender early gives time to compare options and complete the legal work. Your conveyancer can usually agree a completion date that matches the end of your current deal.
What happens if my remortgage is not ready in time?
If your current deal ends before the remortgage completes, your current lender will usually move you onto its standard variable rate until the new mortgage is in place. That can mean higher payments for a short period. You will not normally lose your home or be in breach, but it can cost more.
Can I choose the date my remortgage completes?
To some extent. Your conveyancer and the lender agree a completion date once the legal work is done and the funds are ready. Many people aim to complete the day after their current deal ends to avoid early repayment charges. Your conveyancer can help line this up.
What slows a remortgage down?
Common causes include delays in the lender's own checks, a low valuation, missing information about your current mortgage, consent needed from a landlord, housing provider or equity loan administrator, and problems on the title. Returning paperwork quickly and telling your conveyancer about anything unusual early helps.
Can I remortgage soon after buying?
Yes, although some lenders have rules about lending on a property that was bought very recently, and an early repayment charge on your existing mortgage may make it expensive. If you want to remortgage soon after buying, perhaps to fund works, speak to a broker about the lender's criteria and the costs before you apply.
Do I have to stop paying my old mortgage while I remortgage?
No. Keep paying your existing mortgage as normal until completion. The redemption figure will take account of payments made, and any overpayment is usually refunded by the old lender afterwards. Stopping payments early can lead to arrears, fees and a mark on your credit record, which could affect the new lending.
What is a redemption statement?
It is a document from your current lender showing exactly how much you need to pay to clear the mortgage on a given date. It includes the outstanding balance, interest to that date and any charges. Your conveyancer uses it to make sure the old lender is paid off in full on completion.
What if there is an old charge on my title?
Sometimes a mortgage or loan that was paid off years ago is still shown on the register because it was never formally removed. Your conveyancer will ask the old lender for proof that it was repaid and apply to remove it. This is usually straightforward but can take a little time.
Why do occupiers have to sign a form?
If an adult who is not an owner lives in the property, such as a partner, parent or grown up child, the lender wants to make sure that person cannot claim rights that rank ahead of the mortgage. The occupier is usually asked to sign a consent form, sometimes after taking independent advice.
What if my name on the register is different?
If you have changed your name since you bought, your conveyancer may ask for evidence, such as a marriage certificate or deed poll, and update the register at the same time as registering the new mortgage. It is a routine step but needs the correct documents.
Do I need to tell my landlord about a remortgage?
If your home is leasehold, the lease often requires you to give notice of any new mortgage to the landlord or managing agent, usually with a fee. Your conveyancer will check the lease and send the notice after completion. Some leases also require the landlord's consent before the new mortgage.
What if my lease is short?
Lenders have rules on how many years must remain on a lease, and a short lease can lead to a lower valuation or a refusal to lend. If your lease is getting short, the lender may want it extended first. Your conveyancer can explain the options, and a lease extension is a separate legal process.
What happens if the lender finds a problem with my title?
Your conveyancer will explain the issue and what the lender needs to resolve it. Common fixes include providing evidence, removing an old entry, obtaining a consent or arranging an insurance policy. If the problem cannot be fixed in time, the lender may delay completion or, rarely, withdraw its offer.
Can I remove my ex-partner from the mortgage when I remortgage?
Yes, this is common after a separation. It involves a transfer of equity, where your ex-partner transfers their share in the property to you, as well as the new mortgage in your sole name. The new lender must be satisfied you can afford the loan alone. Your ex-partner usually needs separate advice.
Can I add my partner when I remortgage?
Yes. Adding someone to the ownership as part of a remortgage is done through a transfer of equity. The new lender will assess both of you and the new mortgage will be in joint names. Your conveyancer will explain the ways of owning jointly and any tax that might apply.
Can I remortgage a buy to let property?
Yes, but buy to let remortgages use different lenders and products. The lender may want details of the tenancy and the rent. If the property is owned through a limited company, the legal work is different and the lender usually wants personal guarantees from directors.
Can I remortgage with an equity loan?
Usually, but you need consent from the equity loan administrator, and the new lender must accept the equity loan's position on the title. The consent process can take weeks, so start it early. Your conveyancer will explain the forms and any fees involved.
Can I remortgage a property held in a trust or a company?
It is possible, but the legal work is more involved. The conveyancer must check that the trustees or the company have the power to borrow, that the right people sign, and that any restrictions on the title are met. Not every lender lends in these situations, so a broker can help find one that does.
Is it cheaper to use free legal work?
Often, for a simple remortgage. The lender pays the standard fee, and you only pay for any extra work. But if your case is more complex or you want your own firm, a deal with cashback or a lower rate without free legal work may work out better. A broker can compare the total costs.
What does a remortgage usually cost if I pay?
It depends on the firm, the type of property and any extra work such as leasehold notices, equity loan consents or a transfer of equity. Most firms quote a fixed fee plus disbursements and VAT. The firm we introduce you to will confirm a quote based on your situation.

What is an early repayment charge?
It is a fee some lenders charge if you pay off your mortgage, or part of it, during a fixed or discounted period. The amount is set out in your mortgage terms and the redemption statement. Many people time their remortgage to complete just after the charge ends. Your lender or broker can confirm whether a charge applies to you.

What does transfer of equity mean?
It means changing who legally owns a property without selling it on the open market. Someone is added to or removed from the title, or the property passes from one owner to another, often between family members or partners. The change is recorded at HM Land Registry once the transfer is complete.
Do I need a conveyancer for a transfer of equity?
If there is a mortgage, the lender will almost always require a conveyancer to act for it. Even without a mortgage, the transfer deed must be correctly prepared, signed and registered, and tax needs checking. Mistakes can be expensive to put right, so most people use a regulated firm.
How long does a transfer of equity take?
A simple transfer with no mortgage can be quick once everyone has signed. Where a lender is involved, timescales depend on how long the lender takes to assess and consent, or to issue a new mortgage offer. Separation cases can take longer if the terms are still being negotiated.
Can I transfer a share rather than the whole property?
Yes. A transfer of equity can add someone as a co-owner, so they own the property jointly with you, rather than transferring it outright. The way you hold the property, and any declaration of trust, determines how the value would be split between you.
What is a declaration of trust?
It is a legal document recording how co-owners share the beneficial ownership of a property, such as who put in what money and how proceeds would be split on a sale. It can be signed at the same time as a transfer of equity. It is particularly common where contributions are unequal or owners are not married.
Do all the owners need to sign?
Usually, yes. Everyone transferring their interest and everyone receiving one normally signs the transfer deed. If an owner cannot sign, for example because they lack capacity, someone with legal authority such as a registered attorney or court appointed deputy may need to sign on their behalf.
Is a transfer of equity the same as a sale?
Not quite. There is no open market sale, estate agent or chain, and often no money changes hands. But legally it is still a transfer of land, so many of the same steps apply, including identity checks, a signed deed, a tax check and registration at HM Land Registry. Where money is paid, it can look much like a small purchase.
Can I change from joint tenants to tenants in common without a transfer?
Yes. Co-owners who hold as joint tenants can usually change to tenants in common by a process called severance, often by serving a written notice and registering a restriction at HM Land Registry. No transfer of equity is needed because the owners stay the same. Your conveyancer can explain how severance works and when people use it.
