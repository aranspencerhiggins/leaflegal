About buying and selling together

Most people who move are selling one home and buying another on the same day. Using one firm for both means the two transactions are managed together, so exchange and completion can be lined up and the money from your sale can be used for your purchase.

Your conveyancer keeps track of both sides of the chain and tells you if anything on one transaction might hold up the other.

A good conveyancer keeps you updated without you having to chase, explains anything unusual in the paperwork in plain English and works with your estate agent and mortgage broker to keep the chain moving. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they work and what they need from you to get started.

Buying and selling conveyancing in brief

If you are selling one home and buying another, using one firm for buying and selling conveyancing keeps everything in step. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm that runs both sides of your move and lines up exchange and completion on the same day.

Buying and selling conveyancing fees are often quoted as a package that is cheaper than two separate matters. Ask what is included for each side, and whether the firm charges extra for a chain, a leasehold or a bridging loan.

Buying and selling together explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Moving home usually means two transactions at once. You are a seller to the person buying your current home, and a buyer to the person selling your next one. Each transaction has its own contract, its own checks and its own timetable, but they depend on each other. Most people cannot afford to buy without the money from their sale, and few want to sell without somewhere to go. The legal challenge is keeping the two in step so that they exchange and complete together.

Using one firm for both sides means a single conveyancer, or a single team, can see the whole picture. They know when your sale is ready and when your purchase is ready, and they can line up exchange and completion so that you are never committed to one without the other. They also handle the flow of money from your sale into your purchase on the same day.

This page looks at what is different when you are moving rather than just buying or just selling. The legal steps for each side are much the same as a standalone sale or purchase. What changes is the coordination, the money and the extra risks that come from being in the middle of a chain.

Why the middle of the chain is different

A buyer at the bottom of a chain only has to worry about their own purchase. A seller at the top only has to worry about their sale. When you are in the middle, you depend on the people below you being ready to buy your home and the people above you being ready to sell theirs. A delay anywhere in the chain reaches you, and your own readiness affects everyone else.

Who this page is for

  • Homeowners moving up, downsizing or relocating
  • People selling one home and buying another on the same day
  • Movers who want to buy before they sell, or sell before they buy
  • Couples selling a shared home and each buying separately, where one firm acts for one of them
  • Anyone porting a mortgage or taking a new one as part of a move

Leaflegal does not give legal advice. We introduce you to a regulated firm that will look at both transactions and explain how they fit together for you.

The aim in most moves is to exchange contracts on your sale and your purchase at the same moment, and to complete both on the same day. That way you are never bound to buy without a buyer for your home, or bound to sell without somewhere to go.

Synchronised exchange

When everyone in the chain is ready, the conveyancers agree a completion date and exchange contracts in a set order over the phone, usually starting from one end of the chain. Each conveyancer only commits their client once they know the linked transaction is being exchanged at the same time. This is the moment the whole chain becomes binding.

Linked completion

On completion day, money moves up the chain. The first buyer's money reaches their seller's conveyancer, who adds it to their client's mortgage and funds to complete the next purchase, and so on. Your conveyancer uses the money from your sale, plus any new mortgage and your own funds, to pay for your purchase. If one link is slow, everyone above it waits.

Deposits in a chain

Your buyer pays a deposit to your conveyancer on exchange. With the right wording in the contract, that deposit can often be passed on as part of the deposit for your own purchase. This means you may need less cash of your own at exchange. Your conveyancer will check whether the contracts allow this and how much extra you need to provide.

What your conveyancer is watching

  • Whether your sale's contract pack has been issued and enquiries answered
  • Whether your buyer has their mortgage offer and their own sale is progressing
  • Whether your purchase searches and enquiries are complete
  • Whether your own mortgage offer or porting is in place
  • Whether the sellers above you have found somewhere to go
  • Whether everyone can agree the same completion date

Being ready on both sides

Movers sometimes focus on their purchase and forget that their sale needs just as much attention. A common pattern is a purchase that is ready to exchange while the sale is still waiting for answers to enquiries or a missing certificate. Answering sale questions quickly, ordering any leasehold pack early and keeping your buyer's side informed all help to make sure both transactions reach the finishing line together.

Your conveyancer cannot see inside other people's files, but they can ask the other conveyancers in the chain for updates and tell you where things are being held up.

Moving home involves more money flowing in more directions than a simple purchase or sale. Understanding how it works helps avoid surprises.

The completion statement

For a linked move, your conveyancer prepares a statement that brings both transactions together. It shows the money coming in from your sale and new mortgage, the money going out to pay off your old mortgage and buy your new home, and the fees, disbursements and tax. The balance is what you need to provide, or what you will receive if you are downsizing.

Porting or a new mortgage

Some movers keep their existing mortgage deal and move it to the new property, which is called porting. Others redeem their old mortgage and take out a new one. Either way, the lender will need to approve the new property, and your conveyancer will need to deal with the old lender on the sale and the new or ported lending on the purchase. Your broker or lender can explain which suits you.

Early repayment charges

If you are redeeming a mortgage during a fixed or discounted period, an early repayment charge may apply. Sometimes it is refunded if you port and take a new loan with the same lender within a set time. Check this with your lender before agreeing dates.

Bridging finance

Bridging is short term borrowing to cover a gap, for example to buy before your sale completes. It can keep a move on track, but it is usually more expensive than a normal mortgage and is secured on property. It needs careful thought and independent financial advice, and your conveyancer will need to act for the bridging lender as well.

Property tax when you move

If you buy your new home before your old one is sold, you may be treated as owning more than one home and pay a higher rate of property tax on the purchase. In some cases part of it can be reclaimed if you sell your old main home within a set period. Rules differ between England and Wales and change over time, so ask your conveyancer to check your position.

The question most movers ask is when they can book removals. The honest answer is that nothing is certain until contracts exchange. Before that, dates are only targets.

Picking a completion date

Everyone in the chain must agree a single completion date before exchange. Most chains settle on a weekday, as banks need to be open to send money. Many people avoid Fridays where they can, because a delay late in the day can leave a whole chain waiting over the weekend with no keys. A gap of a week or two between exchange and completion gives time to book removals and give notice to utility companies.

Exchange and completion on the same day

In a chain this is possible but demanding. Everyone must be packed and ready, with money waiting, before anyone is committed. If something goes wrong on the day, people may find themselves with a removal van and nowhere to go. Your conveyancer will talk through whether this approach suits your move.

Moving day

On the day, you need to be out of your old home by the time agreed in the contract, usually so that your buyer can move in once their money arrives. You can usually collect keys for your new home only once your seller's conveyancer has received the money for your purchase. That depends on your own sale money arriving first. In a long chain, the gap between leaving one home and getting into the next can be a few hours.

Renting in between

Some movers choose to complete their sale and move into rented accommodation or stay with family before buying. This removes the pressure of lining up two transactions, and a buyer who has already sold can be attractive to sellers. It does mean paying for storage and rent, moving twice and taking the risk that prices change in the meantime. Your conveyancer will still handle the sale and the later purchase, just at different times.

Practical tips

  • Keep your removal firm informed and ask about their policy if completion is delayed
  • Pack essentials and important documents separately
  • Take meter readings at both properties
  • Keep your phone charged and your conveyancer's direct number to hand
  • Arrange where you will stay if keys are released late

A linked sale and purchase is two pieces of legal work, but many firms offer a combined quote that reflects the saving from handling both together.

What the quote usually covers

  • A legal fee for the sale and a separate fee for the purchase, sometimes discounted when combined
  • Mortgage redemption on the sale and mortgage work on the purchase
  • Searches and HM Land Registry fees on the purchase
  • Official copies of your title on the sale
  • Extra charges for leasehold, new build or a ported mortgage
  • Identity checks, bank transfers and VAT

Costs that come from the chain

If one side falls through, you may have already paid for searches and legal work on the other side. Some firms charge less or nothing for an abortive transaction, while others charge for work done. Ask about this when you compare quotes, especially if your chain is long.

Other moving costs

Outside the legal quote, movers usually pay estate agency fees on the sale, a survey and mortgage fees on the purchase, any early repayment charge, property tax on the purchase and removal costs. Your conveyancer can deduct the agent's fee from the sale money if you ask.

Chains are fragile. The longer the chain, the more chance that one link will slow down or break. Your conveyancer cannot stop that happening, but they can explain your options and help keep your part of the move on track.

Your buyer pulls out

If your buyer withdraws before exchange, your purchase is not affected legally, but you may no longer be able to afford it. Your options might include finding a new buyer quickly, asking your seller to wait, using bridging finance or letting your purchase go. Your conveyancer and estate agent can tell you how others in the chain are likely to react.

Your seller pulls out

If the home you are buying is withdrawn, your sale can usually continue. You could look for another property, move into rented accommodation for a while or ask your buyer to wait. Some movers choose to complete their sale and rent so that they become chain free, which can make their next offer stronger.

Delays in the chain

If one transaction is waiting for something, such as a mortgage offer or a management pack, the others usually cannot exchange. Your conveyancer can ask for regular updates. In some cases parties agree to exchange with a later completion date so that the chain is at least committed.

Delay on completion day

If a buyer further down the chain cannot send their money on time, completion is delayed for everyone above. The contracts usually allow interest to be charged for late completion, and costs can be passed along the chain. Your conveyancer will keep you updated on the day.

Breaking the chain

Some movers decide to sell first and rent, or buy first using savings or bridging, to remove the dependency. Each approach has costs and risks. A conveyancer and a financial adviser can help you weigh them up for your own situation.

Fees and costs

Most conveyancing firms quote a fixed legal fee for the transaction, plus disbursements. Disbursements are costs the firm pays on your behalf, such as searches, Land Registry fees, identity checks and bank transfer fees. If you are buying, Stamp Duty Land Tax may also be due, and the firm usually pays it for you from the money you send them.

Leasehold work, new builds, shared ownership and buying with a company usually cost more because there is more to check. A good firm puts everything in writing before you instruct them, so you can compare quotes like for like and avoid surprises at completion.

Buying and selling together questions and answers

Can one solicitor handle my sale and purchase?
Yes, and most people use the same firm for both. It makes it easier to link exchange and completion and to move money from the sale to the purchase.
Are buying and selling conveyancing fees cheaper together?
Often. Many firms offer a combined quote that costs less than two separate matters. The firm explains exactly what is included.
Is it cheaper to use one firm for both?
Many firms offer a combined quote for a sale and purchase. Ask for a breakdown so you can see the fee for each side and the disbursements.
What does simultaneous exchange and completion mean?
Exchanging contracts and completing on the same day. It is sometimes used to save time, but it leaves little room if something goes wrong, so your conveyancer will talk through whether it suits you.
What if my sale falls through?
Your conveyancer will tell you where that leaves your purchase and what your options are. Until contracts are exchanged, nobody is legally bound.
Does Leaflegal do the conveyancing?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service. The firm we introduce you to does the legal work and is responsible for it.
Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
Should I use the same firm for my sale and purchase?
Many movers do, because one firm can see both sides and coordinate exchange and completion more easily. It also means one completion statement and one point of contact. You can use two different firms, but they will need to communicate closely, which adds a layer of coordination.
Should I sell before I start looking to buy?
There is no single right answer. Having a buyer for your home, or a sale already agreed, usually makes your offer more attractive to sellers. Looking first lets you see what is available. Many people put their home on the market at the same time as starting to view.
When should I instruct my conveyancer?
As soon as you put your home on the market, if not before. The firm can prepare your sale paperwork and carry out identity checks while you look for your next home. When both offers are agreed, your conveyancer is already up and running on the sale, which helps the whole chain.
What if I find a home before I have a buyer?
You can still make an offer, but many sellers prefer buyers who are ready to proceed. If your offer is accepted, your conveyancer can start the purchase, but exchange usually waits until you have a buyer and your sale has caught up. Some sellers may not be willing to wait.
What information will the firm need for both sides?
Expect to provide identity documents, details of your current lender and mortgage, information about your new mortgage or porting, both memoranda of sale, and evidence of any money you are adding. You will also complete the property information forms for your sale.
How long does a linked move usually take?
It often takes a few months from both offers being accepted to completion, but it depends on the length of the chain and how ready everyone is. A chain is only as fast as its slowest link. Your conveyancer can give a better estimate once both transactions are under way.
What if I am buying and selling with different people?
Sometimes the owners of the home being sold are not the same as the buyers of the new one, for example after a separation or when a parent is moving in with family. Your conveyancer needs to know exactly who owns what, who is buying and where the money is going, as this affects the paperwork, identity checks and how the sale money is paid out.
What is a chain?
A chain is a series of transactions linked because each buyer is also selling or each seller is also buying. If you sell to someone who is also selling, and you buy from someone who is also buying, you are in a chain. Everyone needs to exchange and complete together.
Can my conveyancer speed up the chain?
Your conveyancer can make sure your part is ready, chase other conveyancers for updates and tell you where the hold up is. They cannot make other people's lenders, conveyancers or councils move faster. Estate agents often play a role in pushing the chain along as well.
What does chain free mean?
A chain free buyer is not selling a property they need to sell first, such as a first time buyer or someone who has already sold. A chain free seller is not buying elsewhere. Chain free parties can usually move faster and more flexibly, which is why sellers like them.
How many people can be in a chain?
There is no limit. Chains of three or four transactions are common, and some are longer. The more links, the more chance of delays, because everyone must be ready at the same time. A chain often ends with a first time buyer at the bottom and a chain free seller at the top.
What happens if someone else in the chain drops out?
If a transaction anywhere in the chain fails before exchange, the chain may break. Sometimes a new buyer or seller can be found quickly to fill the gap. Your conveyancer will tell you how it affects your sale and purchase and what your options are.
Can we exchange with a long completion date?
Yes, if everyone in the chain agrees. Exchanging with a completion date some weeks away gives everyone certainty while allowing more time to move. It is sometimes used where someone needs to wait for a school term to end, a tenancy to expire or a new build to finish.
What is gazundering?
Gazundering is when a buyer lowers their offer shortly before exchange, knowing the seller may feel under pressure to accept to keep their own purchase on track. It is legal, because nothing is binding before exchange, but it can unsettle a whole chain. If it happens to you, your estate agent and conveyancer can explain the effect on your purchase.
Can I use my buyer's deposit towards my purchase?
Often, yes. Standard contract terms can allow the deposit your buyer pays on exchange to be used as part of your own deposit on the property you are buying. Your conveyancer will check the contracts and tell you if you need to add any money of your own at exchange.
Do I pay property tax on my new home if I have not sold my old one?
If you complete your purchase before your sale, you may own two homes for a time and face a higher rate of property tax. Some of this may be reclaimable if you sell your old main home within a set period. The rules change, so your conveyancer will confirm your position.
What is porting a mortgage?
Porting means moving your existing mortgage deal from your current home to your new one, which can help avoid early repayment charges. The lender still has to approve you and the new property, and if you need to borrow more, that extra amount may be on a different deal. Ask your lender or broker how it works for you.
What if I am downsizing and will have money left over?
Your conveyancer will show the surplus on your completion statement and send it to you after completion. It is sensible to think in advance about where you want it to go, and to confirm bank details by phone, not email. Large sums can attract fraudsters, so be cautious about any request to change details.
What is bridging finance?
Bridging finance is a short term loan that lets you complete a purchase before your sale money arrives. It can keep a move going when timing does not line up, but it is usually expensive and secured on property. Speak to a financial adviser before using it, and your conveyancer will also act for the bridging lender.
Who pays off my old mortgage?
Your conveyancer obtains a redemption figure from your current lender before exchange and again close to completion. On completion day they use the money from your sale to pay off the old mortgage, then use the balance, with any new mortgage and your own funds, towards your purchase. They then make sure the old lender's charge is removed from the register.
When can I book removals?
Only book firm dates once contracts have exchanged and the completion date is fixed. Before exchange, any date is provisional and can change. Some removal firms will pencil in a date and confirm on exchange. Ask them about their policy for delays on the day.
What time do I need to be out of my old home?
The contract usually says vacant possession must be given on completion, and many contracts set a time by which completion should happen. In practice, you should be ready to hand over keys when your buyer's money arrives. Your conveyancer and agent will confirm the arrangements.
Why might I get my new keys late in the day?
In a chain, money passes from one transaction to the next. Your seller's conveyancer only releases keys once they receive your purchase money, and your conveyancer can only send it once your sale money has arrived. A delay anywhere below you in the chain pushes your key release back.
What if completion does not happen on the day?
Occasionally banking problems or a late payment further down the chain mean completion slips to the next working day. The contracts usually allow interest to be charged for late completion. Your conveyancer will keep you informed and explain the effect. Having a backup plan for an overnight stay helps.
Should I avoid completing on a Friday?
Many people prefer to avoid Fridays, because a delay late in the day can mean completion does not happen until the following Monday, leaving people without keys over the weekend. Fridays are still common and usually go smoothly. Your conveyancer can tell you how busy the chosen date might be.
Can I leave things in my old home for the buyer?
Only items agreed on the fittings and contents form should be left behind. Anything else, including rubbish, old furniture and garden items, should be removed unless the buyer agrees otherwise in writing. Leaving unwanted belongings can lead to complaints or a claim for clearance costs, and it can sour relations in the chain.

Yes. Most conveyancing is now done remotely by phone, email and online portals, so it does not matter if your current home and your next home are in different parts of England and Wales. Mention both locations on our form so the firm knows what is involved, such as any local searches or leasehold issues.

The firm usually contacts you within one working day, and often sooner. If both offers are already agreed and the estate agents are asking for your conveyancer's details, say so on the form so the firm knows the matter is ready to start straight away.
What is the difference between a remortgage and a product transfer?
A product transfer is when you take a new deal with your existing lender. The mortgage stays the same legal charge, so usually no conveyancer is involved. A remortgage is when you move to a new lender, or sometimes borrow more in a way that needs a new deed. That requires legal work to pay off the old loan and register the new one.
Who does the conveyancer act for in a remortgage?
The conveyancer acts for the new lender, making sure its mortgage is properly secured, and usually also for you. If the lender's own panel firm is doing free legal work, it may act mainly for the lender. If you choose your own firm, it acts for you and the lender, and must be on the lender's panel.
Do I need searches for a remortgage?
Not always. Many lenders accept a remortgage search insurance policy instead of full searches, which is quicker and cheaper. Some lenders, or some properties, still require searches. Your conveyancer will follow the lender's instructions and tell you if searches are needed.
Can I remortgage to release money from my home?
Yes, many people borrow more when they remortgage, sometimes called equity release in the general sense, though that term also describes specialist lifetime mortgages for older homeowners. Your lender will decide how much you can borrow. Your conveyancer handles the legal side and pays you the extra money on completion.
Is a lifetime mortgage the same as a remortgage?
No. A lifetime mortgage is a specialist form of equity release usually for older homeowners, with its own rules and protections. Lenders normally require you to have independent legal advice from a solicitor before it completes. It is quite different from a standard remortgage and needs specialist advice.
Can I remortgage a property I own outright?
Yes. If you own your home without a mortgage and want to borrow against it, the process is similar but there is no existing lender to pay off. Your conveyancer checks the title, completes the new mortgage and registers it, then sends you the money.
Will I get the extra money on the day of completion?
If you are borrowing more than you owe, your conveyancer receives the full advance from the new lender, pays off the old mortgage and any debts the lender requires to be cleared, deducts agreed fees and sends you the rest. This usually happens on completion day or the next working day, to an account you have confirmed by phone.
How long does a remortgage take?
A straightforward remortgage often takes a few weeks from the mortgage offer to completion. It can take longer if there are title problems, consents to obtain, a leasehold notice process or an equity loan involved. Starting well before your current deal ends gives room for any delays.
