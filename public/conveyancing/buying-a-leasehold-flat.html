About buying a leasehold flat

When you buy a leasehold flat, you are buying the right to live there for the remaining years of the lease. Your conveyancer checks the lease carefully, including how long is left, the ground rent, the service charge, and what you can and can’t do.

They also get information from the freeholder or managing agent about service charges, planned works and any disputes. A short lease can affect your mortgage and the value of the flat, so the firm will flag it early.

A good conveyancer keeps you updated without you having to chase, explains anything unusual in the paperwork in plain English and works with your estate agent and mortgage broker to keep the chain moving. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they work and what they need from you to get started.

Leasehold conveyancing in brief

Leasehold conveyancing needs extra care, because the lease sets what you can do with the flat and what you will pay. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm that checks the lease length, ground rent, service charges and management pack before you commit.

If the lease is short, ask whether the seller will start a lease extension before completion. Your conveyancer can explain the difference between leasehold and freehold and how the lease affects value and mortgage options.

Buying a leasehold flat explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Most flats in England and Wales are sold on a long lease. When you buy one, you are not buying the building or the land beneath it. You are buying the right to occupy your flat for a fixed number of years, on terms set out in the lease. Someone else, usually called the freeholder or landlord, owns the building and has the right to collect ground rent and, in most cases, service charges.

That makes buying a flat a different job for your conveyancer. As well as the usual checks on the title and searches, they need to read the lease line by line, find out how the building is run and what it costs, and check that nothing in the lease or the building would worry you or your lender. A lease can run to dozens of pages, and the important parts are not always obvious.

The lease is the rulebook for your home. It tells you what you must pay, what you must repair, what you can and cannot do, and what the freeholder must do in return. It can affect whether you can let the flat, keep a pet, change the flooring or knock through a wall. It also affects the value of the flat, especially as the number of years left gets shorter.

Many buyers find the leasehold part of a purchase the hardest to follow. A good conveyancer will explain what the lease says in plain language and point out anything unusual, so that you can decide whether you are comfortable before you exchange.

Kinds of leasehold flat

  • Flats in purpose built blocks run by a managing agent
  • Flats in converted houses, sometimes with only two or three flats
  • Flats with a share of the freehold, where the owners together own the building
  • Flats in blocks where the leaseholders have taken over management through right to manage
  • Retirement flats with age limits and extra services
  • Shared ownership flats owned partly by a housing provider

Leasehold purchases involve extra checks at almost every stage. Here is a general picture of what a conveyancer usually looks at. Your firm will tell you what applies to the flat you are buying.

The lease

Your conveyancer checks the length of the lease, the ground rent and how it can increase, the service charge provisions, who repairs and insures what, and the restrictions on how you use the flat. They also check the lease works properly, for example that repair and insurance duties cover the whole building.

The management information

The seller's conveyancer usually orders a management pack from the managing agent or freeholder. This should show service charge accounts and budgets, the reserve fund, buildings insurance, planned major works, any disputes and the fees for notices on completion. A standard form called the LPE1 is often used.

Building safety

For many blocks, your conveyancer will ask about fire safety, cladding and any remediation works. The Building Safety Act 2022 gives some leaseholders protection from certain repair costs, and there are certificates the seller and landlord may need to provide. Lenders may have their own requirements for taller buildings.

The freeholder and managing agent

Your conveyancer checks who owns the freehold, whether there is a management company that is a party to the lease and whether you will need to become a member or shareholder. For share of freehold flats, they check the freehold title and the company that holds it.

Consents and notices

Some leases require the freeholder's consent before a sale or a mortgage, or require the buyer to sign a deed of covenant promising to keep to the lease. After completion, your conveyancer usually serves notice of the purchase and of your mortgage on the freeholder or agent and pays the fee for registering them.

Your plans for the flat

Tell your conveyancer early what you plan to do with the flat. If you want to let it, renovate it, work from home or keep a dog, the firm can look for the parts of the lease that matter to you and raise questions with the freeholder or managing agent before you are committed. It is much easier to ask for consent or clarification before exchange than after.

Service charges pay for running and maintaining the building. They usually cover things like cleaning, lighting, lifts, gardens, buildings insurance, management fees and repairs to the structure, roof and shared areas. They can be a significant part of the cost of owning a flat, so it is worth understanding them before you buy.

How they are worked out

The lease says what can be charged and what share each flat pays. Many blocks set a budget at the start of the year, collect payments in advance and then balance the account at the end of the year. If costs were higher than expected, leaseholders may receive an extra bill.

Reserve funds

Some leases allow the freeholder to collect money towards future major works, such as roof replacement or redecoration, so that costs are spread over time. A healthy reserve fund can reduce the risk of a large one off bill. Not every lease allows a reserve fund.

Major works

For larger works, the freeholder usually has to consult leaseholders before carrying them out, following a set procedure under section 20 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985. If works have been planned or consulted on, the management pack should say so. Your conveyancer may ask for a retention from the seller where a known bill is due.

Arrears and apportionment

Service charges are usually shared out between buyer and seller on the day of completion, so each pays for the time they own the flat. Your conveyancer checks there are no arrears, as unpaid charges can become a problem for the new owner.

Ground rent

Older leases may have a ground rent that rises over time, sometimes by doubling at set intervals. Lenders can be wary of high or fast rising ground rents. Most new long residential leases granted since mid 2022 can only charge a nominal ground rent, but that rule does not change existing leases.

The number of years left on a lease is one of the most important things about a flat. As the lease gets shorter, the flat can become harder to mortgage and sell, and extending the lease can become more expensive.

Why lenders care

Lenders usually want a certain number of years to be left on the lease when the mortgage ends. Each lender sets its own minimum. If the lease is too short, the lender may refuse to lend or require it to be extended before completion. Your conveyancer will check your lender's requirements.

Extending the lease

Leaseholders of flats usually have a legal right to a lease extension. Under the long standing rules, a statutory extension adds 90 years to the remaining term and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn. The freeholder is paid a premium, worked out on a set formula. Some freeholders will also agree an informal extension on different terms.

The 80 year point

Under the current valuation rules, extending a lease with fewer than 80 years left has usually cost more because an extra element called marriage value is added to the premium. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 provides for changes to how premiums are calculated, but not all of it is in force. Check the current position with your conveyancer.

Buying with an extension under way

If the lease is short, the seller can sometimes start a statutory extension claim before the sale and assign the benefit of it to you on completion. Historically this helped because a new owner had to wait two years before making their own claim. The 2024 Act has changed this rule and others, and your conveyancer will explain what applies now.

Share of freehold

Where the leaseholders own the freehold together, they can often agree to extend leases between themselves. The leases still need to be formally extended and registered, so do not assume a share of freehold flat has a long lease.

Leasehold purchases usually cost more in legal fees than freehold ones, because there is much more to read and more people to deal with. There are also fees charged by the freeholder and managing agent.

Legal fee

Most firms charge a fixed fee with a leasehold supplement. There may be further charges for a share of freehold, a lease extension running alongside the purchase or a deed of variation to correct a defective lease.

Management and notice fees

The managing agent charges for the management pack, which is often paid by the seller but sometimes passed on. On completion, the freeholder or agent usually charges fees for registering notice of your purchase and mortgage, and sometimes for a deed of covenant or certificate of compliance.

Disbursements

These include searches, the HM Land Registry fee, identity check fees and bank transfer fees.

Tax

Stamp Duty Land Tax in England or Land Transaction Tax in Wales is worked out on the price. Rates and reliefs change, so check GOV.UK or ask your conveyancer.

Running costs

  • Service charge, usually paid in advance
  • Ground rent, unless the lease is at a peppercorn
  • Your share of any major works
  • Contents insurance, as the building is usually insured by the freeholder
  • Lease extension costs in future if the lease is getting shorter

Leasehold issues are one of the most common reasons flat purchases are delayed. Many can be resolved with time, but it helps to know where the problems usually come from.

Slow management packs

Managing agents can take weeks to return the pack, and replies to further questions can take longer. Some agents charge for every extra answer. Starting early helps.

Short leases

A short lease may stop a lender lending and can affect value. If the lease needs extending, it can add time and cost to the purchase.

Defective leases

Some older leases have gaps, such as nobody being responsible for repairing part of the building, or service charge shares that do not add up. Lenders may require these to be fixed by a deed of variation or covered by insurance.

Absent or unresponsive freeholders

If the freeholder cannot be found or does not reply, getting consents or information can be hard. There are legal routes in some cases, but they take time.

Building safety

Questions about cladding, fire safety or remediation can delay a sale while certificates and information are gathered. Some lenders will not lend until they have the documents they need.

Unexpected bills

A large major works bill or a sharp rise in service charges can arrive soon after you move in. Reading the management pack carefully and asking about planned works before exchange is the best protection.

Breaches by the seller

Sometimes a seller has made alterations without consent, let the flat in breach of the lease or fallen behind with charges. These breaches can pass to you as the new owner. Your conveyancer will ask about them and may suggest that the seller obtains retrospective consent or that the issue is otherwise resolved before completion.

Buying a leasehold flat questions and answers

What should I check when buying a leasehold flat?
The years left on the lease, the ground rent, the service charge and any major works, the rules in the lease and who manages the building. Your conveyancer reports on each.
How many years should be left on a lease?
Many lenders want a minimum term remaining when the mortgage ends. Leases with fewer than 80 years left can be more expensive to extend. Your conveyancer will explain what it means for you.
What is a management pack?
Information from the freeholder or managing agent about service charges, insurance, planned works and any disputes. There is usually a fee for it.
Can the seller extend the lease before I buy?
Sometimes. The seller can start a statutory lease extension and pass the benefit to you, which your conveyancer can arrange.
Does Leaflegal do the conveyancing?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service. The firm we introduce you to does the legal work and is responsible for it.
Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
What does leasehold actually mean?
It means you own the flat for a fixed period, set out in the lease, rather than owning it forever. The freeholder owns the building and the land. The lease sets out your rights and duties, including what you pay, what you repair and what you can do in the flat. When the lease runs out, the flat returns to the freeholder, which is why lease length matters so much.
What will my conveyancer look for in the lease?
They check the length, ground rent, service charge rules, repair and insurance duties and restrictions on use. They look for anything unusual, such as fast rising ground rent, bans on letting or pets, or gaps in who repairs what. They also check the lease meets your lender's requirements. You should receive a report explaining the main points in plain language.
Can the lease stop me keeping a pet or letting the flat?
Yes. Many leases restrict pets, letting, short lets, running a business from the flat or making alterations. Some ban these outright and some require the freeholder's consent. If any of these matter to you, tell your conveyancer at the start so they can check the lease and raise questions before you exchange.
What is a deed of covenant?
It is a document some leases require a buyer to sign, promising the freeholder or management company that they will keep to the terms of the lease. Your conveyancer will prepare or check it and deal with the fee for registering it. It is a routine part of many leasehold purchases.
What is a share of freehold?
It means the flat owners together own the freehold of the building, usually through a company or as joint owners. You still have a lease, but you also have a say in how the building is run. Your conveyancer checks both the lease and the freehold arrangements, and will explain your role in the company if there is one.
Can I make changes to the flat?
It depends on the lease. Many leases allow internal decoration but need consent for structural changes, moving kitchens or bathrooms, or replacing carpets with hard flooring. If you plan any works, ask your conveyancer to check the lease. Works done without consent can cause problems with the freeholder and when you sell.
What if the lease has a mistake in it?
Some older leases have errors or gaps, such as unclear repair duties or service charge shares that do not add up to the whole. Lenders may insist on a fix, which usually means a deed of variation agreed with the freeholder, or in some cases insurance. Your conveyancer will explain what the lender requires and how long it might take.
What is forfeiture?
Forfeiture is a right some leases give the freeholder to end the lease if the leaseholder seriously breaks its terms, for example by not paying charges. There are legal protections for leaseholders, and freeholders usually have to follow set steps and often go to court or a tribunal first. It is rare in practice, but it shows why keeping to the lease and paying charges on time matters. Your conveyancer will point out the forfeiture clause in their report.
What is a management pack?
It is a bundle of information from the managing agent or freeholder about the building and the charges. It usually covers service charge accounts and budgets, the reserve fund, buildings insurance, planned works, disputes and the fees payable on sale. A standard form called the LPE1 is often used. There is a charge for it, usually paid by the seller.
How do I know if a big bill is coming?
The management pack should mention planned major works and any section 20 consultation that has started. Your conveyancer will also ask the seller and managing agent directly. If a known bill is due, buyer and seller sometimes agree that money is held back from the seller's proceeds to cover it. Unexpected works can still arise, so a healthy reserve fund is a good sign.
Who insures the building?
Usually the freeholder insures the building and recovers the premium through the service charge. Your conveyancer checks the insurance is in place and suitable for your lender. You will usually still want contents insurance for your own belongings. For some converted houses or share of freehold flats, the arrangements can be different.
What is right to manage?
It is a legal right that lets qualifying leaseholders take over the management of their building from the freeholder by forming a company. If the building is already managed this way, your conveyancer will explain how the company works and whether you can become a member. If you are interested in using the right in future, a specialist can advise.
Can I challenge service charges?
Leaseholders have legal rights to challenge charges they think are unreasonable or not allowed by the lease, often through the First tier Tribunal (Property Chamber). Your conveyancer checks the charges as part of the purchase, but disputes after you own the flat are a separate matter for a specialist.
What is ground rent and do I still have to pay it?
Ground rent is a payment to the freeholder set out in the lease. On many older leases it continues to apply and may rise over time. Most new long residential leases granted since mid 2022 can only charge a nominal ground rent, but existing leases are not changed by that rule. Your conveyancer will tell you what the lease says and whether the rent could worry a lender.
Who pays the service charge for the current year?
Service charges, and ground rent where it applies, are usually shared between buyer and seller on the completion date, so each pays for the time they own the flat. If the seller has paid in advance, you may reimburse them at completion. If there are end of year balancing charges still to come, your conveyancer may agree a retention from the seller's money. The figures appear in the completion statement.
How many years should be left on the lease?
There is no single answer. Each lender has its own minimum, often linked to how many years will be left when the mortgage ends. Value can also be affected as a lease gets shorter. Your conveyancer will check what your lender needs and tell you if the lease is short enough to cause concern.
Why is 80 years important?
Under the current valuation rules, extending a lease with fewer than 80 years left usually costs more, because marriage value is added to the premium. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 provides for changes to this, but not all of the Act is in force yet. Your conveyancer will explain what applies when you buy.
How does a statutory lease extension work?
Under the long standing rules, a qualifying flat owner can serve a formal notice on the freeholder asking for a new lease. The extension usually adds 90 years to the remaining term and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn, in return for a premium. A surveyor values the premium and solicitors deal with the paperwork. The 2024 Act is set to change some of these rules.
Can the seller extend the lease before I buy?
Sometimes. The seller can start a statutory claim by serving notice and then assign the benefit of that notice to you on completion, so the extension carries on in your name. Alternatively the seller can finish the extension before the sale. Your conveyancer will explain how this would affect the timetable and costs.
Do I have to wait before extending the lease myself?
In the past, a new owner usually had to own the flat for two years before making a statutory claim. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 has changed this and other eligibility rules. Your conveyancer will explain the current position and whether it makes a difference to your plans.
Does a short lease mean I should not buy?
Not necessarily. Some buyers choose a flat with a shorter lease because the price reflects it, and then extend the lease. Others prefer to avoid the extra cost and uncertainty. It depends on your lender, your budget and your plans. Your conveyancer can explain the legal position, and a specialist surveyor can estimate what an extension might cost, but the decision is yours.
Why will my conveyancer ask about cladding and fire safety?
Since the Grenfell Tower fire, lenders and buyers want to know about fire safety in blocks of flats. Your conveyancer will ask the seller and managing agent about the external walls, any remediation works and any certificates. The answers can affect whether a lender will lend and whether costs could fall on leaseholders.
What protection does the Building Safety Act give leaseholders?
The Building Safety Act 2022 limits or removes what some leaseholders can be charged for fixing certain historical building safety defects. Whether a lease qualifies depends on things like the height of the building and how many properties the leaseholder owns. Your conveyancer will check what information is available and explain how the rules may apply.
What is a leaseholder deed of certificate?
It is a document the seller completes to show whether their lease qualifies for protection under the Building Safety Act. The freeholder may also provide a landlord's certificate. These help show who is responsible for paying for certain safety works. Your conveyancer will ask for them where they apply.
What is an EWS1 form?
It is a form used to record an assessment of the external walls of a residential building, created so lenders could value flats in buildings with possible cladding concerns. Not every building needs one, and lender requirements have changed over time. Your conveyancer and lender will tell you whether one is needed for the flat you are buying.
Can I still get a mortgage on a flat with building safety issues?
Sometimes. Lender attitudes vary and have changed over time. Some lend once remediation is planned or funded, while others want works finished first or particular certificates in place. Your mortgage broker can tell you which lenders may help, and your conveyancer will pass on whatever information the lender asks for. Building safety questions can add time to a purchase, so it helps to raise them early.
Why do leasehold purchases take longer?
There is more information to gather and more parties involved. Managing agents can be slow to supply the pack, the lease may need careful review and building safety questions can take time to answer. If the lease needs extending or correcting, that adds further time. Your conveyancer will keep you updated and tell you where any delay is coming from.
Do I need a specialist leasehold conveyancer?
Any regulated conveyancer can handle a leasehold purchase, but experience counts. Ask how many leasehold flats the firm handles, whether it deals with share of freehold and building safety questions, and whether it can handle a lease extension alongside the purchase. Check the firm is on your lender's panel too.

How quickly will the firm contact me?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, often sooner. It helps to have the address, price, anything the agent has told you about the lease and your mortgage details ready when they call.
Can I instruct a conveyancer before I find a property?
Yes. Many buyers choose a firm while they are still looking, so that the firm can carry out identity and money checks early. When an offer is accepted, the estate agent can send the memorandum of sale straight away and the work can begin without delay. This can make your offer more attractive to a seller.
What will my conveyancer need from me at the start?
Expect to provide photo ID, proof of address, details of your mortgage broker or lender, and evidence of where your deposit and any other funds are coming from. If family are giving you money, the firm will need their details too. You will also sign the firm's terms of business and pay money on account for searches.
What is a memorandum of sale?
It is a document the estate agent sends to both conveyancers once an offer is accepted. It sets out the agreed price, the names of the buyer and seller, the property address and the details of each side's conveyancer. It is not a contract and does not commit anyone, but it starts the legal process.
Does the seller's conveyancer act for me too?
No. The seller's conveyancer acts only for the seller. Your own conveyancer acts for you and, if you have a mortgage, usually for your lender as well. Each side's conveyancer looks after their own client's interests, which is why questions pass between them in writing.
Can I buy a property with someone who is not my partner?
Yes. Friends, siblings and parents and children buy together quite often. It is common in these situations to own as tenants in common and to record who has paid what in a declaration of trust, along with what happens if one person wants to sell. Your conveyancer can explain how this works.
Can I buy without a mortgage?
Yes. Cash buyers still need the full legal checks, including the title and searches, because the risks to the buyer are the same. Searches are optional without a lender, but skipping them means you may not learn about issues until later. Cash purchases can be quicker because there is no mortgage offer to wait for.
What if the property is being sold by someone who has died?
When a property is sold by the executors of someone who has died, the executors usually need a grant of probate, or letters of administration, before the sale can complete. Your conveyancer will check who has authority to sell and whether the grant has been issued. Probate sales can take longer, and the sellers may know less about the property's history.
Which searches will my conveyancer order?
Most buyers with a mortgage will have a local authority search, a water and drainage search and an environmental search. Depending on the area, your conveyancer may add others, such as a mining search, a chancel repair check or a flood report. They will explain which searches they recommend and why.
