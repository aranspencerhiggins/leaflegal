About first time buyers

Buying your first home involves a lot of new language, such as searches, enquiries, exchange and completion. A good conveyancer explains each step in plain English and tells you what they need from you and when.

First time buyers may qualify for relief on Stamp Duty Land Tax, and some use schemes such as shared ownership or a Lifetime ISA. Your conveyancer checks how these apply and makes sure the paperwork is right.

A good conveyancer keeps you updated without you having to chase, explains anything unusual in the paperwork in plain English and works with your estate agent and mortgage broker to keep the chain moving. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they work and what they need from you to get started.

First time buyers explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Buying your first home is exciting, but the legal side can feel like a foreign language. Words like title, searches, enquiries, exchange and completion get used as if everyone already knows what they mean. A conveyancer is the person who does the legal work to transfer the property into your name, and a good one will explain each stage in plain English as you go.

As a first time buyer you are usually in a strong position. You have no home to sell, so you are not waiting on a buyer of your own. Sellers and estate agents often like that, because it means one less link in the chain that could break. It also means the timetable depends mostly on your mortgage, the searches and the seller's side.

What you may not have is experience. You might not know what questions to ask, which documents to keep, or what is normal and what is a warning sign. That is where the right firm makes a difference. They should tell you what they need from you, when they need it, and what they have found in the paperwork before you are committed.

What makes a first purchase different

  • You may be using a scheme such as shared ownership, a Lifetime ISA or a deposit gifted by family
  • You may qualify for first time buyer relief on Stamp Duty Land Tax in England
  • Your lender will want to see where every part of your deposit came from
  • You may be renting, so the end of your tenancy needs to line up with completion
  • You will be signing a mortgage deed and a transfer for the first time

Leaflegal does not give legal advice. We introduce you to one regulated conveyancing firm that handles first purchases, and they look at your own situation and explain your options.

No two purchases are identical, but most first time buyers in England and Wales go through the same broad stages. Knowing the order helps you plan, and it helps you understand why some weeks feel busy and others feel quiet.

1. Offer accepted and firm instructed

Once the seller accepts your offer, the estate agent sends a memorandum of sale to both conveyancers. You will receive a client care letter, terms of business and an estimate of costs. The firm will also carry out identity checks and ask about the source of your deposit before it can start work properly.

2. Contract pack and searches

The seller's conveyancer sends a draft contract, a copy of the title and forms completed by the seller about the property. Your conveyancer orders searches from the local authority, the water company and other sources. These can reveal things like planning history, roads, drainage, flood risk and nearby developments.

3. Enquiries

Your conveyancer reads everything and raises questions with the seller's side about anything missing, unclear or worrying. This is often the longest stage. It can involve chasing guarantees, building regulation approvals, boundary details or information about a shared driveway.

4. Mortgage offer and survey

While the legal work goes on, your lender values the property and issues a mortgage offer. Many first time buyers also commission their own survey, which is separate from the lender's valuation and tells you much more about the condition of the building. Your conveyancer will check the mortgage offer conditions and tell you if anything in it affects the purchase.

5. Report and signing

Before exchange, your conveyancer sends a report on the title, the searches and the replies to enquiries. You then sign the contract, the transfer if needed and the mortgage deed. Read the report carefully and ask about anything you do not understand. This is your chance to raise concerns before you are legally bound.

6. Exchange of contracts

At exchange, you and the seller become legally committed and a completion date is fixed. You pay your deposit at this point. If you pull out after exchange, you can lose that deposit and may face other claims, so exchange is a big moment.

7. Completion and registration

On completion day, your lender sends the mortgage money to your conveyancer, who sends the full price to the seller's conveyancer. Once the money arrives, the keys are released. Afterwards your conveyancer pays any Stamp Duty Land Tax due and registers you as the owner at HM Land Registry.

Most first time buyers put together their deposit from more than one place. Each source has its own paperwork, and your conveyancer has to be satisfied about where the money came from before it can accept it. Telling the firm early about every source avoids last minute delays.

Savings

Money you have saved yourself is the simplest source, but you will still be asked for bank statements showing how it built up. Large or unusual deposits into your account may need explaining. This is part of the anti money laundering checks every regulated firm has to carry out.

Gifted deposits

Many first time buyers receive help from parents or other relatives. Lenders usually want a signed letter confirming the money is a gift and not a loan, and that the giver will not have a share in the property. Your conveyancer will often need identity documents and bank statements from the person giving the money too. If the money is really a loan, or the giver expects something back, tell the firm, as it changes things.

Lifetime ISA

If you are using a Lifetime ISA, your conveyancer usually has to request the money from the ISA provider on your behalf, and there are rules about timing and the purchase price. The firm will need to know which provider you use well before completion. The rules can change, so check the current position on GOV.UK.

Shared ownership

With shared ownership you buy a share of the property and pay rent on the rest to a housing provider. The property is almost always leasehold, and the lease contains rules about selling, subletting and buying more shares later, which is called staircasing. Your conveyancer will explain the lease and deal with the housing provider as well as the seller.

Joint buyers

If you are buying with a partner, friend or relative, you will need to decide how you own the property. Joint tenants own it together as a whole, and tenants in common each own a share that can be unequal. A declaration of trust can record who paid what. Your conveyancer will explain the difference, and this decision matters if you later separate or one of you dies.

The total cost of buying your first home goes well beyond the deposit. It helps to understand the different parts so that nothing comes as a surprise near the end.

Legal fee

This is what the firm charges for its own work. Many conveyancers quote a fixed fee for a straightforward purchase, with extra charges for things like a leasehold property, a shared ownership purchase, a Lifetime ISA or a gifted deposit. Ask what is included and what would cost more.

Disbursements

These are costs the firm pays to others on your behalf. They usually include search fees, the HM Land Registry fee, bank transfer fees and electronic identity check fees. They are normally listed separately from the legal fee.

Stamp Duty Land Tax

In England, first time buyers may qualify for relief, which reduces or removes the tax on cheaper homes. The thresholds and rules have changed several times and may change again, so check GOV.UK or ask your conveyancer for the current position. In Wales, Land Transaction Tax applies instead and has its own rates and rules.

Other costs to budget for

  • A survey, if you choose to have one
  • Mortgage arrangement or broker fees
  • Buildings insurance from exchange of contracts
  • Removal costs and any overlap with your rent
  • Service charge and ground rent for a leasehold flat

Most first purchases go through without drama, but some issues come up again and again. Knowing about them in advance helps you stay calm if one appears.

Slow progress up the chain

Even though you have nothing to sell, the seller might be buying another home, and that seller might be buying too. A delay anywhere in the chain can hold everyone up. Your conveyancer can tell you where the hold up is, but cannot force other people's transactions to move faster.

Deposit paperwork

Missing bank statements or a late gift letter are among the most common causes of delay for first time buyers. Gather the documents early and reply quickly when the firm asks for something.

Mortgage offer conditions

Some offers include conditions, such as a retention until repairs are done, or a requirement to pay off a loan before completion. Your conveyancer will flag these, but it is worth reading the offer yourself as soon as it arrives.

Survey findings

A survey might reveal damp, roof problems or alterations done without approval. You may want to get quotes, ask questions or renegotiate. Your conveyancer can raise legal questions about the issue, while the practical decisions are yours.

Rental notice

If you rent, giving notice too early can leave you without a home, and giving it too late can mean paying rent and a mortgage together. Many people wait until exchange before giving notice, but it depends on your tenancy terms.

The conveyancer you choose will be your guide through one of the biggest purchases of your life. Price matters, but so does how easy they are to reach and how clearly they explain things.

Lender panel

If you are buying with a mortgage, your conveyancer usually needs to be on your lender's panel. If they are not, you may need a second firm to act for the lender, which adds cost and time. Ask early.

Communication

Ask how the firm will keep you updated. Some have online portals, some prefer email and some will call. Find out who will handle your file day to day and how quickly they usually reply.

Experience with your situation

If you are using shared ownership, a Lifetime ISA, a gifted deposit or buying a leasehold flat, ask whether the firm deals with these regularly. Experience with the paperwork can save weeks.

Regulation

Conveyancing firms are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority or the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Every firm Leaflegal introduces is regulated, and you can check its details on the regulator's website.

You are free to choose any conveyancer you like. You do not have to use one suggested by an estate agent, a lender or a developer.

Fees and costs

Most conveyancing firms quote a fixed legal fee for the transaction, plus disbursements. Disbursements are costs the firm pays on your behalf, such as searches, Land Registry fees, identity checks and bank transfer fees. If you are buying, Stamp Duty Land Tax may also be due, and the firm usually pays it for you from the money you send them.

Leasehold work, new builds, shared ownership and buying with a company usually cost more because there is more to check. A good firm puts everything in writing before you instruct them, so you can compare quotes like for like and avoid surprises at completion.

First time buyers questions and answers

Do first time buyers pay less for conveyancing?
Not usually, but some firms offer packages for first time buyers. Extra work such as a Lifetime ISA, a gifted deposit or shared ownership can add to the fee, so ask for a full quote.
What does a first time buyer solicitor need from me?
Your ID, the property details, your mortgage in principle or offer and details of any scheme or gift you are using.
What does a conveyancer do for a first time buyer?
The same legal work as for any purchase, but with more explanation along the way. The firm checks the property, reports to you and handles the money and registration.
Will I pay Stamp Duty?
It depends on the price and whether you qualify for first time buyer relief. Your conveyancer will confirm what is due.
How long will it take?
Many purchases take a few months from offer to completion. The length of the chain, searches and mortgage offer all make a difference.
Does Leaflegal do the conveyancing?
No. Leaflegal is a free introduction service. The firm we introduce you to does the legal work and is responsible for it.
Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
When should I look for a conveyancer?
Many first time buyers start looking as soon as they begin viewing properties, so that a firm is ready the moment an offer is accepted. Instructing early means the estate agent can send the memorandum of sale straight away and the firm can complete identity and deposit checks before the seller's paperwork arrives. You usually do not pay for legal work until the purchase is under way, but check the firm's terms about any upfront charges.
Do I need a solicitor or will a licensed conveyancer do?
Either can handle a residential purchase. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and licensed conveyancers by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Both must follow rules on client money, complaints and insurance. For a first time buyer, the more important questions are whether the firm is on your lender's panel, how clearly it explains things, and whether it has experience with any scheme you are using.
What will the firm ask me for at the start?
Expect to provide photo identification, proof of address and details of where your deposit is coming from. The firm will also want the estate agent's memorandum of sale, your mortgage in principle or offer, and details of any scheme such as a Lifetime ISA or shared ownership. If someone is giving you money towards the deposit, the firm may ask for their details at this stage too.
Can I buy a home on my own?
Yes. Many first time buyers buy alone. The legal process is the same as for joint buyers, though your mortgage will be based on your income alone. Some people buy with a guarantor or with a family member on the mortgage, and your conveyancer will explain how these arrangements work and whether anyone involved needs separate advice.
What does being chain free mean for me?
As a first time buyer you have no property to sell, so you are often described as chain free on your side. This can make your offer more attractive to sellers. It does not mean the purchase will be quick, because the seller may still be part of a chain above you. It simply means there is one fewer sale that needs to happen before yours can complete.
Is an offer legally binding once it is accepted?
No. In England and Wales, neither buyer nor seller is legally bound until contracts are exchanged. Until then either side can pull out, and the price can be renegotiated. This is why it is important to let your conveyancer finish the checks before you exchange, and why gazumping, where a seller accepts a higher offer late on, can still happen.
Does it matter whether the home is freehold or leasehold?
Yes. With a freehold you own the building and the land it stands on with no time limit. With a leasehold, which is common for flats, you own the home for a fixed number of years and usually pay a service charge and sometimes ground rent. Leasehold purchases need extra checks and the legal fee is often higher. Your conveyancer will explain what the tenure means for the property you are buying.
What if the seller pulls out before exchange?
Because nobody is bound until exchange, a seller can withdraw, and you may lose money already spent on searches, a survey or a mortgage fee. Some buyers take out insurance against this, sometimes called home buying protection, though it is optional. Your conveyancer cannot stop a seller pulling out, but will tell you what costs have been incurred so far and what can be reused if you find another home.
What are searches and why do I need them?
Searches are enquiries made with the local authority, the water company and other bodies about the property and the area around it. They can show planning decisions, road schemes, drainage arrangements, flood risk and other matters that might affect the home or its value. If you are borrowing, your lender will usually insist on them. Even cash buyers usually have them because the information is hard to get any other way.
What happens at exchange of contracts?
At exchange, your conveyancer and the seller's conveyancer swap signed contracts, usually by telephone using agreed procedures. From that moment you are both legally bound and the completion date is fixed. You pay your deposit at exchange. Before this point your conveyancer will have reported to you and checked that your mortgage money is ready. You should also have buildings insurance in place from exchange in most cases.
What is the difference between exchange and completion?
Exchange is when the deal becomes legally binding. Completion is when the money is paid and you become the owner and get the keys. There is usually a gap between the two, often a week or more, though it can be longer or shorter by agreement. The gap gives time to arrange removals, give notice on a rental and draw down the mortgage.
Do I need to sign anything in front of a witness?
Usually the mortgage deed and sometimes the transfer must be signed in front of an independent adult witness who is not a party to the purchase and ideally not a family member. Your conveyancer will explain exactly who can act as a witness and how documents should be signed. Some firms and lenders now accept electronic signatures for certain documents.
Will I need a survey as well as the lender's valuation?
The lender's valuation is for the lender's benefit and mainly checks that the property is worth what you are borrowing against it. It is not a detailed inspection. A survey is something you commission yourself to learn more about the condition of the home. Whether to have one and what level to choose is your decision, and your conveyancer can explain how survey findings fit into the legal process.
How long does the legal work usually take?
Many purchases take a few months from offer to completion, but it varies widely. Searches, the seller's speed in returning forms, mortgage processing and the length of the chain all play a part. A purchase with no chain and prompt replies can be quicker. Your conveyancer can give you an idea of timing once they see the paperwork.
What happens after I get the keys?
Your conveyancer pays any Stamp Duty Land Tax due and files the return, then applies to HM Land Registry to register you as the owner and record your lender's mortgage. Registration can take some time after you move in. Once complete, the firm will usually send you a copy of the updated title. Keep this and your completion paperwork somewhere safe.
Will I pay Stamp Duty as a first time buyer?
In England, first time buyers may qualify for relief that reduces or removes Stamp Duty Land Tax below certain price levels. The thresholds have changed more than once, so check GOV.UK for the current figures. Relief only applies if every buyer is a first time buyer and the home will be your main residence. Your conveyancer will confirm what is due and file the return for you.
What about buying a home in Wales?
In Wales, Land Transaction Tax applies instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax. It has its own rates and thresholds and is collected by the Welsh Revenue Authority. The rules for first time buyers are different from those in England, so it is worth asking your conveyancer to explain what applies to the property you are buying.
Why does the firm need to know where my deposit came from?
Conveyancers are required by law to carry out anti money laundering checks. They must understand where purchase money has come from before they accept it. This normally means providing bank statements covering a period of months, and explaining any large deposits. Lenders have their own requirements too. These checks apply to everyone, so they are nothing personal.
When do I pay my deposit?
You transfer the deposit to your conveyancer shortly before exchange of contracts, so it is ready to be sent on when you exchange. Traditionally the deposit paid at exchange was ten per cent of the price, but a lower amount is often agreed, especially when the buyer has a smaller mortgage deposit. Your conveyancer will tell you the amount and deadline.
How do I send money to my conveyancer safely?
Conveyancing fraud is a real risk. Criminals sometimes send fake emails pretending to be from a law firm with changed bank details. Always confirm the firm's bank details by phone, using a number you know to be genuine, before sending money. A reputable firm will never change its bank details by email, and it will warn you about this at the start.
What other costs should I budget for?
Besides the deposit, legal fee and any tax, think about survey fees, mortgage fees, buildings insurance, removals and any overlap with your rent. If the home is leasehold, you may have service charges, ground rent and fees for the management pack. Your conveyancer's estimate will list the legal costs, but other costs come from different sources.
Can I use a Lifetime ISA for my first home?
Many first time buyers do. The conveyancer usually has to request the funds from your ISA provider before completion, and there are rules on the purchase price, timing and how the money is paid. If the rules are not met, you can face a charge for withdrawing. Tell your conveyancer as early as possible and check the current rules on GOV.UK, as they can change.
My parents are giving me money. What will the firm need?
Usually a signed gift letter confirming the money is a gift, that it does not have to be repaid and that the giver will not have any interest in the property. The firm will often also ask for identity documents and bank statements from the giver. If the arrangement is not a pure gift, for example if your parents want a share or expect repayment, tell your conveyancer, as it changes how things are documented.
How does buying through shared ownership work legally?
You buy a lease of the whole property but own only a share, paying rent to the housing provider on the rest. The lease sets out your rights, the rent, the service charge, how you can buy more shares and what happens if you want to sell. Your conveyancer will review the lease and the housing provider's requirements and explain how it differs from buying outright.
Can a family member help without giving me a gift?
Some lenders offer arrangements where a family member's savings or property act as extra security, or where a family member is named on the mortgage without owning the home. These can carry risks for the family member, who may need independent legal advice. Your conveyancer and mortgage adviser will explain how any arrangement you are considering affects the legal work.
What if I am buying with a friend rather than a partner?
Buying with a friend is legally the same as buying with a partner, but you may want to think more about what happens if one of you wants to sell or stops paying. Many friends choose to own as tenants in common and record their shares in a declaration of trust. Your conveyancer can explain the options, though the decision about how to own is yours.
How will I know what is happening with my purchase?
Ask the firm at the start how it communicates. Many use online case tracking, while others send updates by email at each stage. It helps to know who is handling your file and how quickly they usually reply. If you have not heard anything for a while, it is fine to ask for an update, though quiet periods often mean the firm is waiting on searches or the seller.
Can I choose my own conveyancer if the estate agent recommends one?
Yes. You are always free to choose your own firm. Estate agents sometimes receive a referral fee for recommending a conveyancer, and they should tell you if so. Whoever you choose, check that the firm is regulated and on your lender's panel.
What if I am not happy with the service?
Every regulated firm must have a complaints procedure, and you should receive details in your client care letter. Raise any concern with the person handling your matter first, then follow the firm's procedure if needed. If the complaint is not resolved, you may be able to take it to the Legal Ombudsman.

How quickly will the firm contact me?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, often sooner. They will normally ask a few questions about the property, the price and your mortgage, and then send you a quote and their terms.
Is buying a rental property different from buying a home?
The core legal work is the same. Your conveyancer checks the title, orders searches, raises enquiries and handles the money. The difference is in the extra checks around letting, such as lease restrictions, licensing, existing tenancies and the conditions in a buy to let mortgage. The firm will also need to know from the start whether you are buying personally or through a company.
Do I need a buy to let mortgage?
If you plan to let the property and are borrowing, you will usually need a mortgage designed for letting. A standard residential mortgage normally forbids letting without the lender's consent. Letting in breach of your mortgage terms can have serious consequences. Your mortgage broker can explain the options, and your conveyancer will check the offer conditions.
Can I let my old home instead of selling it?
Many people do, sometimes called being an accidental landlord. You will usually need your lender's consent to let, or a new buy to let mortgage. Buying your next home while keeping the old one can mean paying higher rates of Stamp Duty Land Tax. Your conveyancer can explain how the tax applies, and a tax adviser can help with the wider picture.
Can I buy a property at auction to let?
Yes, but auction purchases move quickly. When the hammer falls, contracts are usually exchanged and completion often follows within a short fixed period. That means the legal checks need to happen before the auction. A conveyancer can review the legal pack for you in advance, and you will need your finance ready in time.
Can I buy a house in multiple occupation?
Yes, but these properties carry extra rules. A large house in multiple occupation needs a mandatory licence, and smaller ones may need one under local schemes. Planning permission can also be needed. Many lenders have specific products for these properties. Your conveyancer will raise enquiries about licensing and planning, and it is wise to check with the council too.
Does the conveyancer check whether the property is a good investment?
No. Your conveyancer checks the legal title and raises questions about anything that might affect your ownership or your plans. Whether the rent, price and location make sense as an investment is a commercial decision for you. Many landlords speak to letting agents, mortgage brokers and accountants alongside their conveyancer.
How long does a buy to let purchase take?
There is no fixed timescale. A simple purchase of a vacant freehold house with no chain can move quickly, while a leasehold flat with a tenant in place, a company buyer and a lender with strict conditions will usually take longer. Searches, the speed of the seller's side and the managing agent's response all play a part. Your conveyancer can give a better idea once they have seen the paperwork.
Can I buy through a limited company?
Yes. Many landlords now buy through a company set up to hold property. The company is the legal owner. Your conveyancer will need details of the company, its directors and its owners, and evidence that it can buy and borrow. Whether this route suits you is mainly a tax question for an accountant.
