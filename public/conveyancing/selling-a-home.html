About selling a home

When you sell, your conveyancer prepares the contract pack. That includes the title, the property information form, the fittings and contents form and, for leasehold property, the lease and management information. They answer the buyer’s solicitor’s questions and agree a completion date.

On completion they receive the money from the buyer’s solicitor, pay off your mortgage and the estate agent if you ask them to, and send you the balance. Getting the paperwork ready early is the best way to avoid delays later in the chain.

A good conveyancer keeps you updated without you having to chase, explains anything unusual in the paperwork in plain English and works with your estate agent and mortgage broker to keep the chain moving. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they work and what they need from you to get started.

Solicitor for selling a house in brief

A good solicitor for selling a house gets the contract pack out quickly and answers the buyer’s enquiries without delay. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated conveyancing firm that handles sales like yours, including leasehold flats and properties with a mortgage to pay off.

Conveyancing fees for selling a house are usually lower than for buying, because there are no searches to order. Expect a legal fee, VAT, office copy entries, a fee for redeeming your mortgage and, for a leasehold flat, the cost of the management pack.

Selling a home explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Selling a home involves less legal checking than buying one, but the seller's side sets the pace for everything else. The buyer's conveyancer cannot start their work properly until they receive the contract pack, and every question they raise has to be answered by you through your conveyancer. A well prepared seller can take weeks off a transaction. A seller who starts gathering paperwork only after accepting an offer often becomes the reason the chain stalls.

Your conveyancer acts only for you. Their job is to prove that you own the property and have the right to sell it, to provide accurate information to the buyer, to negotiate the contract terms, and to make sure that on completion the money arrives, your mortgage is paid off and the balance reaches you safely.

Sellers also carry a real legal responsibility. The answers you give on the property information forms are relied on by the buyer. If something you say turns out to be wrong or misleading, the buyer may have a claim against you after the sale. Your conveyancer will help you understand the forms, but only you know the history of your home, so honesty and care matter.

Selling also tends to involve more of your own time than people expect. You may be asked to find documents from many years ago, explain work done by builders, or confirm details about boundaries and neighbours. Doing this early, while you are not under pressure from a buyer or a chain, makes the whole process calmer. Many sellers find it helpful to keep a folder of everything relating to the property as they go.

A sale has its own rhythm. Here is the general order of events for a typical residential sale in England and Wales. Your conveyancer will explain any differences for your property.

1. Instructing a firm

You choose a conveyancer, agree the quote and complete identity checks. Ideally this happens when you put the property on the market, not when an offer arrives.

2. Gathering the information

Your conveyancer obtains up to date copies of your title from HM Land Registry and sends you the property information form and the fittings and contents form to fill in. If your home is leasehold, they also ask your landlord or managing agent for a management pack.

3. Issuing the contract pack

Once an offer is accepted and the memorandum of sale arrives, your conveyancer sends the draft contract, title copies, your completed forms and supporting documents to the buyer's conveyancer.

4. Answering enquiries

The buyer's conveyancer reviews the pack and the results of their searches, then sends written questions. Your conveyancer answers what they can from the file and asks you about the rest.

5. Agreeing the contract and the date

The two conveyancers agree the final form of the contract. Your conveyancer obtains a redemption figure from your lender. You sign the contract and the transfer deed, and a completion date is agreed with the buyer and anyone else in the chain.

6. Exchange

Contracts are exchanged and the buyer's deposit is received. From this moment you are legally bound to sell on the agreed date.

7. Completion

On completion day the buyer's money arrives, your conveyancer pays off your mortgage and, if you have asked them to, the estate agent's fee, and then sends you what remains. You hand over the keys, usually through the agent.

8. Tidying up

Your conveyancer makes sure your lender confirms the mortgage is discharged so the buyer's conveyancer can register the buyer as the new owner.

The contract pack is the heart of the seller's side. The more complete it is at the start, the fewer questions come back later.

The property information form

This is a detailed questionnaire about your home. It asks about boundaries and who maintains them, disputes and complaints with neighbours, notices and proposals affecting the area, alterations, planning and building control, guarantees and warranties, insurance claims, environmental matters such as flooding and invasive plants, rights and informal arrangements, parking, occupiers and services. Some questions ask what you know, so answer from your own knowledge and say if you are not sure rather than guessing.

The fittings and contents form

This lists what is included in the sale, what you are taking with you and what is available to buy separately. Light fittings, curtains, kitchen appliances, garden plants and sheds commonly cause arguments on moving day, so it is worth being precise.

Supporting documents

  • Planning permissions and building regulation completion certificates for extensions, conversions or structural changes
  • Certificates for replacement windows and doors
  • Gas safety and boiler installation records
  • Electrical installation certificates for rewiring or new circuits
  • Guarantees for damp proofing, timber treatment, roofing or wall insulation
  • Any agreements with neighbours, such as party wall awards
  • Warranties for newer homes

Leasehold information

For a leasehold property, the buyer will want details of the lease, the ground rent and service charge, buildings insurance, planned major works, any disputes and the management arrangements. Much of this comes from the landlord or managing agent in a management pack, which usually has a fee and can take time, so ordering it early helps.

Unregistered property

A small number of homes, often owned by the same family for decades, have never been registered at HM Land Registry. Ownership is then proved by a bundle of paper deeds going back many years. Your conveyancer will need the original deeds and will check that they show an unbroken chain of ownership. The buyer's conveyancer then applies for first registration after completion. If deeds have been lost, it is still possible to sell, but it takes extra work and time, so it is worth mentioning straight away.

If a document is missing, tell your conveyancer as soon as possible. There is often a way round it, but it is much easier to deal with early.

Most sellers still have a mortgage when they sell. Paying it off is part of your conveyancer's job, but there are a few things it helps to understand in advance.

The redemption figure

Before exchange, and again close to completion, your conveyancer asks your lender for a redemption statement. This shows exactly how much is needed to pay off the mortgage on the completion date, including interest up to that day and any fees.

Early repayment charges

If you are within a fixed or discounted deal, your lender may charge a fee for paying off early. Some mortgages can be moved to a new property, which is called porting, and this may avoid the charge. Talk to your lender or broker about this before you agree a completion date.

More than one charge

If you have a second loan secured on the property, such as a secured loan or a charge in favour of a relative or a council, that also needs to be paid off or dealt with on completion. Your conveyancer will check the title for all charges and restrictions and ask the relevant lenders for figures.

Negative equity

If the sale price will not cover what you owe, the sale cannot usually complete unless the shortfall is covered or the lender agrees to release its charge. Your conveyancer and lender will need to know about this early.

Equity loans and shared ownership

If you bought with a government backed equity loan, the loan usually has to be repaid when you sell, based on the value of the property at the time rather than the amount you borrowed. That normally needs a fresh valuation and the scheme administrator's involvement, which can take a while. Shared ownership homes have their own rules on selling, and the housing provider may have the right to find a buyer first. Your conveyancer will explain the steps if either applies.

Undertakings

On completion your conveyancer gives the buyer's conveyancer a formal promise, called an undertaking, to pay off your mortgage and send proof that it has been discharged. This is why the buyer can complete before the lender's paperwork has come through.

Selling usually costs less in legal fees than buying, but there are still several items to budget for.

What a sale quote usually includes

  • The firm's legal fee for the sale
  • Extra charges for redeeming a mortgage, a leasehold title or an unregistered property
  • Fees for official copies of your title from HM Land Registry
  • Bank transfer and identity check fees
  • VAT on the fee and on some disbursements
  • For leasehold, the landlord or managing agent's fee for the management pack

Other costs of selling

Outside the legal quote, the main cost is usually the estate agent's fee, which your conveyancer can pay from the sale money if you ask. You may also need to pay early repayment charges to your lender, removal costs, and the cost of any indemnity insurance or regularisation certificates needed to deal with missing paperwork.

Tax on the sale

Selling your main home is often free of Capital Gains Tax, but if you are selling a second home, a rented property or a home you have not always lived in, tax may be due and there can be a short deadline to report it. Rules and rates change, so check GOV.UK or speak to a tax adviser. Your conveyancer will not usually give tax advice unless they offer that service.

Many of the delays in a sale come from issues that could have been spotted months earlier. These are the ones conveyancers see most often.

Missing certificates

Work done years ago, sometimes by a previous owner, may not have the right sign off. Options can include obtaining copies from the council or installer, applying for a retrospective certificate, or offering the buyer indemnity insurance. Contacting the council about the missing paperwork can sometimes affect whether insurance is available, so it is worth talking to your conveyancer before doing so.

Title discrepancies

The name on the register might not match your current name, the title plan might not match the boundaries on the ground, or a right of way you use might not be written down. Your conveyancer can often correct these, but some fixes need HM Land Registry applications that take time.

Other occupiers

Buyers expect vacant possession. Anyone aged 17 or over who lives in the property but is not an owner, such as an adult child or a partner, is usually asked to sign a form agreeing to leave on completion.

Leasehold delays

Management packs can be slow, and buyers often raise questions about service charges, major works and the remaining length of the lease. If your lease is getting short, the buyer or their lender may want it extended before or as part of the sale.

Buyers pulling out or renegotiating

Until exchange, a buyer can withdraw or try to lower the price, sometimes after a survey. Your conveyancer cannot prevent this, but a complete and accurate contract pack reduces the chances of late surprises.

Sellers moving before completion

Some sellers move out early, for example into a new home or care. An empty property can raise insurance issues, and buyers may worry about its condition. If you plan to leave the property empty for a period before completion, tell your insurer and your conveyancer, and make sure someone can give access for meter readings and any agreed visits.

Fees and costs

Most conveyancing firms quote a fixed legal fee for the transaction, plus disbursements. Disbursements are costs the firm pays on your behalf, such as searches, Land Registry fees, identity checks and bank transfer fees. If you are buying, Stamp Duty Land Tax may also be due, and the firm usually pays it for you from the money you send them.

Leasehold work, new builds, shared ownership and buying with a company usually cost more because there is more to check. A good firm puts everything in writing before you instruct them, so you can compare quotes like for like and avoid surprises at completion.

Selling a home questions and answers

When should I instruct a solicitor for selling my house?
When you put the property on the market. Early instruction lets the firm prepare the contract pack and spot title problems before a buyer is found.
What are typical conveyancing fees for selling a house?
Fees depend on the price and whether the property is leasehold. The firm we introduce you to gives you a written quote that lists the legal fee, VAT and disbursements.
Can I instruct a conveyancer before I find a buyer?
Yes, and it often helps. The firm can check your title and prepare the contract pack early, so it is ready to send the day you accept an offer.
What if I can’t find a guarantee or certificate?
Tell your conveyancer. Missing documents can often be dealt with by indemnity insurance or by getting a copy, and the firm will explain the options.
Who pays off my mortgage?
Your conveyancer gets a redemption figure from your lender and pays it from the sale money on completion.
Does Leaflegal do the conveyancing?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service. The firm we introduce you to does the legal work and is responsible for it.
Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
When should I instruct a conveyancer to sell?
Ideally when you put your home on the market. The firm can carry out identity checks, obtain your title, send you the forms and request any leasehold information straight away. That way the contract pack is ready to send on the day you accept an offer, which can make your property more attractive to buyers.
What does my conveyancer need from me at the start?
Your conveyancer will need photo ID, proof of address, your mortgage lender and account details, and the details of your estate agent. They will also ask you to complete the property information form and the fittings and contents form, and to send any certificates, guarantees and planning documents you have.
Do I need my original deeds?
For most registered properties, the official record is held electronically by HM Land Registry, so original paper deeds are not essential. Any old deeds you have can still be useful, especially if they explain rights or boundaries. If your property is unregistered, the deeds are vital and your conveyancer will need to see them.
Do I need an Energy Performance Certificate?
Homes marketed for sale in England and Wales generally need a valid Energy Performance Certificate, and the agent will usually arrange this before marketing begins. If you already have one that is still valid, you can usually use it. Your estate agent can confirm what is needed.
Can I sell if my name has changed since I bought?
Yes. If you have changed your name through marriage, civil partnership or deed poll, your conveyancer will usually ask for evidence, such as a marriage certificate, and may update the register or explain the change to the buyer. It is a common issue and rarely causes a problem if dealt with early.
What if I own the property with someone else?
All registered owners usually need to agree to the sale and sign the contract and transfer. If one owner has died, your conveyancer will need to see the death certificate and may need probate depending on how the property was owned. If owners disagree about selling, it is a different situation and legal advice is needed.
Can I use the same firm as my buyer?
Professional rules usually prevent one firm acting for both buyer and seller in a residential sale, because their interests conflict. There are limited exceptions, but in practice most transactions have a separate firm on each side. Your conveyancer will tell you if a conflict arises.
How detailed should my answers be on the forms?
Answer accurately and from your own knowledge. If you are not sure, say so rather than guessing, and do not leave out something you know about because you think it is minor. The buyer relies on your answers, and inaccurate replies can lead to claims after the sale. Your conveyancer can help with anything you find unclear.
Do I have to tell buyers about disputes with neighbours?
The property information form asks about disputes and complaints, including ones that have been resolved. If you have had a disagreement over boundaries, noise, parking or anything else, it usually needs to be disclosed. Your conveyancer can help you describe it accurately and in proportion.
What if I cannot find a building regulation certificate?
Tell your conveyancer early. They may be able to obtain a copy from the local authority's records, or explain options such as a regularisation application or indemnity insurance for the buyer. The right approach depends on the work, how long ago it was done and what the buyer and their lender will accept.
What is the fittings and contents form for?
It sets out exactly what is staying and what is going. Without it, there can be disagreements about light fittings, curtains, appliances, shelving and garden items. Once it forms part of the contract, you are expected to leave what you said you would leave, in the condition it was in.
Do I need to mention Japanese knotweed?
The property information form asks whether the property is affected by Japanese knotweed. If it is, or has been, it needs to be disclosed, along with any treatment plan in place. Buyers' lenders often want a management plan with an insurance backed guarantee, so having this ready can help.
What is a management pack?
For leasehold property, the management pack is information from the landlord or managing agent about service charges, ground rent, insurance, planned works, the building's management and any disputes. The buyer's conveyancer needs it to advise their client. There is usually a fee, and it can take a few weeks to arrive.
Why is the buyer's conveyancer asking so many questions?
Their job is to protect the buyer and the buyer's lender, so they check every document and ask about anything unclear or missing. Some questions may seem unnecessary, but answering them fully and quickly is usually the fastest way to reach exchange. Your conveyancer will tell you which ones need your input.
Can I accept a higher offer after agreeing a sale?
Until contracts are exchanged, a sale is not legally binding, so a seller can in principle accept a different offer. Your estate agent will have obligations about passing on offers. Changing buyers late can cause delay and cost for others, and your conveyancer can explain the practical position.
What if the buyer tries to reduce the price?
Buyers sometimes ask for a reduction after a survey or valuation. You can accept, negotiate or refuse. If the price changes, your conveyancer updates the contract and the buyer's lender needs to be told. The decision is yours, and your estate agent can help with the negotiation itself.
What if my buyer's mortgage is delayed?
A delayed mortgage offer is one of the most common causes of hold ups. Your conveyancer can ask the buyer's conveyancer for updates, and the estate agent can chase the buyer and their broker. Until the offer is issued, the buyer's conveyancer usually cannot exchange contracts.
Can exchange and completion happen on the same day?
Yes, it is possible, particularly where nobody is in a chain. It leaves no gap between being committed and moving, so removals and money must be ready in advance. Your conveyancer will talk through whether it suits your circumstances and the rest of the chain.
Do I need to be available on completion day?
You do not usually need to be at your conveyancer's office, but you need to have moved out and left the keys with the estate agent or as agreed. Keep your phone to hand in case your conveyancer needs to contact you. You should also take meter readings before you leave.
Who sets the completion date?
The completion date is agreed between you and the buyer before exchange, usually through the conveyancers and the estate agent. In a chain, everyone has to agree the same date, so there is often some give and take. Once contracts have exchanged, the date is fixed in the contract and can only be changed if everyone agrees.
When will I receive the money from my sale?
On completion, your conveyancer receives the buyer's money, pays off your mortgage and any other charges, deducts their fees and any estate agent's fee you have authorised, and sends you the balance. This is usually sent the same day or the next working day, depending on timing and bank processes.
Who pays the estate agent?
You pay the estate agent under the agreement you signed with them. Many sellers ask their conveyancer to pay the agent from the sale money on completion, which is convenient. Your conveyancer will need the agent's invoice before completion to include it in the completion statement.
What happens to my buildings insurance?
Keep your buildings insurance in place until completion. Even though the buyer may arrange their own cover from exchange, the property is still yours until completion, and your lender will expect cover to continue until the mortgage is paid off. Cancel it only once completion has happened.
What happens if the buyer does not complete on time?
If the buyer fails to complete on the agreed date, the contract usually allows you to charge interest and, after serving a notice, to end the contract and keep the deposit. You may also be able to claim further losses. Your conveyancer will explain your position under the contract if it happens.
Will I owe tax when I sell?
Selling your only or main home is often free of Capital Gains Tax, but tax can apply to second homes, rented properties, homes with large grounds or homes used partly for business. There can be a short reporting deadline. Tax rules change, so speak to an accountant or check GOV.UK for your situation.
Why must I sign a transfer deed?
The transfer deed is the document that actually moves legal ownership from you to the buyer. Your conveyancer will send it for you to sign in front of an independent witness, or electronically if the firm uses that method. It is held until completion, then sent to the buyer's conveyancer so they can register the buyer as owner.
Can I sell a property I inherited?
Yes, but the personal representatives usually need a grant of probate or letters of administration before the sale can complete. Marketing and agreeing a sale can often start earlier. The executors sign the documents, and the buyer's conveyancer will check that they have authority to sell.
Can I sell my home during a divorce?
Yes, if both owners agree, or if a court orders the sale. Each person may have their own solicitor for the family side, and the conveyancer needs clear instructions on how the money will be divided on completion. If you are separating, it helps to tell the conveyancer at the start.
Can I sell a property with tenants in it?
Yes. You can sell with the tenants staying, usually to an investor, or with vacant possession once the tenancy has been lawfully ended. The buyer will want to see the tenancy agreement and evidence of deposit protection. Ending a tenancy has its own rules, so get advice before serving any notice.
What is a power of attorney sale?
If an owner cannot manage their own affairs, an attorney appointed under a registered lasting power of attorney can usually sell on their behalf. The conveyancer will need to see the registered power and may need to check that the sale is within its terms. If there is no power in place, a court deputy may be needed.
Can I sell if there is a restriction on my title?
Many titles have restrictions, for example one requiring a lender's consent or one that applies where owners hold as tenants in common. Your conveyancer will check what each restriction requires and deal with it, which may mean obtaining a consent, a certificate or appointing a second trustee. Some take time, so they are best spotted early.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, often sooner. If you have just accepted an offer and the agent needs your conveyancer's details, mention that on the form. The firm can then confirm its quote and start preparing your contract pack without delay.
Should I use the same firm for my sale and purchase?
Many movers do, because one firm can see both sides and coordinate exchange and completion more easily. It also means one completion statement and one point of contact. You can use two different firms, but they will need to communicate closely, which adds a layer of coordination.
Should I sell before I start looking to buy?
There is no single right answer. Having a buyer for your home, or a sale already agreed, usually makes your offer more attractive to sellers. Looking first lets you see what is available. Many people put their home on the market at the same time as starting to view.
When should I instruct my conveyancer?
As soon as you put your home on the market, if not before. The firm can prepare your sale paperwork and carry out identity checks while you look for your next home. When both offers are agreed, your conveyancer is already up and running on the sale, which helps the whole chain.
What if I find a home before I have a buyer?
You can still make an offer, but many sellers prefer buyers who are ready to proceed. If your offer is accepted, your conveyancer can start the purchase, but exchange usually waits until you have a buyer and your sale has caught up. Some sellers may not be willing to wait.
What information will the firm need for both sides?
Expect to provide identity documents, details of your current lender and mortgage, information about your new mortgage or porting, both memoranda of sale, and evidence of any money you are adding. You will also complete the property information forms for your sale.
How long does a linked move usually take?
It often takes a few months from both offers being accepted to completion, but it depends on the length of the chain and how ready everyone is. A chain is only as fast as its slowest link. Your conveyancer can give a better estimate once both transactions are under way.
What if I am buying and selling with different people?
Sometimes the owners of the home being sold are not the same as the buyers of the new one, for example after a separation or when a parent is moving in with family. Your conveyancer needs to know exactly who owns what, who is buying and where the money is going, as this affects the paperwork, identity checks and how the sale money is paid out.
What is a chain?
A chain is a series of transactions linked because each buyer is also selling or each seller is also buying. If you sell to someone who is also selling, and you buy from someone who is also buying, you are in a chain. Everyone needs to exchange and complete together.
