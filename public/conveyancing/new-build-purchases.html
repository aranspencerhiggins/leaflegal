About new build purchases

New build purchases work differently. Developers set tight deadlines to exchange, the property may not be finished when you sign, and completion happens on notice once it is ready.

Your conveyancer checks the developer’s contract, the planning and building warranty, any estate management charges and the specification, so you know exactly what you are buying.

A good conveyancer keeps you updated without you having to chase, explains anything unusual in the paperwork in plain English and works with your estate agent and mortgage broker to keep the chain moving. New build conveyancing in brief

New build conveyancing has its own pressures, including tight developer deadlines, plans and estate charges to check and a build that may not be finished at exchange. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated new build solicitor who handles purchases from housebuilders regularly.

Before buying a new build, ask your conveyancer to check the warranty, the estate management arrangements and any leasehold terms, and plan your snagging. Make sure the firm can meet the developer’s exchange deadline.

New build purchases explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Buying a brand new home from a developer is not the same as buying from another homeowner. There is usually no chain, the seller is a business with its own standard paperwork, and the home may still be a plot of mud when you sign. The legal work follows a different rhythm, with a quick push to exchange and then a wait until the property is built and ready.

Developers want sales secured fast. Most ask for a reservation fee and then set a deadline to exchange contracts, often a matter of weeks. Meeting that deadline means your conveyancer, your mortgage lender and you all need to move quickly. Once you have exchanged, completion happens when the developer serves notice that the home is finished, which may be weeks or months later.

Because the home is new, there is no history to check. There is no seller's property information form about past disputes or works, no guarantee file built up over years and no previous owner to ask. Instead your conveyancer looks at planning permission, the developer's title, the estate layout, the roads and sewers, the building warranty and the developer's contract terms.

Lenders also treat new builds a little differently. Some limit how much they will lend on a new flat, ask for a larger deposit or want extra paperwork about incentives and the warranty. Your mortgage broker can explain lender appetite, while your conveyancer makes sure the legal documents meet whatever the lender has asked for.

Types of new build purchase

  • Houses on a new estate, often freehold with an estate management charge
  • Flats in a new block, which are leasehold
  • Homes bought off plan before building has started
  • Shared ownership homes sold through a housing provider on a new development
  • Conversions of offices or older buildings into new flats
  • Self build or custom build plots where you buy land and arrange your own build

Every developer runs its sales slightly differently, but the general pattern is similar across most sites in England and Wales. Your conveyancer will explain the timetable for your purchase.

Reservation

You choose a plot and pay a reservation fee to take it off the market. You sign a reservation agreement, which usually sets out the price, any incentives and the time allowed to exchange. Read what it says about refunds if you pull out. Developers who follow the New Homes Quality Code have to meet certain standards when taking reservations.

Sales pack and instruction

The developer's solicitor sends your conveyancer a pack, often containing the draft contract, a transfer or lease, plans, the planning permission, warranty details and information about the estate. Your conveyancer reviews the pack, orders searches and raises enquiries. Instructing a firm the same day you reserve gives the best chance of meeting the deadline.

Mortgage and incentives

Your lender needs to know about any incentive the developer is offering, such as paid fees, upgrades or cash contributions. Developers usually complete a standard form for lenders setting these out. Mortgage offers on new builds can be issued for a longer period, but they still expire, so timing matters if the build is slow.

Exchange

Once your conveyancer has reported and you have signed, contracts are exchanged and you pay your deposit. You are now committed to buying when the home is finished. There is no fixed completion date at this point.

Building and notice

The developer builds or finishes the home and arranges for it to be inspected by the building control body and the warranty provider. When the home is ready, the developer serves notice to complete. The contract sets the notice period, commonly around ten working days, so your money needs to be ready quickly.

Completion and after

Your conveyancer draws down the mortgage, pays the developer and confirms completion. You collect the keys, usually from the site sales office. The firm then pays any Stamp Duty Land Tax and registers you at HM Land Registry, which can take longer for new plots because the estate needs to be mapped.

Developers use standard contracts drafted in their favour, and they rarely agree large changes for individual buyers. Your conveyancer reads the contract to explain what you are signing up to, so that you know where the risks sit before you exchange.

Completion date and long stop date

Because the home may not be built, the contract usually gives an estimated finish date and a later long stop date. If the home is not finished by the long stop date, you may be able to withdraw and get your deposit back. Check how far away the long stop date is and how it fits with your mortgage offer.

Changes to the home

Most contracts let the developer make some changes to the specification, layout or materials, as long as the home is broadly similar. Your conveyancer will explain how much freedom the contract gives the developer and what happens if a change is significant.

Plans and boundaries

The plot shown on the plans should match what you think you are buying, including parking spaces, gardens and any shared areas. Plot numbers and postal addresses can differ, so make sure you are checking the right one.

Roads and sewers

On many estates, the roads and sewers are built by the developer and later handed over to the council and the water company under adoption agreements. Until that happens, they may be private. Your conveyancer checks whether agreements are in place and whether there is a bond to cover the cost if the developer does not finish them.

Restrictions

New estates often have rules in the transfer, such as limits on extensions, parking commercial vehicles, putting up fences or running a business from home. Some last only while the developer is selling the estate and some last indefinitely. Your conveyancer will list the main ones in their report.

Your deposit if things go wrong

Some contracts let the developer use your exchange deposit towards the build, while others require it to be held by the developer's solicitor until completion. Building warranties often include some protection for deposits if the developer becomes insolvent before the home is finished, but the cover and its limits vary. Your conveyancer will explain how your deposit is held and what protection applies.

New homes in England and Wales usually come with a building warranty from a provider such as NHBC, Premier Guarantee or LABC Warranty. Most lenders will not lend on a new home without one. Your conveyancer checks that a recognised warranty is in place and that the right documents are issued at completion.

What a warranty usually covers

A typical warranty lasts ten years. During an early period, often the first two years, the developer is responsible for putting right many defects that breach the warranty standards. In the later years, cover is usually limited to certain structural problems. Each provider's policy is different, so read the terms you are given.

Snagging

Snags are small defects or unfinished items, such as poorly fitted doors, paint marks or missing sealant. Many buyers inspect the home before completion or soon after, and some pay a professional snagging inspector. The developer is usually expected to fix snags reported within its customer care period.

The New Homes Ombudsman

Developers registered under the New Homes Quality Code must follow rules on how they sell homes and handle complaints. If a complaint about a registered developer cannot be resolved, it may be possible to take it to the New Homes Ombudsman. Your conveyancer can tell you whether your developer is registered.

Building regulations

New homes must be signed off by a building control body. Your conveyancer will check that the completion certificate and the warranty cover note or certificate will be provided, as your lender will want them.

The costs of a new build purchase look much like any other purchase, but some extras are common. Knowing them in advance helps you compare the true cost of different plots and developers.

Legal fee

Most firms quote a fixed fee, often with a supplement for new build work because of the extra checks and the deadline pressure. Leasehold flats, shared ownership and incentive schemes can add further charges.

Disbursements

These usually include searches, the HM Land Registry fee, identity check fees and bank transfer fees. For a leasehold flat there may also be fees for registering your ownership with the management company.

Stamp Duty Land Tax

Tax is worked out on the price you pay. Some incentives can affect the figure, so your conveyancer will check how they are treated. Rates and reliefs change, so check GOV.UK or ask your firm. In Wales, Land Transaction Tax applies instead.

Ongoing estate costs

Many new estates charge an annual fee for looking after shared areas such as green spaces, play areas, private roads and drainage ponds. Flats have a service charge. Ask your conveyancer to explain how these charges are worked out and whether they can rise.

Paid fees and incentives

Some developers offer to pay legal fees if you use a firm they recommend. You are always free to use your own firm, and you can weigh up the saving against having a firm that acts only for you.

New build purchases often go smoothly, but there are some well known pressure points. Knowing them helps you ask the right questions before you exchange.

Tight exchange deadlines

Developers may threaten to cancel the reservation if you do not exchange in time. A good conveyancer will tell you if the deadline is realistic and what information is still missing. Some developers will extend a deadline if there is a good reason.

Build delays

Homes can take longer to finish than planned. A delay can mean your mortgage offer expires, your rental tenancy ends before you can move or your circumstances change. Your conveyancer will explain the long stop date and what the contract says about delays.

Valuation shortfalls

If the lender's valuer thinks the home is worth less than the price, the lender may lend less than you expected. This can happen where incentives have pushed up the headline price. You would then need to find the difference or renegotiate.

Unadopted roads and estate charges

If roads and sewers are never adopted, or estate charges rise sharply, it can affect running costs and future sales. Your conveyancer will report on what the paperwork shows.

Defects after moving in

Developers are expected to deal with defects reported within the warranty period, but getting them fixed can take persistence. Keep a written record of every issue and report it in writing.

Paid extras not recorded

Buyers often pay for upgrades such as better kitchens, flooring or extra sockets. If these are agreed only in conversation with the sales team, there may be no record if they are not delivered. Ask for every extra to be confirmed in writing and tell your conveyancer, so the paperwork and the lender's information match what you are actually buying.

Flats and building safety

New blocks of flats are subject to building safety rules, and taller buildings have extra requirements under the Building Safety Act 2022. Your conveyancer will ask about the building's sign off and management arrangements, and lenders may have their own requirements for higher buildings.

New build purchases questions and answers

Why do I need a new build solicitor?
Developer contracts, deadlines and estate arrangements differ from a normal purchase. A new build solicitor is used to these and can move quickly.
Why do developers set short deadlines?
They want to secure the sale quickly. Your conveyancer will tell you if the deadline is realistic and what happens if it is missed.
Should I use the developer’s recommended solicitor?
You are free to choose your own. An independent firm acts only for you.
What if the home isn’t finished on time?
Your contract will say what happens. The conveyancer will explain your position before you exchange.
Does Leaflegal do the conveyancing?
No. Leaflegal is a free introduction service. The firm we introduce you to does the legal work and is responsible for it.
Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
What is a reservation fee?
It is a payment to the developer that takes a plot off the market for a set time while you arrange your mortgage and legal work. It is usually deducted from the price on completion. Whether you get it back if you pull out depends on the reservation agreement, so read it carefully. Developers registered under the New Homes Quality Code must follow rules about how reservations work.
When should I instruct a conveyancer?
Ideally on the day you reserve, or before. Developers often give a short time to exchange, and every day counts. If you have a firm ready, the developer's solicitor can send the sales pack straight away and searches can be ordered immediately. Waiting even a week can make a tight deadline much harder to meet.
Can I use the developer's recommended solicitor?
You can, but you do not have to. Some developers offer to pay legal fees or other incentives if you use a firm on their list. A firm you choose yourself acts only for you and has no connection to the developer. Whoever you choose, check that the firm is regulated, experienced with new builds and on your lender's panel.
Can I negotiate the price on a new build?
Developers often prefer to offer incentives rather than reduce the headline price, though both are possible. Any incentive must be disclosed to your lender, and some lenders treat incentives as reducing the price for lending purposes. Your conveyancer will check the incentives are recorded properly in the paperwork.
What is the New Homes Quality Code?
It is a code of practice for developers who have registered with the New Homes Quality Board. It sets standards for how homes are marketed and sold, what information buyers are given, how reservations work and how complaints are handled. Registered developers can be referred to the New Homes Ombudsman. Your conveyancer can check whether your developer is registered.
Can I buy a new build home off plan?
Yes. Buying off plan means exchanging before the home is built, often based on plans, a show home and a specification. It can mean a long wait before completion. Your conveyancer will look closely at the long stop date, the developer's right to make changes and what happens to your deposit if the developer runs into problems.
What is part exchange?
Some developers offer to buy your current home as part of the deal, so you do not need to find a buyer yourself. The developer usually values your home and makes an offer, which may be lower than you would get on the open market in exchange for speed and certainty. Your conveyancer would handle the sale to the developer and the new purchase together, and will explain how the two are linked in the contract.
Why are new build deadlines so short?
Developers want sales secured so they can plan their finances and build programme. A deadline to exchange within a few weeks is common. It is not a legal requirement, it is a commercial term. Your conveyancer will tell you what is needed to meet it and let you know early if it looks unrealistic.
What happens if I miss the exchange deadline?
The developer may cancel the reservation and keep some or all of the reservation fee, depending on the agreement. In practice, many developers extend deadlines if the buyer and their conveyancer are clearly working towards exchange and the delay is outside their control. Keep in touch with the sales team and let your conveyancer know if anything is holding you up.
Can the developer change the home after I exchange?
Most contracts allow some changes to materials, specification or layout, as long as the home stays broadly similar. Larger changes may give you rights. Your conveyancer will explain what your contract allows. If a feature really matters to you, such as a particular garden size, it is worth raising before exchange.
What is a long stop date?
It is the last date by which the developer must finish the home. If the home is not ready by then, the contract usually lets you withdraw and get your deposit back. Long stop dates can be many months after the estimated finish date. Your conveyancer will point it out and explain how it fits with your mortgage offer and any rental notice.
What is completion on notice?
On a new build, there is often no fixed completion date when you exchange. Instead, the developer sends notice when the home is finished, and you must complete within the period set by the contract, commonly around ten working days. Your mortgage and deposit need to be ready to go at short notice.
What deposit do I pay on exchange?
Developers often ask for a deposit of a set percentage of the price, though the figure varies and can sometimes be reduced if you have a smaller mortgage deposit. The reservation fee may count towards it. Your conveyancer will confirm what the contract requires and when the money must reach them.
What happens to my deposit if the developer goes out of business?
It depends on how the deposit is held and what protection the warranty provides. Some deposits stay with the developer's solicitor until completion, while others are released to the developer. Warranties often include some insolvency cover for deposits paid before completion, but there are limits and conditions. Your conveyancer will explain the position on your purchase before you exchange.
What if my mortgage offer expires before the home is finished?
Mortgage offers on new builds are often valid for longer than usual, but they still have an end date. If the home is delayed, you may need to ask for an extension or apply again, which could mean different terms. Speak to your broker or lender as soon as delays appear. Your conveyancer can tell you what the contract says about timing.
Can I visit the home before completion?
Many developers offer a pre completion visit or home demonstration so you can see the finished property and point out obvious snags. Access to a building site is usually restricted for safety reasons, so you will need to arrange it with the sales team. Some buyers take a professional snagging inspector with them.
What documents should I get at completion?
Your conveyancer should receive evidence of building regulations sign off and the building warranty documents, which your lender usually requires. You may also receive manuals and guarantees for appliances and systems in the home. Keep everything together, as you will need it when you sell.
Why does Land Registry registration take longer on a new estate?
Each new plot needs to be created as a separate title from the developer's larger title, and HM Land Registry has to approve the estate plan. This can take longer than registering an ordinary purchase. It does not usually affect your ability to live in the home, and your conveyancer will keep you updated.
What should I do about snags after I move in?
Report them to the developer in writing as soon as you find them and keep a copy. Most developers have a customer care team and a period during which they put defects right. If problems are not dealt with, the warranty provider's dispute service or, for registered developers, the New Homes Ombudsman may be able to help.
Can I order upgrades or extras?
Many developers offer upgrades such as different flooring, kitchen finishes or appliances, sometimes as an incentive and sometimes at extra cost. Make sure every extra is confirmed in writing with the price and what is included. Tell your conveyancer, because extras may need to be disclosed to your lender and recorded in the contract. Paid extras can also be relevant to the tax calculation.
What is an estate management charge?
Many new estates have shared areas such as open spaces, private roads, play areas or drainage features that are not adopted by the council. A management company looks after them, and homeowners pay an annual charge. Your conveyancer will explain who runs the company, how the charge is worked out and what happens if it is not paid.
What does road adoption mean?
It means the council takes over responsibility for maintaining the estate roads. Until then, the roads are private and the developer or a management company maintains them. Developers usually sign an agreement with the council, often backed by a bond, to finish the roads to an adoptable standard. Your conveyancer will check what is in place.
Are new build houses ever leasehold?
Some new houses have been sold as leasehold in the past, which drew a lot of criticism. The law on new leasehold houses and on ground rent has been changing. Most new long residential leases granted since mid 2022 can only have a nominal ground rent. Your conveyancer will explain the tenure of the home you are buying and how the current rules apply.
How long does a building warranty last?
A typical warranty lasts ten years from completion. During the early years, often the first two, the developer is usually responsible for fixing defects. Later on, cover is usually limited to certain structural defects. Each warranty provider has its own terms, so ask your conveyancer for the policy details and read them.
Does the warranty cover everything?
No. Warranties usually exclude wear and tear, normal shrinkage cracks, damage caused by the owner and some fittings that have their own guarantees. Claims often have to be made within set time limits. The policy document explains what is covered. Your conveyancer checks the warranty is in place, but questions about a claim go to the provider.
Is buying a new build flat any different?
Yes. A new flat is leasehold, so your conveyancer will review the new lease as well as the developer's contract. They will look at the length of the lease, the service charge estimate, who manages the building, what the reserve fund will be and the rules for residents. Most new long leases now carry only a nominal ground rent. First year service charges are often estimates and can rise once the building is fully occupied.
Are legal fees higher for a new build?
Often slightly, because the work is done to a tight deadline and involves checks that do not arise on a resale, such as the estate plan, adoption agreements and warranty. Ask for a written quote that shows any new build supplement and lists disbursements separately.
Is Help to Buy still available?
The Help to Buy equity loan scheme in England has closed to new buyers. Some developers offer their own incentives, shared ownership homes or deposit schemes instead. If you are looking at any scheme, ask your conveyancer what extra paperwork it involves, and check the current position on GOV.UK.
Will I pay Stamp Duty on a new build?
New builds are taxed in the same way as other homes, based on the price you pay. First time buyer relief and higher rates for additional properties can apply in the same way. Some incentives can affect the figure. Rates change, so check GOV.UK or ask your conveyancer. In Wales, Land Transaction Tax applies instead.

The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, often sooner. Because new build deadlines are tight, it helps to have the reservation agreement, the developer's details and your mortgage information to hand when they call.
What does leasehold actually mean?
It means you own the flat for a fixed period, set out in the lease, rather than owning it forever. The freeholder owns the building and the land. The lease sets out your rights and duties, including what you pay, what you repair and what you can do in the flat. When the lease runs out, the flat returns to the freeholder, which is why lease length matters so much.
What will my conveyancer look for in the lease?
They check the length, ground rent, service charge rules, repair and insurance duties and restrictions on use. They look for anything unusual, such as fast rising ground rent, bans on letting or pets, or gaps in who repairs what. They also check the lease meets your lender's requirements. You should receive a report explaining the main points in plain language.
Can the lease stop me keeping a pet or letting the flat?
Yes. Many leases restrict pets, letting, short lets, running a business from the flat or making alterations. Some ban these outright and some require the freeholder's consent. If any of these matter to you, tell your conveyancer at the start so they can check the lease and raise questions before you exchange.
What is a deed of covenant?
It is a document some leases require a buyer to sign, promising the freeholder or management company that they will keep to the terms of the lease. Your conveyancer will prepare or check it and deal with the fee for registering it. It is a routine part of many leasehold purchases.
What is a share of freehold?
It means the flat owners together own the freehold of the building, usually through a company or as joint owners. You still have a lease, but you also have a say in how the building is run. Your conveyancer checks both the lease and the freehold arrangements, and will explain your role in the company if there is one.
Can I make changes to the flat?
It depends on the lease. Many leases allow internal decoration but need consent for structural changes, moving kitchens or bathrooms, or replacing carpets with hard flooring. If you plan any works, ask your conveyancer to check the lease. Works done without consent can cause problems with the freeholder and when you sell.
What if the lease has a mistake in it?
Some older leases have errors or gaps, such as unclear repair duties or service charge shares that do not add up to the whole. Lenders may insist on a fix, which usually means a deed of variation agreed with the freeholder, or in some cases insurance. Your conveyancer will explain what the lender requires and how long it might take.
What is forfeiture?
Forfeiture is a right some leases give the freeholder to end the lease if the leaseholder seriously breaks its terms, for example by not paying charges. There are legal protections for leaseholders, and freeholders usually have to follow set steps and often go to court or a tribunal first. It is rare in practice, but it shows why keeping to the lease and paying charges on time matters. Your conveyancer will point out the forfeiture clause in their report.
