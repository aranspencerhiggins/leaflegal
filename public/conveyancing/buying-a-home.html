About buying a home

Once your offer is accepted, a conveyancer does the legal work to make sure you get good title to the property. They check the title deeds, order searches, review the contract and the information the seller provides, and raise questions with the seller’s solicitor about anything that looks wrong.

If you are buying with a mortgage, your conveyancer also acts for your lender, checks the mortgage offer conditions and makes sure the money is in place for completion. After completion, they pay any Stamp Duty Land Tax due and register you as the new owner at HM Land Registry.

A good conveyancer keeps you updated without you having to chase, explains anything unusual in the paperwork in plain English and works with your estate agent and mortgage broker to keep the chain moving. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they work and what they need from you to get started.

Buying a home explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Having an offer accepted on a home is exciting, but in England and Wales it is only the start. Until contracts are exchanged, the seller is free to accept a higher offer and you are free to walk away. The weeks in between are when your conveyancer finds out everything the law can tell you about the property, so that you can decide whether to commit with your eyes open.

A buyer's conveyancer has two jobs. The first is to protect you, by checking that the seller owns the property, that there is nothing hidden in the title or the local records that would affect your use or enjoyment of it, and that the contract reflects what you agreed. The second, if you have a mortgage, is to protect your lender by making sure the property is good security for the loan and the mortgage is properly registered.

Buying is the side of the transaction with the most checks. The seller's conveyancer mainly provides information. Your conveyancer reads it, compares it with the searches and the title, and asks questions about anything that does not add up. That is why the buyer's side often feels slower, and why so much of the timetable depends on how quickly answers come back.

It also helps to understand what a conveyancer does not do. They do not value the property, inspect its condition, negotiate the price or arrange your mortgage. Those jobs belong to the surveyor, the estate agent and your broker or lender. The conveyancer brings all of that together on the legal side, checks that what you are buying matches what you have been told, and makes sure the transfer of ownership and money happens safely.

Every purchase is a little different, but most follow a similar order. Knowing the shape of the process makes it easier to see where you are and what is coming next.

1. Instructing the firm

You accept the firm's quote and terms, and the firm checks your identity and where your money is coming from. The estate agent sends a memorandum of sale to both conveyancers, setting out the price, the parties and the property.

2. The contract pack arrives

The seller's conveyancer sends a draft contract, a copy of the title from HM Land Registry, the seller's answers on the property information form, the fittings and contents form and, for leasehold property, the lease. Your conveyancer reviews all of this.

3. Searches are ordered

Your conveyancer orders searches from the local authority, the water company and other providers. Some come back within days and some take weeks, depending on the area.

4. Enquiries

Your conveyancer raises written questions with the seller's conveyancer about anything unclear, missing or worrying. This back and forth can take several rounds.

5. Mortgage offer and survey

While the legal work goes on, your lender values the property and issues a mortgage offer, and you may commission your own survey. Your conveyancer checks the offer conditions and may raise further enquiries if the survey finds something.

6. Report and signing

Your conveyancer sends you a report on the property, explaining what they have found and anything you need to know. You sign the contract and the mortgage deed, and transfer your deposit.

7. Exchange

Once everyone in the chain is ready and a completion date is agreed, the conveyancers exchange contracts by phone. From this point you are legally committed to buy.

8. Completion and registration

On completion day your conveyancer sends the purchase money, the seller's conveyancer confirms receipt and the keys are released. Your conveyancer then deals with the property tax return and registers you as owner.

Most of the risks in buying a home are not visible on a viewing. A conveyancer's checks are designed to bring them to light before you are committed.

The title

The register shows who owns the property, the boundaries shown on the title plan, any rights the property benefits from, such as a right of way over a shared drive, and any burdens on it, such as restrictive covenants or rights for neighbours. Your conveyancer checks that the seller is the registered owner and that nothing on the title would stop you using the property as you intend.

Local authority search

This covers matters the council holds records about, including planning decisions, building control records, whether the road outside is maintained by the council, tree preservation orders, conservation areas and some enforcement notices. It does not usually cover neighbouring properties, so it will not tell you about planning applications next door unless you ask separately.

Water and drainage

This confirms whether the property is connected to the public sewer and mains water, and whether there are public sewers within the boundaries. A sewer running under where you plan to extend can affect building work later.

Environmental and other searches

An environmental search looks at things like past industrial use, flood risk and ground stability. Depending on the location, your conveyancer may also suggest searches for coal or other mining, chancel repair liability, HS2 or other infrastructure, or common land.

The seller's information

The seller answers a standard set of questions about boundaries, disputes, alterations, guarantees, services, flooding and more. Your conveyancer compares these answers with the title and searches. Where something does not match, such as an extension with no building regulation sign off, they will ask about it.

Material information

Estate agents are expected to give buyers key information about a property in the listing, such as the tenure, council tax band, any service charges and certain physical features. This is helpful, but it does not replace the legal checks. Your conveyancer will still verify the position from the title, the searches and the seller's formal answers, and will tell you if anything differs from what you saw when you made your offer.

A conveyancer does not inspect the physical building. That is the job of a surveyor. The legal checks and a survey work together, so many buyers arrange both.

The contract sets out exactly what you are buying, for how much and on what terms. In residential sales, most contracts are based on standard conditions that both conveyancers know well, with extra clauses added to suit the particular sale.

What the contract covers

  • The property being sold and the title it is sold with
  • The price and the deposit
  • What happens if either side fails to complete on time
  • Any items included or excluded, as listed on the fittings and contents form
  • Any special conditions, such as works the seller has agreed to carry out
  • Whether the property is sold with vacant possession or subject to a tenancy

The deposit

On exchange the buyer pays a deposit, which is held by the seller's conveyancer. The amount is agreed between the parties. In a chain, your deposit may be passed up to the seller's own purchase. If the buyer fails to complete, the seller can usually keep the deposit and may claim further losses.

Buildings insurance

The contract and your lender's rules decide when you need to insure the building. Many lenders require buildings insurance to be in place from exchange, so your conveyancer will tell you when cover needs to start.

How you will own it

If you are buying with someone else, you will need to decide whether to own the property as joint tenants or tenants in common. Joint tenants own the whole property together, and if one dies the other automatically takes it. Tenants in common each own a share, which passes under their will. Your conveyancer will explain the difference, and some buyers also record unequal contributions in a declaration of trust.

Your conveyancer will not ask you to sign the contract until they have reported to you and you are ready to go ahead.

Buying usually costs more in legal fees than selling, because there are more checks and more steps after completion.

What a purchase quote usually includes

  • The firm's legal fee for the purchase
  • Extra charges for things like a mortgage, a leasehold title, a gifted deposit or a Help to Buy or Lifetime ISA
  • Search fees
  • HM Land Registry registration fee
  • Identity and bank transfer fees
  • VAT on the fee and on some disbursements
  • For leasehold, notice fees and other charges set by the landlord or managing agent

Property tax

Buyers in England may need to pay Stamp Duty Land Tax, and buyers in Wales Land Transaction Tax. The amount depends on the price and on your circumstances, such as whether you are a first time buyer or already own another home. The rates and reliefs change, so check GOV.UK or the Welsh Revenue Authority for the current position, and ask your conveyancer to confirm your figure. They usually submit the return and pay the tax for you after completion.

Other costs to budget for

Outside the legal quote there are other costs, including a survey, mortgage arrangement fees, broker fees, removals and buildings insurance. It is worth listing these early so that there are no surprises on the completion statement.

Most purchases go through without serious problems, but some issues come up again and again. Knowing about them helps you understand why your conveyancer may ask for more time or more information.

Alterations without paperwork

Extensions, loft conversions, removed walls and new windows usually need planning permission, building regulation approval or both. If the seller cannot produce the paperwork, your conveyancer will explain the options, which may include asking the seller to get a regularisation certificate or provide indemnity insurance.

Boundary and access issues

The title plan shows general boundaries rather than exact lines, so disagreements with neighbours are not unusual. Shared drives and paths with no clear right of way can also cause problems. Your conveyancer will check what the title says and ask the seller about any disputes.

Gifted deposits

Family help with a deposit is common, but lenders and conveyancers need to know about it. The person giving the money usually signs a letter confirming it is a gift and that they will have no share in the property, and they may need to provide ID and bank statements.

Mortgage delays

A mortgage offer can be delayed by a down valuation, missing documents or questions from the underwriter. If the valuation is lower than the price, you may need to renegotiate or find extra money. Your broker and conveyancer can work together on this.

Gazumping

Because nothing is binding before exchange, a seller can accept a higher offer from someone else late in the process. This is frustrating and costly, but it is legal. Being ready to move quickly is the best protection, and some buyers and sellers use reservation agreements or lock out agreements to give extra certainty.

Chain problems

Even when your own purchase is straightforward, a problem further along the chain can hold everyone up. A buyer below you may be waiting for a mortgage offer, or a seller above you may not have found somewhere to move to. Your conveyancer can usually find out where the delay is, but cannot force other people to move faster. If the chain breaks, they will explain where that leaves you.

Most conveyancing firms quote a fixed legal fee for the transaction, plus disbursements. Disbursements are costs the firm pays on your behalf, such as searches, Land Registry fees, identity checks and bank transfer fees. Buying a home questions and answers

When should I instruct a conveyancer?
As soon as your offer is accepted, or even before. Having a firm lined up means the estate agent can send out the memorandum of sale straight away and the work can start.
Do I need a solicitor or a licensed conveyancer?
Either can do the work. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and licensed conveyancers by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. What matters most is experience with your type of property and being on your lender’s panel.
What are disbursements?
Costs the firm pays to third parties on your behalf, such as search fees, Land Registry fees and bank transfer fees. A good quote lists them separately from the legal fee.
Does Leaflegal do the conveyancing?
No. Leaflegal is a free introduction service. The firm we introduce you to does the legal work and is responsible for it.
Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
Can I instruct a conveyancer before I find a property?
Yes. Many buyers choose a firm while they are still looking, so that the firm can carry out identity and money checks early. When an offer is accepted, the estate agent can send the memorandum of sale straight away and the work can begin without delay. This can make your offer more attractive to a seller.
What will my conveyancer need from me at the start?
Expect to provide photo ID, proof of address, details of your mortgage broker or lender, and evidence of where your deposit and any other funds are coming from. If family are giving you money, the firm will need their details too. You will also sign the firm's terms of business and pay money on account for searches.
What is a memorandum of sale?
It is a document the estate agent sends to both conveyancers once an offer is accepted. It sets out the agreed price, the names of the buyer and seller, the property address and the details of each side's conveyancer. It is not a contract and does not commit anyone, but it starts the legal process.
Does the seller's conveyancer act for me too?
No. The seller's conveyancer acts only for the seller. Your own conveyancer acts for you and, if you have a mortgage, usually for your lender as well. Each side's conveyancer looks after their own client's interests, which is why questions pass between them in writing.
Can I buy a property with someone who is not my partner?
Yes. Friends, siblings and parents and children buy together quite often. It is common in these situations to own as tenants in common and to record who has paid what in a declaration of trust, along with what happens if one person wants to sell. Your conveyancer can explain how this works.
Can I buy without a mortgage?
Yes. Cash buyers still need the full legal checks, including the title and searches, because the risks to the buyer are the same. Searches are optional without a lender, but skipping them means you may not learn about issues until later. Cash purchases can be quicker because there is no mortgage offer to wait for.
What if the property is being sold by someone who has died?
When a property is sold by the executors of someone who has died, the executors usually need a grant of probate, or letters of administration, before the sale can complete. Your conveyancer will check who has authority to sell and whether the grant has been issued. Probate sales can take longer, and the sellers may know less about the property's history.
Which searches will my conveyancer order?
Most buyers with a mortgage will have a local authority search, a water and drainage search and an environmental search. Depending on the area, your conveyancer may add others, such as a mining search, a chancel repair check or a flood report. They will explain which searches they recommend and why.
How long do searches take?
It depends largely on the local authority. Some councils return searches within days, while others can take several weeks. Water, drainage and environmental searches are usually quicker. Your conveyancer can tell you the typical turnaround for the area you are buying in.
What if a search shows a problem?
Your conveyancer will explain what the result means and whether it affects the property, your mortgage or your plans. Some results are routine and need no action. Others may need further enquiries, specialist reports or insurance. The decision about whether to go ahead remains yours.
Do I need a survey as well as searches?
Searches tell you about legal and local authority matters. A survey looks at the physical condition of the building. A lender's valuation is not a survey and is mainly for the lender's benefit. Many buyers commission their own survey, especially for older properties or homes that have been altered.
What are restrictive covenants?
They are promises in old deeds that limit what can be done with a property, such as not running a business from it or not building without someone's consent. Some are out of date or unlikely to be enforced, while others still matter. Your conveyancer will point out any that affect you.
What is indemnity insurance?
It is a one off insurance policy that covers the financial risk of a specific legal problem, such as a missing building regulation certificate or a lack of a formal right of way. It does not fix the problem itself. Your conveyancer will explain what a policy covers and its limits.
What is the report on title?
Before you sign the contract, your conveyancer will usually send you a written report. It summarises the property's title, the results of the searches, the seller's answers, the key terms of the contract and anything unusual. Read it carefully and ask about anything you do not understand, as it is your chance to raise concerns before you are committed.
When do I pay my deposit?
Your deposit is usually transferred to your conveyancer shortly before exchange, so that it is ready to be sent to the seller's conveyancer when contracts are exchanged. Your firm will tell you how much is needed and by when. Allow time for the bank transfer to clear and confirm the account details by phone.
What happens if my mortgage valuation is low?
If the lender values the property at less than the agreed price, it may lend less than you hoped. You might renegotiate the price with the seller, put in more of your own money or look at other lenders. Your broker can help with the lending side, and your conveyancer can explain how any price change is handled.
Can family members help with the deposit?
Yes, this is common. Lenders and conveyancers need to know about any gift, and the person giving it will usually sign a gift letter and provide identity and bank statements. If the money is a loan or comes with conditions, it must be disclosed, as it can affect your mortgage.
What if my mortgage offer expires?
Mortgage offers are valid for a limited time. If your purchase is delayed, you may need to ask your lender for an extension or a new offer. Keep your broker informed about the expected timetable, and tell your conveyancer if the offer is close to expiring.
What is a completion statement?
It is a document from your conveyancer showing the money coming in, such as your mortgage and your own funds, and the money going out, such as the purchase price, fees, disbursements and tax. It tells you exactly how much you need to send before completion.
When is Stamp Duty or Land Transaction Tax paid?
Your conveyancer usually collects the tax from you before completion and submits the return and payment after completion, within a strict deadline. If the return is late, penalties and interest can apply. The amount depends on the price and your circumstances, and the rules change, so your firm will confirm the figure.
Why does my conveyancer need so much detail about my savings?
Regulated firms must check the source of funds for every purchase under money laundering rules. That usually means bank statements covering several months and an explanation of where larger sums came from, such as savings, a bonus, an inheritance or the sale of shares. It is a legal requirement and applies to every client, not just you.
What does exchange of contracts mean?
Exchange is the moment the contract becomes binding. Your conveyancer and the seller's conveyancer agree by phone that the signed contracts are exchanged, and your deposit is sent. From then on, if you do not complete, you could lose your deposit and be liable for the seller's losses.
Can I choose my completion date?
The completion date is agreed between buyer and seller before exchange, and in a chain everyone must agree the same date. You can suggest a date, but it needs to work for others too. Avoid booking removals until contracts have exchanged and the date is fixed.
What happens if completion is delayed?
If one side does not complete on the agreed date, the contract usually allows the other side to charge interest and, after a notice period, to cancel the contract and keep the deposit or claim losses. Delays are often short, but your conveyancer will explain what your contract says if it happens.
When do I get the keys?
You can usually collect the keys once the seller's conveyancer confirms they have received the money. The estate agent then releases the keys. In a chain, money often has to pass along several transactions, so the time you get the keys can vary on the day.
Can I visit the property between exchange and completion?
You have no automatic right to enter before completion, but sellers often agree to a visit to measure up or check something. Arrange it through the estate agent. Some buyers like to visit before completion to make sure fixtures and fittings agreed in the contract are still there.
Can I pull out after exchange?
Once contracts are exchanged, you are legally bound to complete. Withdrawing at that stage usually means losing your deposit and possibly paying the seller's extra losses, such as the cost of a delayed sale. That is why your conveyancer will check that your mortgage and funds are in place, and that you are ready, before exchanging.
How do I know I am registered as the owner?
Your conveyancer applies to HM Land Registry after completion. Once the application is processed, they will usually send you a copy of the updated register showing you as owner. Registration can take some time, but your ownership is protected while the application is pending.
What happens to my deeds?
For registered property, the official record is held electronically by HM Land Registry, so there may be few paper deeds. Your conveyancer will send you copies of the key documents, such as the register and any guarantees. Keep these safe, as they will be useful when you sell.
What if I find a problem after moving in?
If something turns out to be different from what the seller said in their answers or the contract, you may have a claim. Speak to your conveyancer or a solicitor as soon as possible and keep any evidence. What you can do depends on what was said and agreed before exchange.
Do I need to tell anyone that I own the property now?
Your conveyancer deals with HM Land Registry and the tax return. You will usually need to update the council tax account, utility suppliers, your insurance and your address with banks and other organisations yourself. For leasehold property, your conveyancer normally gives notice of the purchase and any mortgage to the landlord or managing agent.
Should I make a will after buying a home?
Buying a home is a common moment to think about a will, particularly if you own with someone else as tenants in common, have children or have a partner you are not married to. Your conveyancer may be able to help or refer you to a colleague who handles wills.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm usually contacts you within one working day of your enquiry, and often sooner. If you have a deadline, such as an agent asking for your conveyancer's details today or a seller who wants to see that you are ready to proceed, mention it on the form so the firm can respond quickly.
When should I instruct a conveyancer to sell?
Ideally when you put your home on the market. The firm can carry out identity checks, obtain your title, send you the forms and request any leasehold information straight away. That way the contract pack is ready to send on the day you accept an offer, which can make your property more attractive to buyers.
What does my conveyancer need from me at the start?
Your conveyancer will need photo ID, proof of address, your mortgage lender and account details, and the details of your estate agent. They will also ask you to complete the property information form and the fittings and contents form, and to send any certificates, guarantees and planning documents you have.
Do I need my original deeds?
For most registered properties, the official record is held electronically by HM Land Registry, so original paper deeds are not essential. Any old deeds you have can still be useful, especially if they explain rights or boundaries. If your property is unregistered, the deeds are vital and your conveyancer will need to see them.
Do I need an Energy Performance Certificate?
Homes marketed for sale in England and Wales generally need a valid Energy Performance Certificate, and the agent will usually arrange this before marketing begins. If you already have one that is still valid, you can usually use it. Your estate agent can confirm what is needed.
Can I sell if my name has changed since I bought?
Yes. If you have changed your name through marriage, civil partnership or deed poll, your conveyancer will usually ask for evidence, such as a marriage certificate, and may update the register or explain the change to the buyer. It is a common issue and rarely causes a problem if dealt with early.
What if I own the property with someone else?
All registered owners usually need to agree to the sale and sign the contract and transfer. If one owner has died, your conveyancer will need to see the death certificate and may need probate depending on how the property was owned. If owners disagree about selling, it is a different situation and legal advice is needed.
Can I use the same firm as my buyer?
Professional rules usually prevent one firm acting for both buyer and seller in a residential sale, because their interests conflict. There are limited exceptions, but in practice most transactions have a separate firm on each side. Your conveyancer will tell you if a conflict arises.
How detailed should my answers be on the forms?
Answer accurately and from your own knowledge. If you are not sure, say so rather than guessing, and do not leave out something you know about because you think it is minor. The buyer relies on your answers, and inaccurate replies can lead to claims after the sale. Your conveyancer can help with anything you find unclear.
