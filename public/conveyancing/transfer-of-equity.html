About transfer of equity

A transfer of equity changes who owns a property without it being sold. People do this when they marry or move in together, when a relationship ends, or when a parent wants to add a child to the title.

If there is a mortgage, the lender has to agree. The conveyancer prepares the transfer deed, deals with the lender and registers the change at HM Land Registry. Stamp Duty Land Tax can apply in some cases, and the firm will tell you if it does.

A good conveyancer keeps you updated without you having to chase, explains anything unusual in the paperwork in plain English and works with your estate agent and mortgage broker to keep the chain moving. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they work and what they need from you to get started.

Transfer of equity explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A transfer of equity is a change in who legally owns a property, without the property being sold on the open market. One or more people are added to the title, removed from it, or both. The property stays where it is and the people living there may not change at all, but the record of ownership at HM Land Registry is updated to show the new owners.

The phrase can be confusing, because equity usually means the value of a home above the mortgage. In this context it means ownership. A transfer of equity might involve money changing hands, such as one partner paying the other for their share, or it might be a gift with nothing paid at all.

Although no one is buying in the usual sense, a transfer of equity is still a legal transaction with real consequences. The people giving up ownership lose their rights in the property. The people taking it on may take on responsibility for a mortgage. There can be tax to think about, and the lender usually has to agree. That is why a conveyancer is normally involved.

A transfer of equity is different from selling a share to an outsider or selling the whole property. The people involved usually already know each other well, and there is no estate agent or chain. That can make it feel informal, but it is worth treating with the same care as a purchase. The transfer deed is permanent once registered, and undoing it later usually needs the agreement of everyone involved or a court order.

What a transfer of equity can involve

  • Adding a spouse, civil partner or partner to the title
  • Removing an owner after a separation or divorce
  • A parent adding an adult child, or transferring the home to them
  • One co-owner buying out another, such as siblings who inherited together
  • Changing the shares in which co-owners hold the property
  • Moving a property between an individual and a trust

Changing the ownership of a home can affect inheritance, tax, benefits and family relationships, so it is worth understanding the wider picture before going ahead.

The exact steps depend on whether there is a mortgage, whether money is being paid and whether the owners agree. Here is the usual shape of a transfer of equity in England and Wales.

1. Taking instructions

The conveyancer checks the identity of everyone involved, finds out what is being transferred and why, and checks the title. They also look at whether anyone needs separate advice because their interests are different.

2. The lender

If there is a mortgage, the lender is asked whether it will consent to the change. The lender may want to assess anyone being added or the person who will be left with the mortgage. Sometimes it agrees to the change on the existing mortgage, and sometimes it requires a new mortgage altogether.

3. Preparing the transfer deed

The conveyancer prepares a transfer deed, which sets out who is transferring the property to whom and whether any money is being paid. Everyone named in the deed signs it, usually in front of an independent witness.

4. Tax check

The conveyancer considers whether any property tax is payable. If it is, a return must be filed and the tax paid within a strict deadline.

5. Completion

On the agreed day, any money changes hands, any new mortgage completes, and the transfer becomes effective. If someone is leaving the mortgage, the lender releases them at this point.

6. Registration

The conveyancer applies to HM Land Registry to register the new owners and any new mortgage. Once registered, the updated title shows who now owns the property.

What you will need

  • Photo ID and proof of address for everyone being added or removed
  • The property address and, if you have it, the title number
  • Your mortgage lender and account details
  • Details of any money being paid for a share and where it is coming from
  • Any court order, separation agreement or will that the transfer is based on

Joint tenants or tenants in common

When more than one person owns a property, they choose how to hold it. Joint tenants own the whole together, and when one dies the survivor takes it automatically. Tenants in common each own a share, which passes under their will or the rules on intestacy. A transfer of equity is a good moment to think about which suits you, and some owners also sign a declaration of trust recording how the value would be split.

People transfer equity for many different reasons. Each brings its own points to think about.

Moving in together or marrying

When one person already owns a home and their partner moves in, couples often want both names on the title. The partner joining may be contributing to the mortgage or putting money into the home. A transfer of equity makes them a legal owner. A declaration of trust can record what each person has contributed if they want that reflected if they later sell or separate.

Separation and divorce

When a relationship ends, one partner often keeps the home and the other is removed from the title and the mortgage. The person staying usually needs to show the lender they can afford the mortgage on their own, or take out a new one. For married couples and civil partners, the terms are often set out in a financial order from the family court, and the conveyancer will work to that order.

Helping children

Some parents add a child to the title of the family home, or transfer a property to them. This can have consequences for tax, for the parent's security in their own home and for what happens if the child divorces, becomes bankrupt or dies. It is important that everyone understands the effects before signing.

Inherited property

Where several people inherit a home together, one may want to buy the others out. This is often done as a transfer of equity once the property has been transferred to the beneficiaries, or sometimes as part of the estate administration.

Changing shares

Co-owners sometimes want to change the proportions in which they own a property, for example after one has paid off more of the mortgage. This can be done by transfer, by declaration of trust, or both, depending on what is being recorded.

Most transfers of equity involve a mortgaged property, and the lender's position is often the deciding factor in how and when the change can happen.

Why the lender must agree

Your mortgage is a contract between the lender and the borrowers. If someone is being removed, the lender is losing a person it can look to for repayment. If someone is being added, it needs to check them. Most mortgage terms, and most registered titles, prevent the ownership being changed without the lender's consent.

Possible outcomes

  • The lender agrees to the change on the existing mortgage, often called a transfer of equity with consent
  • The lender requires a new mortgage in the new owners' names, sometimes with a new product
  • The remaining owner remortgages to a different lender and pays off the old mortgage
  • The lender declines, and the owners need to find another way forward

Releasing someone from the mortgage

Being removed from the title does not automatically remove you from the mortgage. Unless the lender formally releases you, you may still be liable for the debt even though you no longer own the property. Making sure the lender's release is part of the transaction is one of the most important things for the person leaving.

Affordability

Lenders apply their normal checks to the person or people who will be responsible for the mortgage after the transfer. If one person cannot afford it on their own, they may need a guarantor, a longer term, a smaller loan or help from a broker.

No mortgage

If the property is mortgage free, there is no lender to consult and the process is usually quicker. The legal and tax issues still need checking, and a conveyancer still prepares and registers the transfer.

Tax is one of the most misunderstood parts of a transfer of equity. Whether any is due depends on what is being given in return for the share and on the reason for the transfer.

Property tax

Stamp Duty Land Tax in England and Land Transaction Tax in Wales can apply to a transfer of equity even though nobody is buying in the usual way. Tax can be due if the person receiving the share pays money for it or takes over responsibility for part of the mortgage. Some transfers between separating spouses and civil partners under a court order or formal arrangement are relieved from the tax. The rules and thresholds change, so your conveyancer will check your position.

Other taxes

Capital Gains Tax can apply when a share in a property that is not someone's main home is transferred, and there are special rules for spouses, civil partners and separating couples. Inheritance Tax rules can affect gifts of property, particularly where the person giving the share carries on living there. Your conveyancer may recommend speaking to an accountant or tax adviser alongside the legal work.

Legal costs

Firms usually charge a fixed fee for a transfer of equity, with extra charges if a new mortgage, a declaration of trust or separate representation is needed. Disbursements usually include HM Land Registry fees, title copies, identity checks and bank transfer fees. Some lenders charge their own administration fee for consenting.

A transfer of equity is often straightforward on paper but can have long lasting effects. These are some of the wider points owners commonly think about.

Independent advice

Where people's interests are different, such as a parent gifting a share to a child or a separating couple, one firm may not be able to act for everyone. A lender may also insist that someone giving up their share, or guaranteeing a mortgage, takes independent legal advice so it is clear they understand what they are signing.

Pressure and capacity

Everyone signing must understand the transaction and agree to it freely. If an owner is elderly, unwell or under pressure from family, the conveyancer will take extra care. If someone lacks capacity, an attorney or a court appointed deputy may need to act for them, and there are limits on what gifts they can make.

Benefits and care

Giving away a share in a home can be relevant to means tested benefits and to how care costs are assessed. Rules on deliberately reducing assets can mean a gift is ignored for those purposes. Anyone considering a transfer for these reasons should take specialist advice.

Wills

A change in ownership often means a will needs reviewing, especially if the owners now hold as tenants in common or if someone has been removed from a home they used to share. Many firms can help with a will at the same time.

Credit and bankruptcy

If a new co-owner later runs into financial trouble, their share in the property could be affected by creditors. Equally, transferring property to avoid creditors can be challenged. A conveyancer or solicitor can explain how this works in general and point you to specialist advice.

Keeping records

Whatever the reason for the transfer, it helps to keep copies of the signed deed, any declaration of trust, any court order and the updated register in a safe place. If the property is sold years later, or one of the owners dies, these documents can save time and avoid arguments about who owns what and in what shares.

Transfer of equity questions and answers

Do I need the lender’s permission?
Yes, if the property is mortgaged. The lender will want to check the person being added can afford the mortgage, or that the remaining owner can after someone is removed.
Does a transfer of equity cost Stamp Duty?
It can, depending on whether money or debt changes hands. Your conveyancer will check and explain.
Can the same firm act for everyone?
Sometimes. Where owners’ interests differ, for example after a separation, the firm may recommend that one person takes separate advice.
Does Leaflegal do the conveyancing?
No. Leaflegal is a free introduction service. The firm we introduce you to does the legal work and is responsible for it.
Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
What does transfer of equity mean?
It means changing who legally owns a property without selling it on the open market. Someone is added to or removed from the title, or the property passes from one owner to another, often between family members or partners. The change is recorded at HM Land Registry once the transfer is complete.
Do I need a conveyancer for a transfer of equity?
If there is a mortgage, the lender will almost always require a conveyancer to act for it. Even without a mortgage, the transfer deed must be correctly prepared, signed and registered, and tax needs checking. Mistakes can be expensive to put right, so most people use a regulated firm.
How long does a transfer of equity take?
A simple transfer with no mortgage can be quick once everyone has signed. Where a lender is involved, timescales depend on how long the lender takes to assess and consent, or to issue a new mortgage offer. Separation cases can take longer if the terms are still being negotiated.
Can I transfer a share rather than the whole property?
Yes. A transfer of equity can add someone as a co-owner, so they own the property jointly with you, rather than transferring it outright. The way you hold the property, and any declaration of trust, determines how the value would be split between you.
What is a declaration of trust?
It is a legal document recording how co-owners share the beneficial ownership of a property, such as who put in what money and how proceeds would be split on a sale. It can be signed at the same time as a transfer of equity. It is particularly common where contributions are unequal or owners are not married.
Do all the owners need to sign?
Usually, yes. Everyone transferring their interest and everyone receiving one normally signs the transfer deed. If an owner cannot sign, for example because they lack capacity, someone with legal authority such as a registered attorney or court appointed deputy may need to sign on their behalf.
Is a transfer of equity the same as a sale?
Not quite. There is no open market sale, estate agent or chain, and often no money changes hands. But legally it is still a transfer of land, so many of the same steps apply, including identity checks, a signed deed, a tax check and registration at HM Land Registry. Where money is paid, it can look much like a small purchase.
Can I change from joint tenants to tenants in common without a transfer?
Yes. Co-owners who hold as joint tenants can usually change to tenants in common by a process called severance, often by serving a written notice and registering a restriction at HM Land Registry. No transfer of equity is needed because the owners stay the same. Your conveyancer can explain how severance works and when people use it.
Does my lender have to agree?
If the property is mortgaged, the lender's consent is almost always needed. The lender will check that whoever will be responsible for the mortgage after the change can afford it. Some lenders allow the change on the current mortgage, while others require a new mortgage or a remortgage elsewhere.
Will I be removed from the mortgage automatically?
No. Coming off the title does not take you off the mortgage. The lender has to formally release you, usually as part of the transaction. If this does not happen, you could still be liable for the debt. Your conveyancer will make sure the release is dealt with properly.
Can I add my partner to the deeds but not the mortgage?
Most lenders require everyone on the title to be on the mortgage, although policies differ. Some may allow it in limited situations. Your conveyancer or broker can check your lender's rules. If your partner cannot be added to the mortgage, other options may need to be considered.
What if the lender refuses?
If the lender will not consent, the owners may need to look for a new mortgage with another lender, change the plan, or wait until circumstances change. In separation cases, the court or the owners may need to consider other arrangements. Your conveyancer can explain the options available.
Can a transfer of equity be done alongside a remortgage?
Yes, and it often is. A new lender can lend to the new owners at the same time as the ownership changes. The old mortgage is paid off, the transfer and new mortgage are completed together, and both are registered at HM Land Registry.
Does the lender charge for a transfer of equity?
Some lenders charge an administration fee for considering and consenting to a change of ownership on an existing mortgage. If a new mortgage is needed, there may be arrangement or valuation fees. Your conveyancer or broker can confirm what your lender charges.
What if the property has an equity loan or is shared ownership?
If you bought with a government backed equity loan or through shared ownership, changing the owners usually needs consent from the scheme administrator or the housing provider as well as the lender. There may be rules about who can be an owner and extra fees. Your conveyancer will check the scheme documents and apply for any consents needed.
Can I take my ex-partner off the title?
Yes, if they agree or a court orders it. Your ex-partner signs a transfer deed giving up their interest, and if there is a mortgage the lender must agree to release them. Each of you may need separate legal advice, as your interests are different.
Do we need a court order?
Married couples and civil partners often record their financial agreement in a court order as part of the divorce or dissolution, and the transfer follows that order. Unmarried couples do not divorce, so they usually rely on an agreement between themselves. A family solicitor can explain what is needed in your situation.
What if my ex will not sign?
If one owner refuses to cooperate, the property cannot usually be transferred without their signature. For married couples and civil partners, the family court can make orders about the home. For unmarried couples, other court applications may be possible. This is outside conveyancing and needs advice from a solicitor.
Is there tax when an ex-partner is bought out?
Some transfers between separating spouses and civil partners under a court order or formal agreement are relieved from property tax. For unmarried couples, tax may be due if money is paid or the mortgage is taken over. Capital gains rules also differ. Your conveyancer will check the property tax position and may suggest a tax adviser.
Can the same firm act for both of us?
Sometimes, if your interests do not conflict and you both agree. In many separation cases, the interests are different, so firms often act for one person and recommend that the other takes separate advice. The firm will explain its position at the start.
Should the transfer wait until the divorce is final?
It depends on the circumstances. For married couples and civil partners, a financial order often only takes effect once the final order of divorce or dissolution has been made. The timing of the transfer usually follows that order. Your family solicitor and conveyancer will coordinate so that the transfer happens at the right point.
What happens to the deposit I put in if I am removed?
What you receive for your share depends on what you agree with your former partner or what a court orders, not just on the original deposit. A declaration of trust signed when you bought may be relevant. The conveyancer carries out the agreed terms, while a family solicitor can explain how agreements and orders on the home are usually reached.
Can I add my child to the deeds of my home?
Yes, but it can have significant effects. Your child would own a share, which could be affected by their divorce, debts or death. It may also have tax and benefits consequences, and it may not achieve what some parents expect for inheritance. Everyone should understand the effects before going ahead.
Can I gift my home to my children and keep living there?
You can transfer ownership, but if you continue living there rent free the gift may not be effective for Inheritance Tax purposes. It may also be relevant to how care costs are assessed. These areas are complex, and specialist legal and tax advice is usually needed before making such a gift.
We inherited a house together. Can one of us buy the others out?
Yes. Once the property is in the beneficiaries' names, or as part of the estate administration, one beneficiary can buy the others' shares. The transfer deed records the change and any money paid. Tax and mortgage issues are checked as with any other transfer of equity.
What if a family member is pressured into a transfer?
Everyone giving up or taking on ownership must do so freely and with a full understanding. Conveyancers watch for signs of pressure, and lenders often require independent advice for someone giving up their share. If there are concerns, the transaction may be paused until they are resolved.
Can I transfer my home into a trust?
It is possible to transfer a property into a trust, and some people do this for estate planning reasons. Trusts have their own tax rules, ongoing duties for the trustees and possible effects on care fee and benefit assessments. This is specialist work, and a solicitor who handles trusts and estates can explain how it would work in general.
Can my child buy a share of my home from me?
Yes. A child can pay for a share of a parent's home, either with their own money or with a mortgage, and the transfer deed records the payment. Property tax may be due depending on the amount paid, and lenders may need to approve both the transfer and any new lending. Your conveyancer will explain the steps.
How much does a transfer of equity cost?
Most firms charge a fixed fee, with extras for a new mortgage, a declaration of trust or other complexities. Disbursements, VAT and any lender fees are added. Property tax, if due, is separate. The firm we introduce you to will give you a quote based on your circumstances.
Who pays the legal costs?
It is up to the owners to agree who pays. In separation cases, the court order or agreement may say who is responsible. In family transfers, the person making the gift often pays. If separate firms act for different owners, each usually pays their own firm unless agreed otherwise.
Is a transfer of equity cheaper than a purchase?
Usually the legal fee is lower than for a full purchase, because there is no seller's conveyancer, no contract negotiation and often no searches. The cost rises if a new mortgage, separate advice for one party, a declaration of trust or a tax return is needed. Asking for a written quote that lists each part makes it easier to compare.

How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm usually contacts you within one working day of your enquiry, and often sooner. If there is a court order with a deadline, a remortgage date or a lender's offer that is due to expire, mention it on the form so the firm knows to prioritise the timetable when it gets in touch.
When should I look for a conveyancer?
Many first time buyers start looking as soon as they begin viewing properties, so that a firm is ready the moment an offer is accepted. Instructing early means the estate agent can send the memorandum of sale straight away and the firm can complete identity and deposit checks before the seller's paperwork arrives. You usually do not pay for legal work until the purchase is under way, but check the firm's terms about any upfront charges.
Do I need a solicitor or will a licensed conveyancer do?
Either can handle a residential purchase. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and licensed conveyancers by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Both must follow rules on client money, complaints and insurance. For a first time buyer, the more important questions are whether the firm is on your lender's panel, how clearly it explains things, and whether it has experience with any scheme you are using.
What will the firm ask me for at the start?
Expect to provide photo identification, proof of address and details of where your deposit is coming from. The firm will also want the estate agent's memorandum of sale, your mortgage in principle or offer, and details of any scheme such as a Lifetime ISA or shared ownership. If someone is giving you money towards the deposit, the firm may ask for their details at this stage too.
Can I buy a home on my own?
Yes. Many first time buyers buy alone. The legal process is the same as for joint buyers, though your mortgage will be based on your income alone. Some people buy with a guarantor or with a family member on the mortgage, and your conveyancer will explain how these arrangements work and whether anyone involved needs separate advice.
What does being chain free mean for me?
As a first time buyer you have no property to sell, so you are often described as chain free on your side. This can make your offer more attractive to sellers. It does not mean the purchase will be quick, because the seller may still be part of a chain above you. It simply means there is one fewer sale that needs to happen before yours can complete.
Is an offer legally binding once it is accepted?
No. In England and Wales, neither buyer nor seller is legally bound until contracts are exchanged. Until then either side can pull out, and the price can be renegotiated. This is why it is important to let your conveyancer finish the checks before you exchange, and why gazumping, where a seller accepts a higher offer late on, can still happen.
Does it matter whether the home is freehold or leasehold?
Yes. With a freehold you own the building and the land it stands on with no time limit. With a leasehold, which is common for flats, you own the home for a fixed number of years and usually pay a service charge and sometimes ground rent. Leasehold purchases need extra checks and the legal fee is often higher. Your conveyancer will explain what the tenure means for the property you are buying.
What if the seller pulls out before exchange?
Because nobody is bound until exchange, a seller can withdraw, and you may lose money already spent on searches, a survey or a mortgage fee. Some buyers take out insurance against this, sometimes called home buying protection, though it is optional. Your conveyancer cannot stop a seller pulling out, but will tell you what costs have been incurred so far and what can be reused if you find another home.
