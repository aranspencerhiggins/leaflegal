Leaflegal works in three simple steps. You tell us what you need help with, we introduce you to one regulated firm that handles that kind of matter, and the firm contacts you to talk it through. The whole thing is free for you, and nothing you do on our website commits you to instructing anyone.

This page goes into more detail about each step, what happens behind the scenes and what you can expect from the firm. If you just want to get started, the form takes about two minutes.

1. You tell us what is going on

The form starts by asking which area of law your matter falls under. It then asks a handful of questions that are specific to that area. For a house purchase it might ask whether the property is freehold or leasehold. For a driving matter it might ask whether you have a court date. For a business debt it might ask roughly how much is owed and how old the invoice is. These questions are the same ones a firm's intake team would ask on a first call.

At the end you give your name, a mobile number and an email address. You can also add a short note. This is a good place to mention a deadline, a language preference or the best time to call.

2. We check it and introduce one firm

When you submit, we check that your enquiry is complete and that it falls within the areas we cover. We then pass it to one regulated firm that handles that type of matter and covers your area. For immigration, we pass it to a regulated immigration adviser. The firm receives your answers and the consent you gave, so it knows you asked to be contacted.

We never pass the same enquiry to several firms. That is a deliberate choice. It means you are not flooded with calls, and the firm that contacts you knows it is the only one introduced to you by us. If you would like to speak to other firms as well, you are free to contact them yourself.

3. The firm gets in touch

The firm usually contacts you the same working day, most often by phone and sometimes by email or text first. On that first contact it will ask a few more questions, explain whether and how it can help and tell you about its fees. From that point you deal with the firm directly. Leaflegal is not part of your conversations with the firm and does not see the advice it gives.

Why one firm and not several

Some websites send your details to three, four or more firms at once. That can mean a lot of calls in a short space of time, often from firms that are competing to speak to you first. We think one careful introduction is better for most people. You get a single, focused conversation with a firm that handles your kind of matter, and you keep control over whether you look further.

It also matters to the firm. Because each enquiry goes to one firm only, the firm can afford to spend time on a proper first call rather than racing other firms to the phone. That usually makes for a calmer and more useful conversation.

You do not need to prepare anything special before filling in the form. A little thought beforehand can make the first call more useful, though, and help the firm judge how quickly it needs to move.

Know roughly what kind of help you need

You do not need the legal name for your problem. It is enough to know that you are selling a flat, that you have received a court summons, or that a customer has not paid. If you are not sure which area fits, pick the nearest one and explain in the notes.

Gather the key dates

Many legal matters have deadlines that cannot easily be moved. Court hearings, reply dates on letters, completion dates, licence renewal dates and dates on immigration letters are all worth noting down. Put the most important one in the form.

Find the paperwork

Letters, contracts, court papers, notices, invoices and emails all help the firm understand your matter. You do not need to send them through Leaflegal and we do not ask you to upload anything. Just keep them handy for the call. A photo on your phone is usually enough at the start.

Think about what you want

It helps to have a rough idea of the outcome you are hoping for, even if it changes once you have spoken to a lawyer. You might want a quick sale, an agreed arrangement for your children, to keep your licence or to be paid what you are owed. The firm will tell you what is realistic in your situation.

Check your contact details

Most problems with introductions come from a mistyped phone number or email address. Take a second to check them before you submit. Use a number you will answer, and let the firm know in the notes if there are times when you cannot talk.

If your matter involves more than one area

Some situations cross over. A separation might involve selling the family home. A business might be owed money and also need help with a licence. If that applies to you, choose the area that feels most pressing and mention the other in the notes. You can also complete the form a second time for a separate matter.

If someone else is involved

If you are enquiring for a relative, a partner or a business you work for, make sure they are happy for you to do so. The firm will need to speak to the person or business it would be acting for, and it will usually need their own identity documents before it can take instructions.

Once you press submit you will see a confirmation on screen. Your enquiry is checked and passed to the firm as soon as possible. Here is what usually happens next.

Straight away

Your enquiry is recorded together with the consent you gave and the time you gave it. That record is sent to the firm with your answers, so the firm can see that you asked to be contacted about this matter.

Usually the same working day

The firm calls or emails to introduce itself. If you submit outside office hours, at the weekend or on a bank holiday, the firm will usually be in touch on the next working day. Some firms send a short text or email first so you know who is calling.

On the first call

The firm asks some questions about your situation, explains how it could help and talks about fees. It may ask you to send copies of documents so it can look at them properly. It will not expect you to make a decision there and then.

Before any work starts

If you want to go ahead, the firm sends you its terms of business and confirms its fees in writing. If it is a firm of solicitors, it must also tell you in writing about the fee it pays Leaflegal for the introduction. The firm will also carry out identity checks, which regulated firms are required to do before they act for a new client.

If you do not hear anything

Check your voicemail, your text messages and your junk email folder. Firms often try more than once. If you still have not heard anything by the end of the next working day, contact us and we will chase it up.

What the firm can see

The firm sees the answers you gave, your contact details, the note you added and the record of your consent. It does not see anything else about your visit to our website. If the firm needs more information, it will ask you directly.

The first call with the firm is a chance for both sides to find out whether they are a good fit. It is not a commitment, and you do not have to agree to anything on the call.

What the firm will want to know

The firm will usually ask you to explain what has happened in your own words, what stage things are at and what deadlines you are facing. It may ask about the other people involved, so it can check it does not already act for them. That check is called a conflict check and every regulated firm has to do it.

What you can ask

  • Have you dealt with many matters like mine
  • Who will handle my matter day to day, and what is their role
  • How do you charge, and what is included
  • What are the likely next steps and timescales
  • How will you keep me updated
  • Is there anything I need to do urgently

Advice and information

Some firms give a short amount of general guidance on the first call free of charge. Others prefer to arrange a paid consultation before they look at the details. Both are normal. Ask the firm how it works so there are no surprises.

Taking time to decide

You can end the call by saying you would like to think about it. A good firm will understand. If there is a deadline coming up, ask the firm how long you have to decide before it becomes a problem.

If the call is not convenient

If the firm calls at a bad time, it is fine to ask them to ring back later or to arrange a time by email. Many firms can also offer a video call or an appointment at their office if you would prefer to meet in person. Tell them what works for you.

If you need support on the call

If you would like someone with you on the call, or you need information in a different format or language, let the firm know. Regulated firms are used to making reasonable adjustments, and it helps them to know in advance.

Instructing a firm simply means asking it to act for you. Here is what that usually involves once you decide to go ahead.

Terms of business

The firm sends you a client care letter and its terms of business. These explain who will do the work, how fees and billing work, what the firm will and will not do, and how to complain if something goes wrong. Read them carefully and ask about anything that is not clear.

Identity checks

Regulated firms have to confirm who their clients are. You will usually be asked for photo identification and proof of address, and sometimes information about where money for a transaction is coming from. Many firms use an online identity check that you can complete on your phone.

Money on account

Some firms ask for a payment before they start, which is held towards their fees or disbursements. Others bill at set stages. The firm will tell you what it needs and when.

Working with the firm

From here on you deal with the firm directly. Keep copies of everything you send and receive, reply promptly to requests for information and tell the firm straight away if something changes. If you are unhappy with the service, raise it with the firm first. Every regulated firm must have a complaints procedure.

Leaflegal's role after the introduction

Once you are introduced, Leaflegal steps back. We do not manage your matter or see your files. We are still happy to hear from you if something about the introduction went wrong, or if a firm did not treat you fairly.

Costs and how Leaflegal is paid

The firm's own charges, and any disbursements such as court or search fees, are agreed between you and the firm in its terms of business.

That depends entirely on the type of matter and the people involved. A house purchase, a lease extension, a divorce and a court case all run on very different timescales. The firm will give you a realistic idea once it understands your situation.

The firm will explain its fees on the first call and confirm them in writing before any work starts. Ask whether the fee is fixed or hourly, what is included, what disbursements are likely and whether VAT is included in the figures.

No. Leaflegal introduces people who are looking for paid legal help. Legal aid is only available for certain kinds of matter and depends on your circumstances. If you think you might qualify, the legal aid checker on GOV.UK can tell you more and point you to a provider.

Not by Leaflegal. The firm may ask for money on account once you decide to instruct it, usually to cover early disbursements or the first stage of work. It will explain this in its terms of business, and you do not have to pay anything until you have agreed to go ahead.
Who sees my answers?
We share your answers and contact details with the one firm we introduce you to, and only because you agreed to that on the form. We keep a record so that we can deal with questions and complaints. We do not share the details of your matter with advertisers or data brokers.

We record the consent wording you agreed to and when you agreed, and send that with your enquiry. It shows the firm that you asked to be contacted about this matter. It also protects you, because it is clear exactly what you agreed to.

Once your enquiry is submitted it is passed to the firm quickly, so it may already have been sent. If you change your mind, tell the firm when it contacts you, or email us and we will let the firm know. There is no charge and no need to explain.

No. Leaflegal does not give legal advice or immigration advice at any stage. We may ask questions to understand what kind of matter you have, but only so we can introduce the right firm. The firm's lawyers will give you advice about your own situation.
Is my enquiry kept secure?
Your enquiry is sent to the firm securely and we limit who can see it. Once the firm has your details, it is responsible for keeping them safe under its own data protection and confidentiality duties. Our privacy policy explains how we look after your information and how long we keep it.
Can I make a complaint about Leaflegal?
Yes. Email us with complaint in the subject line, explaining what went wrong and what you would like us to do. We acknowledge complaints within three working days and aim to send a full written response within fifteen working days. Complaints about the firm should go to the firm first.

More questions people ask

What is conveyancing?
Conveyancing is the legal work involved in transferring the ownership of property, or registering a mortgage against it. It includes checking the title, carrying out searches, preparing or reviewing the contract, handling the money and registering the change at HM Land Registry. It applies to buying, selling, remortgaging and changing who owns a home.
Do I legally have to use a conveyancer?
It is possible to do your own conveyancing in some situations, but it is rare and difficult. If you have a mortgage, your lender will almost always insist that a regulated firm acts for it. Most people use a professional because the legal and financial risks of getting things wrong are high and hard to put right later.
What is the difference between freehold and leasehold?
Freehold means you own the building and the land it stands on with no time limit. Leasehold means you own the right to occupy the property for a set number of years under a lease, with a landlord who owns the freehold. Leasehold property usually comes with ground rent, service charges and extra checks during conveyancing.
What is HM Land Registry?
HM Land Registry keeps the official record of who owns most land and property in England and Wales. After a purchase, remortgage or transfer, your conveyancer applies to update the register. Some older properties are still unregistered, and the first sale or mortgage usually triggers an application for first registration, which takes more work.
What are searches?
Searches are enquiries made to the local authority, water companies and other bodies to find out about things that affect a property but may not be obvious from viewing it. They can reveal planning history, road adoption, drainage, flood risk, environmental issues and mining in some areas. Lenders usually require them when you buy with a mortgage.
When does a sale or purchase become legally binding?
In England and Wales, a sale or purchase only becomes binding when contracts are exchanged. Before that, either side can withdraw without penalty, even late in the process. Once contracts are exchanged, pulling out usually means losing the deposit or facing a claim for losses, so the timing of exchange matters a great deal.
What is the difference between a survey and a valuation?
A mortgage valuation is carried out for the lender to check the property is worth enough to lend against. It is not a detailed inspection and may not tell you about defects. A survey is commissioned by the buyer and looks at the condition of the property in more depth. Your conveyancer deals with the legal side and does not inspect the building, so many buyers arrange a survey as well.
What is a chain?
A chain is a series of linked sales and purchases that depend on each other. If you are selling to someone who is also selling, and you are buying from someone who is buying elsewhere, you are in a chain. Everyone in it usually needs to exchange contracts and complete on the same day, so a delay anywhere can affect everyone else.
Should I use the firm my estate agent recommends?
You are free to choose any firm. Agents sometimes receive a referral fee for recommending a conveyancer, and they should tell you if they do. A recommended firm may be perfectly good, but it is sensible to compare what is on offer and to choose a firm you are comfortable with.
What is the Conveyancing Quality Scheme?
It is an accreditation run by the Law Society for solicitors' firms that meet certain standards in residential conveyancing. Many lenders require their panel firms to hold it. It is one useful sign of a firm's standards, but it is not the only one, and licensed conveyancers have their own regulatory framework.
Does it matter if the firm is local?
Much conveyancing is now done remotely, so a firm does not need to be near the property. A local firm may know common local issues, such as mining areas or flood zones. An online firm may offer longer opening hours and case tracking. Responsiveness and experience usually matter more than distance.
Can I change firms partway through?
Yes, you can move your file to another firm, but there may be charges for work already done, and the change can add delay while the new firm catches up. If you are unhappy, it is usually worth raising your concerns with the firm first and using its complaints process.
What if I am unhappy with my conveyancer?
Every regulated firm must have a complaints process and tell you about it at the start. If the firm does not resolve your complaint, you can usually take it to the Legal Ombudsman. Concerns about professional conduct can go to the Solicitors Regulation Authority or the Council for Licensed Conveyancers.
Will the same person deal with my file throughout?
It depends on the firm. Some assign a named conveyancer who handles the file from start to finish, while others use a team approach with several people involved. Ask how the firm works and how you can get updates, as this often matters more to clients than they expect.
How do I protect myself from email fraud?
Criminals sometimes target property transactions by sending fake emails that appear to come from a conveyancer, asking for money to be sent to a different account. Reputable firms warn clients about this at the start and will not change their bank details by email. Before sending any money, call your firm on a number you already know and confirm the account details.
