Taxi licences explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Taxis and private hire vehicles are licensed by local councils in England and Wales, and by Transport for London in London. The licences are separate from the ordinary DVLA driving licence. A driver needs a council licence, often called a badge, before they can carry passengers for hire, and the vehicle and, for private hire, the operator taking bookings need their own licences too.

There are two main types of service. Hackney carriages, often called taxis or black cabs, can be hailed in the street and wait on ranks in the area where they are licensed. Private hire vehicles, which include minicabs and most app based services, must be booked in advance through a licensed operator. Outside London, hackney carriages are licensed mainly under the Town Police Clauses Act 1847, and private hire under the Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1976. London has its own laws.

Each council sets its own policy within the law. Requirements for medical checks, knowledge tests, vehicle age, signage, training and conduct can differ a great deal from one area to the next. The Department for Transport has published statutory standards that councils must take into account, which has brought more consistency on safeguarding and suitability, but local differences remain.

Why drivers and operators need help

For most drivers, the council licence is their livelihood. A refusal, suspension or revocation can stop them working overnight. Councils take passenger safety very seriously, and they can act on complaints, police information or driving convictions, sometimes before any criminal case has finished. Decisions are often made quickly, and the time to respond or appeal is short.

Operators face their own pressures. They must keep proper records, use only licensed drivers and vehicles from the right council and meet the conditions of their operator licence. Problems with a single driver can lead to questions about the operator's systems.

A first application can take some time, because councils usually require several checks before a licence is granted. The exact requirements are set out in each council's policy, so it is worth reading it before you start.

Driver licences

Most councils require an enhanced criminal record check, including a check of the relevant barred lists, a medical assessment, a check of the applicant's DVLA driving record, proof of the right to work in the UK and a knowledge test about the area, the law and safe practice. Many also require safeguarding training, disability awareness training and, in some areas, an English language or communication assessment. Some ask for a minimum period of holding a full driving licence.

Vehicle licences

A vehicle must meet the council's standards on age, size, condition, emissions, insurance and signage, and pass a council test or inspection. Hackney carriages often have stricter requirements, such as being wheelchair accessible in some areas. Vehicle licences usually need renewing more often than driver licences.

Operator licences

An operator must satisfy the council that they are a fit and proper person to run a private hire business. Operators usually need to keep records of bookings, drivers and vehicles, have suitable premises or systems and take care over who they employ in roles such as booking and dispatch. Councils increasingly look at operators' safeguarding policies.

Renewals

Licences are granted for set periods and must be renewed. A renewal is not automatic. The council will look again at the person's record, including any complaints, convictions or points on their driving licence since the last grant. Applying in good time avoids a gap during which you cannot work.

Refusals

If a council is minded to refuse an application, it often invites the applicant to an interview or a hearing first. A refusal can usually be appealed. A solicitor can help present the application in the best light, especially where there is something in the applicant's history the council may be concerned about.

Councils must not grant a driver licence unless they are satisfied that the applicant is a fit and proper person to hold one. The same test applies, in broad terms, to private hire operators. It is the central question in most disputes about taxi and private hire licences.

What the test means

There is no fixed definition, but councils often ask themselves whether they would be comfortable allowing a vulnerable member of their own family to travel alone with the driver late at night. The focus is on passenger safety. The council does not have to be sure that something happened, and it can take into account information that has not led to a conviction.

Convictions and cautions

Most councils have a policy setting out how they treat previous convictions and cautions, often based on national guidance. These policies usually say how long a person should be free of certain types of offence before a licence will normally be granted. Serious violent or sexual offences can lead to a refusal in almost all cases, while minor or very old matters may carry less weight. Policies are a starting point, and councils must still consider each case on its own facts.

Driving record

Points on a DVLA licence can affect a taxi or private hire licence, as can a driving ban. Many councils treat a certain number of points, or particular offences such as using a mobile phone while driving, as a reason to review a licence. A driver facing a motoring prosecution should be aware of the possible effect on their council licence.

Complaints and allegations

Councils receive complaints from passengers, other drivers and the police. Some are minor, such as route choice or manner. Others are serious, such as allegations of assault or inappropriate behaviour. Councils can act on complaints even where the police take no further action, because the standard they apply is different from a criminal court.

Information sharing

Councils now share information about drivers who have been refused or had licences revoked, through a national register, and are required by law to share certain safeguarding information with other councils. A driver refused in one area cannot assume another council will not find out.

Honesty with the council matters a great deal. Failing to declare a conviction, caution or previous refusal, or not telling the council about an arrest or charge when the licence conditions require it, is often treated as a serious matter in its own right. Many councils require drivers to report certain events within a short time, so it is worth checking the conditions on your licence.

A council can suspend or revoke a driver licence for certain reasons set out in the law. These include a conviction for an offence involving dishonesty, indecency or violence, breaching taxi and private hire legislation, or any other reasonable cause. Vehicle and operator licences can also be suspended or revoked on their own grounds.

When a decision takes effect

Usually, a decision to suspend or revoke a driver licence does not take effect until the time for appealing has passed or any appeal is decided. This lets the driver keep working while challenging the decision. But if the council decides that the interests of public safety require it, the decision can take effect immediately. In that case the driver must stop working straight away.

Immediate suspensions

Immediate suspensions and revocations are usually reserved for serious allegations, such as offences against passengers or other matters of public safety. The council must give reasons. Even where the decision takes effect immediately, the driver can still appeal, and the speed of the appeal process then becomes very important.

Vehicle suspensions

Authorised officers and police officers can suspend a vehicle licence immediately if they find the vehicle unfit for use, for example during a roadside check. The suspension usually lasts until the problem is fixed and the vehicle is re-inspected. Vehicle licences can also be suspended or revoked for other reasons, such as a failed test or a missing insurance certificate.

Council procedure

Before making a decision, most councils invite the driver to give their side, either in an interview with a licensing officer or at a hearing before a licensing sub-committee. Some decisions are made by officers under delegated powers. It is important to respond to any invitation and to take it seriously, because what is said can affect the outcome and any later appeal.

Warnings and penalty points

Many councils use lesser sanctions for less serious matters, such as written warnings or an internal points system for breaches of licence conditions. Building up too many points can lead to a suspension or a review of the licence.

Drivers sometimes assume that a suspension will simply end when a police investigation is dropped. That is not always the case. The council may want to look at the information itself before deciding whether to lift the suspension, and the driver may need to ask for the matter to be reconsidered.

Most councils hear contested taxi and private hire matters before a licensing sub-committee of councillors, although some decisions are delegated to senior officers. Hearings are less formal than court, but they are an important chance to be heard.

Preparing for a hearing

Preparation usually means understanding exactly what the council is concerned about, gathering documents and deciding how best to explain the position. Useful material might include the driver's licensing history, character references from regular customers or operators, evidence of training and any relevant documents from a criminal case. If there is dashcam or app data that helps, it can be important to preserve it early.

At the hearing

The licensing officer usually presents a report. The driver or their representative can then respond, call evidence and answer questions from the councillors. The committee decides and must give reasons. A solicitor can make sure the right points are put across clearly, and that the committee follows a fair process.

It is also important to keep the tone respectful. Committees understand that drivers are worried about their income, but anger or blaming the council rarely helps. A calm, clear explanation, with evidence to back it up, usually makes a better impression.

Appeals to the magistrates' court

Most decisions to refuse, suspend, revoke or refuse to renew a driver licence can be appealed to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being notified. The court hears the case afresh and can consider new evidence. It gives weight to the council's decision and policy, and the appellant usually needs to persuade the court that the decision was wrong. Some decisions can be taken further on appeal to the Crown Court.

Vehicle and operator appeals

Decisions about vehicle and operator licences can also usually be appealed. The court and route of appeal can differ depending on the type of licence and whether it is in London or elsewhere. Your solicitor will confirm the correct route and time limit in your case.

Costs of an appeal

If an appeal fails, the court can order the appellant to pay the council's costs, which can be significant. Your solicitor will explain the strength of the appeal and the likely costs before you decide.

There are council fees for licences and the costs of professional help if you choose to use it.

Council fees

Taxi and private hire licence fees are set locally by each council to cover the cost of running the licensing service. They vary a lot between areas and change from time to time. Applicants also usually pay for medicals, criminal record checks, knowledge tests and training. Check the current amounts on your council's website.

Solicitor fees

Many firms offer fixed fees for help with an interview or hearing before the council. Appeals to the magistrates' court are usually priced separately, sometimes with a fee for preparation and another for attending the hearing. Some firms help with first applications where there is something in the applicant's history that needs explaining.

Thinking about value

For a working driver, the cost of losing a licence for weeks or months can be much greater than the cost of help. Equally, not every case needs a solicitor. The firm can talk through your situation and give you an idea of what help would cost before you decide.

What is the difference between a taxi and a private hire vehicle?
A hackney carriage, often called a taxi or black cab, can be hailed in the street and use ranks in the area where it is licensed. A private hire vehicle must be booked in advance through a licensed operator and cannot pick up people who flag it down. They are licensed under different laws and often have different requirements.
Who licenses taxis and private hire vehicles?
Outside London, the district, borough, city or unitary council licenses drivers, vehicles and operators for its area. In London, Transport for London is responsible. Each licensing authority sets its own policy, fees and requirements within the law, so they differ from one area to another.
What checks do I need for a driver licence?
Most councils require an enhanced criminal record check with barred lists, a medical, a DVLA driving record check, proof of the right to work and a knowledge test. Many also require safeguarding and disability awareness training, and some require an English or communication assessment. Your council's policy sets out the full list.
Can I get a licence with a criminal record?
It depends on the offence, how long ago it was and your record since. Most councils have a policy setting out how long a person should normally be free of certain types of offence. Serious violent or sexual offences usually lead to refusal. Each case is still considered individually. A solicitor can look at your history and the policy.
Can I drive for a private hire operator licensed in a different area?
For private hire, the driver, vehicle and operator generally all need to be licensed by the same council. Hackney carriages can be pre-booked to go to other areas. The rules on cross-border working are complex, and a solicitor or the council can explain how they apply to your work.
What does fit and proper mean?
It is the test councils use to decide whether someone can hold a driver or operator licence. There is no fixed definition, but the focus is on passenger safety and whether the person can be trusted. Councils look at convictions, complaints, driving record and conduct. They do not need a criminal conviction to decide someone is not fit and proper.
Do I need to renew my licence?
Yes. Driver, vehicle and operator licences are granted for set periods and must be renewed. Renewal is not automatic, and the council will look again at your record. Applying in good time avoids a gap during which you cannot work. Check your council's renewal deadlines.
Can I transfer my badge to a new council?
No. A badge issued by one council cannot simply be transferred to another. If you want to work in a different area, you usually need to apply to that council and meet its requirements, which may include its own knowledge test and checks. Your history with the first council will usually need to be declared.
Why might the council suspend or revoke my licence?
Reasons include a conviction for an offence involving dishonesty, indecency or violence, breaches of taxi and private hire law, complaints about conduct, driving offences and points, or any other reasonable cause. The council must act fairly and give reasons. What counts as reasonable cause depends on the facts.
Can my licence be suspended immediately?
Yes, if the council decides that the interests of public safety require it. The suspension or revocation then takes effect straight away and you must stop working. You can still appeal. Without an immediate effect decision, the licence usually continues until the appeal period ends or any appeal is decided.
Can the council act before a police investigation finishes?
Yes. The council applies its own test focused on passenger safety, and it does not have to wait for a criminal case to finish. It can act on information from the police or complaints even if no charge is brought. A solicitor can help you respond while being careful about any related criminal case.
Will points on my driving licence affect my badge?
They can. Many councils treat a certain number of points, or particular offences such as using a mobile phone while driving, as a reason to review a licence. A driving ban will usually mean you cannot drive for hire. If you are facing a motoring prosecution, think about the effect on your badge too.
What happens after a passenger complaint?
The council usually investigates, which may involve asking for your account, checking operator records and speaking to the complainant. Minor complaints may lead to advice or a warning. Serious complaints can lead to a hearing, suspension or revocation. It is important to respond to the council promptly and carefully.
Should I attend an interview with a licensing officer?
It is usually wise to engage with the council's process. An interview is often your first chance to give your side, and what you say may be relied on later. Some drivers ask a solicitor to help them prepare or to attend with them. Check the invitation for details of what will be discussed.
Can my vehicle licence be suspended at the roadside?
Yes. Authorised council officers and police officers can suspend a vehicle licence immediately if they find the vehicle unfit, for example because of a defect. It stays suspended until the problem is fixed and the vehicle is re-inspected. You cannot use it for hire while suspended.
Do I have to tell the council if I am arrested?
Many councils include a condition on driver licences requiring drivers to report arrests, charges, cautions and convictions within a short period. The exact requirement depends on your council's conditions. Failing to report when required can itself lead to action against your licence. A solicitor can check what your conditions say.
What happens at a licensing hearing?
A sub-committee of councillors, or sometimes an officer, considers the case. A licensing officer usually presents a report, and you or your representative can respond, give evidence and answer questions. The committee decides and must give reasons. Hearings are less formal than court but are taken seriously.
Can I bring someone to the hearing?
Yes. You can usually be represented by a solicitor or bring a friend, family member or interpreter, depending on the council's procedure. Character witnesses can sometimes give evidence or provide written references. Check the council's hearing procedure, which is often sent with the notice of hearing.
How do I appeal a revocation?
Most driver licence decisions can be appealed to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being notified. The court hears the case afresh and can consider new evidence. Some decisions can be appealed further to the Crown Court. The time limit is strict, so speak to a solicitor as soon as you receive the decision.
Can I keep working while I appeal?
If the council's decision was not given immediate effect, you can usually keep working until the appeal period ends or the appeal is decided. If it was given immediate effect on public safety grounds, you cannot work during the appeal. A solicitor can explain your position and whether the appeal can be heard quickly.
What are the risks of appealing?
If the appeal fails, the court can order you to pay the council's legal costs, which can be significant. The court also gives weight to the council's decision and policy. A solicitor will look at the strength of your case and the likely costs before you decide whether to appeal.
Can I apply to a different council instead?
You can apply elsewhere, but you must usually declare previous refusals and revocations, and councils share this information through a national register and safeguarding duties. Failing to disclose them can lead to refusal or revocation. A solicitor can explain how a previous decision may be viewed.
What does a private hire operator licence involve?
An operator licence allows a person or business to accept and arrange private hire bookings. The operator must be fit and proper, keep records of bookings, drivers and vehicles, and use only drivers and vehicles licensed by the same council. Conditions often cover insurance, premises, complaints handling and safeguarding.
Can operators face action because of their drivers?
Yes. If an operator uses unlicensed drivers or vehicles, fails to keep proper records or does not deal with safeguarding concerns, the council can take action against the operator licence. Problems with individual drivers can lead to questions about the operator's systems. A solicitor can help operators review their processes.
Can an operator appeal a council decision?
Yes. Refusals, suspensions and revocations of operator licences can usually be appealed to the magistrates' court within a strict time limit. The process is similar to a driver appeal. A solicitor can confirm the correct route and help with preparation.
Do operators need to check their own staff?
Councils increasingly expect operators to check that staff in roles such as booking and dispatch are suitable, often through basic criminal record checks, and to have a policy on employing people with convictions. Requirements vary between councils and are often set out in operator licence conditions or the council's policy.
How much are taxi licence fees?
Taxi and private hire fees are set locally by each council and vary between areas. On top of the licence fee, applicants usually pay for medicals, criminal record checks, knowledge tests and training. Fees change from time to time, so check your council's website for current amounts.
How much does a solicitor charge for a taxi licence matter?
Many firms offer a fixed fee for help with a council interview or hearing, and a separate fee for an appeal. More complex cases, such as those linked to a criminal investigation, may cost more. Ask the firm for a written quote before you agree to anything.

What should I have ready when the firm calls?
Have your badge number, the name of your council, any letter or email from the council, details of any complaint or police matter and any hearing or appeal deadline. Mention urgent dates on the form so the firm knows to contact you quickly. Give a phone number you can answer during the day.
Do I need a premises licence or a temporary event notice?
A temporary event notice is designed for occasional, small scale events with limits on numbers, length and how often notices can be given. A business that wants to trade regularly from the same place will usually need a premises licence. A licensing solicitor can look at what you plan to do and explain which option fits.
Can I apply before I own or lease the premises?
In many cases a person who carries on or proposes to carry on a business at the premises can apply. In practice many operators apply while a lease is being agreed, sometimes with the lease made conditional on the licence. Your solicitor can talk through the timing alongside any property transaction.
Do I need planning permission first?
Licensing and planning are separate, and an application does not usually have to wait for planning. However, if the planning permission does not allow the use or hours you want, having a licence will not fix that. It is common to deal with both at the same time. Your solicitor can tell you if planning advice is also needed.
What hours should I apply for?
That depends on your business, your location and the council's policy. Some councils have framework hours or stricter policies in certain areas. Asking for longer hours than you need can attract objections, while asking for too little can mean a variation later. A solicitor will explain how hours are usually viewed in your area.
Does a takeaway need a premises licence?
A takeaway that sells hot food or hot drink to the public between 11pm and 5am is usually providing late night refreshment and needs a licence for those hours. There are some exemptions. A takeaway that closes before 11pm and does not sell alcohol may not need one. A solicitor can check how the rules apply to your business.
What is a provisional statement?
It is an application made where premises are being built, extended or changed. If granted, it gives a strong indication that a licence will follow once the work is finished in line with the plans. It can be useful for investors and lenders. A solicitor can explain whether it is worth applying for one in your case.
Is there a right to work check?
Yes. If an individual applies for a premises licence, the council must check that they have the right to work in the UK. Copies of documents usually go in with the application. Companies are not checked in the same way. Your solicitor will tell you what documents are needed.
What is an operating schedule?
It is the part of the application that sets out what you will do, when you will do it and the steps you will take to promote the four licensing objectives. Those steps usually become conditions on your licence. A clear and realistic operating schedule makes it easier for the authorities to support the application and for you to comply later.
