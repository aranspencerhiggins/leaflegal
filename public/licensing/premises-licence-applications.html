Premises licence in brief

A premises licence lets a venue sell alcohol, provide regulated entertainment or serve late night refreshment. A premises licence application needs an operating schedule, plans and notices, and responsible authorities and residents can object.

A licensing solicitor prepares the alcohol licence application, deals with objections and represents you at a hearing. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated licensing firm.

Premises licence applications explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A premises licence is the permission a particular building or site needs to sell alcohol, put on regulated entertainment or serve hot food and drink late at night. It is granted by the local council under the Licensing Act 2003 and it attaches to the premises, although it is held by a named person or company. Once granted, it usually lasts indefinitely unless it is surrendered, revoked or lapses, or the council grants it for a limited period at the applicant's request.

People usually apply for a new licence when they are opening a new bar, restaurant, shop, hotel, venue or takeaway, or when they are taking over a building that has never been licensed or whose old licence has been lost. Some apply when a business has changed so much that a variation of the old licence no longer makes sense.

An application is more than a form. It sets out exactly what the business will do, when it will do it and how it will keep customers and neighbours safe. Whatever goes into the application can become a condition of the licence. That is why the wording matters so much. A sensible condition today can become an expensive problem in five years if the business changes.

It also helps to think beyond opening day. Many operators start with a quiet trading model and later want to add live music, a late night offer or a delivery service. A licence that allows some flexibility from the start can save a variation application later, as long as what is asked for is realistic and the steps to manage it are sensible.

Who can apply

Applicants usually include individuals aged 18 or over who carry on a business at the premises, limited companies, partnerships, some charities and certain public bodies. An individual applicant must also have the right to work in the UK, and the council will check this. Your solicitor will confirm who should be named as the applicant, which can matter later if the business is sold or restructured.

When the building is not ready yet

If premises are being built, extended or substantially changed, it is sometimes possible to apply for a provisional statement first. This gives an early indication of whether a licence is likely to be granted once the work is finished, which can help with funding and planning. A solicitor can explain whether a provisional statement or a full application suits your project.

A new premises licence application is made to the council for the area where the premises is. It usually includes several documents, and each one has to meet the requirements in the regulations. Small mistakes can mean the application is not valid and has to start again.

The application form and fee

The form gives details of the applicant, the premises and the activities applied for. The fee depends on the rateable value band of the premises, and some larger venues pay extra. The current fee is set out by the council or on GOV.UK.

The operating schedule

The operating schedule is the heart of the application. It sets out the licensable activities, the days and times for each one, the opening hours, whether alcohol is for consumption on or off the premises, the proposed designated premises supervisor and the steps the business will take to promote each of the four licensing objectives. Those steps are usually turned into conditions on the licence, so they need to be realistic and clearly worded.

Good conditions are specific, measurable and within the operator's control. For example, a condition requiring CCTV should say where cameras cover, how long recordings are kept and who can produce them. A vague condition, such as keeping noise to a minimum, can be hard to comply with and hard to defend if an officer later says it has been breached. Your solicitor will help turn your plans into wording that works.

The plan

A plan of the premises must be sent with the application. It must show things such as exits, escape routes, fire safety equipment, the bar area, any stage or raised area, toilets and the boundary of the licensed area. It must use the standard symbols or include a key. Many operators use a specialist plan drawer, and an outdoor drinking area or a beer garden needs careful thought about whether it is inside or outside the licensed area.

DPS consent

If the application includes the sale of alcohol, it must name a designated premises supervisor who holds a personal licence. That person signs a consent form, which goes in with the application.

Copies to the responsible authorities

Copies of the application must go to each responsible authority, such as the police, fire service, environmental health and planning, unless the council accepts electronic applications and sends them on itself. The council will tell you its own arrangements.

Many councils now accept applications online, either through their own system or through GOV.UK. The same rules about plans, notices and copies still apply, and the date the application is treated as made can affect the notice period, so it is worth confirming the arrangements first.

A new application has to be advertised so that local people and businesses know about it. If the advertising is not done properly, the application can be invalid even if everything else is correct.

The blue notice at the premises

A notice on pale blue paper, of a set size and print, must be displayed prominently at or on the premises where it can easily be read from outside. It has to stay up for a continuous period of 28 days starting the day after the application is given to the council. Larger premises may need more than one notice. It helps to take dated photographs showing the notice in place throughout.

The newspaper notice

A notice must also be published in a local newspaper, or a local newsletter or circular if there is no local paper, within a short period after the application goes in. The wording follows a set format and must include the key details of the application and where it can be viewed.

What happens if a notice goes wrong

If a notice falls down, is covered up or appears late, the council may say the application has not been properly advertised. In some cases this can be put right by displaying the notice again for the full period, which pushes back the timetable. Your solicitor can check the notices before they go up and help if something goes wrong.

The council also publishes details of applications on its website, and many residents now find out about applications that way. Some councils send letters to neighbours too.

Once the application is properly made, there is a 28 day period during which the responsible authorities and other people can make representations. This is the stage that often decides whether the licence is granted easily or goes to a hearing.

Talking to the authorities early

Many licensing solicitors contact the police licensing team and environmental health before the application goes in. They share a draft operating schedule and ask whether there are any concerns. This can avoid formal objections later and lets the business agree conditions it can actually live with. Some councils offer a paid pre-application advice service.

Representations from residents

It is also worth keeping a record of every contact with the authorities and objectors during this period. If a hearing does take place, being able to show that you listened and offered reasonable changes can help the committee understand your approach.

Anyone can make a representation, as long as it relates to one of the licensing objectives and is not frivolous or vexatious. Residents often raise noise, people gathering outside, smoking, litter, parking or late night disturbance. Some representations support the application. Your solicitor may contact objectors, or invite them to meet, to see whether their concerns can be dealt with.

Agreeing conditions

Often the police or environmental health will say they are content if certain extra conditions are added, such as CCTV, door supervisors at weekends, a dispersal policy or earlier closing for an outside area. If all parties agree, the council can usually grant the licence with those conditions without a hearing. Your solicitor will check each proposed condition is clear, workable and proportionate.

If no representations are made

If no relevant representations are made, or they are all withdrawn, the council must grant the licence in the terms applied for, with the mandatory conditions and conditions consistent with the operating schedule.

If relevant representations remain at the end of the consultation period, the council must hold a hearing, unless everyone agrees one is not needed. The hearing usually takes place within 20 working days of the end of the consultation, and the parties are given notice of the date.

What happens at the hearing

The hearing is before a licensing sub-committee, usually three councillors, with a legal adviser present. The council's licensing officer introduces the report. The applicant, the responsible authorities and the people who made representations then have a chance to speak and to ask questions through the chair. The committee then retires to make its decision.

Preparing for a hearing

Preparation is usually the key to a good hearing. That can include a clear written submission, photographs, a dispersal plan, a noise management plan, evidence of the operator's track record and sometimes an expert report. The committee can only consider matters relevant to the licensing objectives, and your solicitor will focus on those.

It is common for the applicant or the operator to attend the hearing as well as the solicitor. The committee may want to hear from the person who will actually run the premises, and to ask them about their experience and how they will handle problems. Your solicitor will help you prepare for the kind of questions you may be asked.

Possible outcomes

  • Grant the licence as applied for
  • Grant it with changes to the conditions
  • Exclude some licensable activities or reduce the hours
  • Refuse to accept the proposed designated premises supervisor
  • Refuse the application

The council must give reasons for its decision. The applicant, and anyone who made a relevant representation, can usually appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision.

After the grant

Once the licence is issued, the holder must keep the licence or a certified copy at the premises and display a summary. The annual fee has to be paid on time, as non-payment can lead to the licence being suspended. Your solicitor can explain the ongoing duties.

If the licence is granted but an appeal is lodged by an objector, the decision usually takes effect in the meantime. If the licence is refused and you appeal, you cannot trade under the refused licence while the appeal is waiting to be heard.

The cost of a new premises licence has several parts. Some are fixed and some depend on how the application goes.

Council fees

The application fee and the annual fee are set nationally and depend mainly on the rateable value of the premises. Large venues may pay an extra amount based on capacity. These figures change from time to time, so check them with the council or on GOV.UK.

Solicitor fees

Many firms offer a fixed fee for preparing and submitting an application, including drafting the operating schedule, the notices and talks with the authorities. Attending a hearing is often charged separately because not every application needs one. Ask for a written quote and check what is included.

Other costs

If a project is large or contentious, ask the firm for an estimate of the total cost in stages, from early talks with the authorities through to a possible hearing. That makes it easier to budget for the best and worst case.

Premises licence applications questions and answers

How long does a new licence take?
There is a consultation period after the application, and longer if there is a hearing. Allow plenty of time before opening.
What happens if someone objects?
The council holds a hearing. A solicitor can negotiate conditions beforehand and represent you.
Do I need a personal licence too?
Premises selling alcohol need a designated premises supervisor with a personal licence.
Which areas do you cover?
Licensing matters in England and Wales.
Do you help individuals as well as businesses?
Yes, for example personal licence holders and taxi or private hire drivers.
Does it cost anything?
Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Do I need a premises licence or a temporary event notice?
A temporary event notice is designed for occasional, small scale events with limits on numbers, length and how often notices can be given. A business that wants to trade regularly from the same place will usually need a premises licence. A licensing solicitor can look at what you plan to do and explain which option fits.
Can I apply before I own or lease the premises?
In many cases a person who carries on or proposes to carry on a business at the premises can apply. In practice many operators apply while a lease is being agreed, sometimes with the lease made conditional on the licence. Your solicitor can talk through the timing alongside any property transaction.
Do I need planning permission first?
Licensing and planning are separate, and an application does not usually have to wait for planning. However, if the planning permission does not allow the use or hours you want, having a licence will not fix that. It is common to deal with both at the same time. Your solicitor can tell you if planning advice is also needed.
What hours should I apply for?
That depends on your business, your location and the council's policy. Some councils have framework hours or stricter policies in certain areas. Asking for longer hours than you need can attract objections, while asking for too little can mean a variation later. A solicitor will explain how hours are usually viewed in your area.
Does a takeaway need a premises licence?
A takeaway that sells hot food or hot drink to the public between 11pm and 5am is usually providing late night refreshment and needs a licence for those hours. There are some exemptions. A takeaway that closes before 11pm and does not sell alcohol may not need one. A solicitor can check how the rules apply to your business.
What is a provisional statement?
It is an application made where premises are being built, extended or changed. If granted, it gives a strong indication that a licence will follow once the work is finished in line with the plans. It can be useful for investors and lenders. A solicitor can explain whether it is worth applying for one in your case.
Is there a right to work check?
Yes. If an individual applies for a premises licence, the council must check that they have the right to work in the UK. Copies of documents usually go in with the application. Companies are not checked in the same way. Your solicitor will tell you what documents are needed.
What is an operating schedule?
It is the part of the application that sets out what you will do, when you will do it and the steps you will take to promote the four licensing objectives. Those steps usually become conditions on your licence. A clear and realistic operating schedule makes it easier for the authorities to support the application and for you to comply later.
What must the plan show?
The plan must show the boundaries of the premises, exits and escape routes, fire safety equipment, any stage or raised area, the bar area, toilets and the areas used for licensable activities, using standard symbols or a key. Councils can reject applications with poor plans. Many operators use a specialist plan drawer to avoid problems.
Can I include an outside area?
Yes, outside areas such as beer gardens and pavement seating can be included on the plan. Whether to include them inside the licensed area affects where alcohol can be sold and consumed. Pavement seating on the public highway usually needs separate permission from the council. A solicitor can explain how to deal with outdoor space.
Who should be the DPS?
The designated premises supervisor must hold a personal licence and is usually the person in day to day charge of the premises. The police will treat them as the main contact. They need to be suitable, available and willing to take on the role. Your solicitor can explain what the role involves before you choose someone.
How long must the blue notice be displayed?
The notice must be displayed for a continuous period of 28 days, starting the day after the application is given to the council. It must be prominently placed where it can easily be read from outside. If it is missing for part of that time, the council may require the notice period to start again.
Do I have to advertise in a newspaper?
Yes, for a new premises licence a notice must be published in a local newspaper, or a local newsletter or circular if there is no local paper, within a short time after the application goes in. The wording follows a set format. Your solicitor will usually draft and arrange it for you.
Can I change the application after it goes in?
Minor amendments, especially agreeing extra conditions with the authorities, are often possible during the consultation. Bigger changes, such as adding activities or extending hours, may need a new application and new notices. Your solicitor will explain what can be changed without starting again.
Who can object to my application?
The responsible authorities, such as the police, environmental health, fire and planning, and any other person can make representations. They must relate to the licensing objectives and not be frivolous or vexatious. Business competitors cannot object simply to protect their trade, although they can raise genuine licensing concerns.
Will I have to go to a hearing?
Only if relevant representations are made and not withdrawn, unless everyone agrees a hearing is unnecessary. Many applications are resolved by agreeing conditions with the police or environmental health during the consultation. If a hearing is needed, your solicitor can prepare your case and speak for you.
What can I do to reduce objections?
Speaking to the authorities early, being clear about how the premises will be run and offering sensible conditions often helps. Some operators also meet neighbours to explain their plans. A solicitor can identify likely concerns from the council's policy and the area, and suggest ways to deal with them.
Can the council refuse my application?
Yes, if it decides that refusal is appropriate to promote the licensing objectives. In practice, councils often grant licences with extra conditions or shorter hours rather than refusing outright. The outcome depends on the evidence, local policy and how the application is presented. Nobody can guarantee a particular result.
Can I appeal if my application is refused?
Yes. An applicant can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. Anyone who made a relevant representation can also appeal if they are unhappy with a grant. Appeals take time and carry a costs risk, so your solicitor will explain the options before you decide.
How long does a new premises licence take?
There is a 28 day consultation period after a valid application is made. If there are no representations, the licence is usually issued soon after. If there is a hearing, it is normally held within 20 working days of the end of the consultation, with the decision following. Allow extra time for preparing the documents before you apply.
Can I open before the licence is granted?
You should not carry on licensable activities until the licence is in force. Some operators use temporary event notices for a launch, within the limits that apply, but these can be objected to. Your solicitor can explain what is possible if your opening date is close.
How much is the council fee?
The fee depends on the rateable value band of the premises, and there is an annual fee once the licence is granted. Very large venues may pay more. Fees change from time to time, so check the current figures with the council or on GOV.UK.
How much will a solicitor charge?
Many firms quote a fixed fee for preparing and submitting the application, with a separate fee for attending a hearing if one is needed. More complex applications, such as late venues in busy areas, may cost more. Ask for a written quote and check whether plans and advertising are included.
Does Leaflegal charge for the introduction?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The licensing firm we introduce you to pays us a fee, and that does not change what you pay them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.
How long does a premises licence last?
Most premises licences last indefinitely, as long as the annual fee is paid and the licence is not surrendered, revoked or lapsed. A licence can be granted for a limited period if that is what was applied for, such as for a seasonal event. Your solicitor can explain anything specific to your licence.
What are the mandatory conditions?
All licences that permit alcohol sales carry certain mandatory conditions. These include having a DPS with a personal licence, every sale being made or authorised by a personal licence holder, an age verification policy, offering free tap water, making small measures available and rules on irresponsible promotions and minimum pricing. Your solicitor can explain each one.
What if I do not pay the annual fee?
If the annual fee is not paid when due, the council must usually suspend the licence, which means you cannot carry on licensable activities. There are limited exceptions where the failure is due to an administrative error or a dispute. Keeping a note of the due date avoids a lot of trouble.
What if the business changes later?
If you want different hours, a new layout or different activities later, you will usually need to vary the licence. If someone else takes over the business, the licence will need to be transferred. A solicitor can help with these changes when the time comes.
Who can inspect my premises?
Police officers and authorised officers from the council and other responsible authorities have powers to enter licensed premises to check that licensable activities are being carried on in line with the licence. They may ask to see the licence, the summary, staff training records or CCTV. Your solicitor can explain what officers usually look for and how to prepare.
Can a neighbour complain after the licence is granted?
Yes. If problems arise later, a resident or responsible authority can apply for a review of the licence. Many complaints are dealt with informally first, through the council's licensing or environmental health team. Running the premises in line with the conditions and dealing promptly with any concerns usually reduces the risk of a review.
What is the difference between a minor and a full variation?
A minor variation is a quicker process for small changes that could not adversely affect the licensing objectives. It has a shorter notice period, no hearing and a lower fee. A full variation is needed for bigger changes such as later alcohol hours. It involves a 28 day consultation, newspaper and blue notices and the possibility of a hearing.
Can I extend my alcohol hours with a minor variation?
No. A minor variation cannot be used to extend the hours during which alcohol is sold or supplied, or to change alcohol sales between 11pm and 7am. Those changes need a full variation. A minor variation can, however, be used to reduce hours if you no longer need them.
Can I add alcohol sales to my licence?
If your licence does not currently permit alcohol, adding it needs a full variation, not a minor one. You will also need a designated premises supervisor who holds a personal licence. A solicitor can explain whether a full variation or a new application is more suitable.
When do I need a new licence instead of a variation?
A variation cannot be used to vary the premises substantially. If a building is being completely rebuilt, extended into a different property or changed so much that it is effectively new premises, a fresh application may be required. Where the line falls is a matter of judgement, and a solicitor can advise on your project.
Can I change the DPS through a variation?
Changing the designated premises supervisor uses a separate, specific application, not a minor or full variation. It is usually quicker and can take immediate effect. Our page on transfers and DPS changes explains more about it.
Do I need to vary my licence after a refurbishment?
If the refurbishment changes the layout shown on your licence plan, such as moving the bar, changing exits or adding a new area, the plan will need updating. Small changes may be dealt with by a minor variation. Bigger changes may need a full variation. Trading on an out of date plan can cause problems.
How long does a minor variation take?
The council must make a decision within a set number of working days from receiving the application, and the white notice must be displayed for a shorter period than a full variation. In practice a minor variation is usually much quicker than a full variation. If the council does not decide in time, the application is treated as refused.
How long does a full variation take?
There is a 28 day consultation period. If there are no unresolved representations, the variation is usually granted shortly after. If a hearing is needed, it is normally held within 20 working days of the end of the consultation. Allow time beforehand for preparing the application and any plans.
