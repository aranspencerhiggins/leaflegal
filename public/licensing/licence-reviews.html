Licence review in brief

A premises licence review can lead to new conditions, suspension or revocation. Reviews are often brought by the police or council, and preparing early is vital.

A licensing solicitor can negotiate conditions and represent you at the hearing.

Licence reviews and hearings explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A licence review is the process the Licensing Act 2003 uses when there are concerns that a licensed premises is undermining one or more of the four licensing objectives. It gives the police, the council, other responsible authorities and local people a way to ask the licensing authority to look again at a premises licence or club premises certificate. At the end of the process, the council can leave the licence as it is, change it, suspend it or take it away completely.

For most operators, a review is the most serious licensing event they will face. The licence may be central to the value of the business, the jobs of its staff and in many cases the owner's own livelihood. A review also tends to come with a fixed timetable, so there is limited time to prepare.

Reviews do not usually come out of nowhere. Most follow a period of complaints, incidents or contact with the police or environmental health. Some follow a single serious event, such as a violent assault, a drugs seizure, a failed test purchase or the discovery of illegal workers. A small number follow a closure order made by a court.

What a review is not

A review is not a criminal prosecution, and nobody is fined or sent to prison as a result of it. It is a regulatory process focused on the future. The question for the committee is what, if anything, it is appropriate to do now to promote the licensing objectives. Past problems are relevant because they help show what may happen next, and what the business has done since to put things right can matter a great deal.

Acting early

The earlier help is sought, the more options there usually are. An operator who brings in a solicitor when the police first raise concerns may be able to agree an action plan or voluntary conditions that avoid a review altogether. Once a review application has been made, the focus shifts to preparing for the hearing, although agreement is still often possible.

Some events that lead to a review can also lead to separate criminal proceedings, for example for selling alcohol to children or employing illegal workers. A licensing solicitor can explain how the two processes interact and whether you need help with both.

Leaflegal does not give legal advice and cannot predict the outcome of a review. We introduce you to a regulated licensing firm that can look at your situation and explain your options.

An ordinary review follows a timetable set out in regulations. Every step has a purpose, and knowing what comes next can make the process feel less overwhelming.

The application

A review can be applied for by any responsible authority, such as the police, environmental health, trading standards or the licensing authority itself, or by any other person, such as a local resident or business. The grounds must relate to at least one of the licensing objectives. The applicant must send a copy to the premises licence holder and to the responsible authorities.

Filtering out weak applications

For applications from people other than responsible authorities, the council can reject a review if it is frivolous, vexatious or repetitive. A repetitive application is broadly one that is the same or similar to an earlier one, made too soon after it. Most reviews brought by the police or other authorities go ahead.

The consultation

The council displays a notice of the review at or near the premises and publishes it, usually on its website. There is then a 28 day period during which responsible authorities and other persons can make representations. These can support the review, oppose it or suggest particular outcomes. Some reviews attract many representations and some attract very few.

The hearing

The council must hold a hearing, usually within 20 working days of the end of the consultation period. The licence holder receives notice of the hearing and copies of the documents. At the hearing, the licensing sub-committee hears from the applicant, the licence holder and those who made representations, and then makes its decision.

Before the hearing

The period between the application and the hearing is often the most important time in a review. It is when the licence holder can talk to the authorities, gather evidence, make changes to how the premises runs and in some cases agree a set of conditions that everyone can support. Many reviews end with agreed proposals being put to the committee.

It can be tempting to wait and see what is said at the hearing. In practice, a licence holder who engages early, answers concerns in writing and shows changes already made is usually in a stronger position than one who arrives with nothing prepared.

A summary review, sometimes called an expedited review, is a faster process available to the police where a premises licensed to sell alcohol is associated with serious crime, serious disorder or both. It moves very quickly and can lead to steps that take effect almost at once.

How it starts

A senior police officer signs a certificate stating that, in their opinion, the premises is associated with serious crime or serious disorder. The police then apply to the council for a summary review, with the certificate attached.

Interim steps

Within 48 hours of receiving the application, not counting non-working days, the council must consider whether to take interim steps while the full review is pending. These can include changing the conditions, stopping alcohol sales, removing the DPS or suspending the licence. Interim steps can take effect immediately and can be decided without the licence holder being present.

Challenging interim steps

The licence holder can make representations against interim steps. If they do, the council must hold a hearing to consider them within a short time. This is often the first chance to put forward the operator's side and propose a different way of addressing the police concerns.

The full hearing

The council must hold the full review hearing within 28 days of receiving the police application. At that hearing it can take any of the steps available on an ordinary review and must review any interim steps still in place.

Why speed matters

Because the timetable is so tight, the first few days matter. If you receive notice of a summary review, or the police tell you one is being considered, contact a licensing solicitor straight away.

A strong response to a review usually combines three things. It shows a clear understanding of what went wrong, evidence of what has been done since and a realistic plan for the future. Committees are often more reassured by practical changes than by arguments alone.

Understanding the case

The first step is to read the review application and any representations carefully. What exactly is said to have happened? Which objectives are said to be undermined? What does the applicant want the committee to do? Your solicitor may ask the authorities for more information, such as police incident reports or CCTV.

Gathering evidence

  • CCTV footage and incident logs for the relevant dates
  • Staff training records, including age verification and conflict management
  • Refusals registers and test purchase results
  • Door staff records and security procedures
  • Noise monitoring results or acoustic reports
  • Letters of support from customers or neighbours
  • Evidence of right to work checks for all staff

Making changes

Changes made before the hearing can carry real weight. Examples include replacing the DPS, bringing in a new management team, improving CCTV, employing more door staff, reducing hours voluntarily, introducing a dispersal policy or banning problem customers. The aim is to show the committee that the concerns have been taken seriously.

Proposing conditions

Your solicitor may draft a set of conditions to offer the committee. Good conditions address the specific problems raised and are clear enough to be enforced. Offering conditions does not mean admitting every allegation, but it shows a willingness to work with the authorities.

Talking to the authorities

Many reviews are narrowed or resolved through discussion. The police or environmental health may be prepared to support a package of conditions or management changes rather than seek revocation. Your solicitor can lead these talks and make sure anything agreed is put to the committee clearly.

Staying calm with neighbours and authorities

Reviews can feel personal, especially when they are brought by neighbours. Angry emails, confrontations at the door or public posts about objectors rarely help and may be used as evidence. Your solicitor can handle contact on your behalf, and can arrange meetings if there is a realistic chance of resolving concerns.

At the end of a review, the committee must decide what steps, if any, are appropriate to promote the licensing objectives. It must give reasons for its decision.

What the committee can do

  • Take no formal action
  • Issue an informal warning or recommendation
  • Modify the conditions of the licence
  • Exclude a licensable activity, such as live music
  • Remove the designated premises supervisor
  • Suspend the licence for up to three months
  • Revoke the licence

Changes to conditions and exclusions of activities can be permanent or for a period of up to three months. The national guidance asks committees to treat certain criminal activity particularly seriously, such as drug dealing, the sale of smuggled goods, knowingly employing illegal workers and repeated underage sales. In those cases, revocation may be considered even on a first occasion.

When the decision takes effect

On an ordinary review, the decision does not usually take effect until the time for appealing has passed or any appeal has been decided. This means the premises can often continue to trade under the existing licence while an appeal is pending. Interim steps from a summary review follow different rules.

Appeals

Some appeals are settled before the court hearing. The licence holder and the council, sometimes with the police, may agree revised conditions that the court is asked to approve. This can save time and cost for everyone.

The licence holder, the review applicant and anyone who made relevant representations can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. The court hears the case afresh but gives weight to the council's decision, and the appellant usually needs to show that the decision was wrong. The court can dismiss the appeal, substitute a different decision or send the matter back to the council.

Costs risk

Councils often ask for their costs if an appeal fails, and the court can order them. Your solicitor will explain the merits and likely costs before you decide whether to appeal, and whether there are other options, such as negotiating with the authorities or applying for a variation later.

After the review

Whatever the outcome, the premises is likely to be watched closely for a while. Sticking to the conditions, keeping good records and maintaining contact with the authorities all help to reduce the risk of further action.

There is no council fee to respond to a review. The main cost is professional help, and that depends on how complex and contested the review is.

Solicitor fees

Some firms quote a fixed fee for the whole review, from the application through to the hearing. Others charge in stages, such as an initial review of the papers, negotiations with the authorities, preparation of evidence and attendance at the hearing. Summary reviews may involve extra hearings and so cost more. Appeals are usually priced separately.

Other costs

It is also worth weighing the cost of help against what is at stake. For many businesses the licence is the core of their value, and a well prepared response may protect far more than it costs. That is a decision for you, and the firm can help you think it through.

  • Experts such as acoustic consultants or security advisers
  • Barristers for complex hearings or appeals
  • Improvements such as CCTV, extra door staff or training
  • The other side's costs if an appeal fails

It is worth asking the firm for a realistic estimate at each stage and how they will keep you updated if things change. A good firm will explain where the money is likely to make a difference and where it may not.

Licence reviews and hearings questions and answers

Can my licence be revoked?
Yes. That is why expert representation is important.
Can I keep trading during a review?
Usually, unless there has been a summary review with interim steps.
Can I appeal?
Yes, to the magistrates’ court, within a time limit.
Which areas do you cover?
Licensing matters in England and Wales.
Do you help individuals as well as businesses?
Yes, for example personal licence holders and taxi or private hire drivers.
Does it cost anything?
Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Who can apply for a review of my licence?
Any responsible authority, such as the police, environmental health, trading standards, the fire service or the licensing authority, can apply. So can any other person, including a local resident or business. The application must relate to at least one of the licensing objectives. The council can reject applications from other persons that are frivolous, vexatious or repetitive.
What kind of problems lead to reviews?
Common reasons include crime and disorder linked to the premises, noise and nuisance affecting neighbours, sales of alcohol to children, breaches of licence conditions, public safety failings, drug use or dealing and the employment of illegal workers. Many reviews follow a build up of complaints, while some follow a single serious incident.
Will I get warning before a review?
Often there is some warning, such as letters, meetings or action plans from the police or council. But there is no legal requirement for a warning before an application is made, and some reviews follow a single incident. If you are receiving complaints or warnings, a licensing solicitor can help you respond before matters escalate.
Can a resident apply for a review on their own?
Yes. Any person can apply, as long as the grounds relate to the licensing objectives. The council will consider whether the application is frivolous, vexatious or repetitive before accepting it. Reviews brought by residents often concern noise and nuisance, and the outcome depends on the evidence like any other review.
What is a review after a closure order?
If a magistrates' court makes certain closure orders in respect of licensed premises, the council is required to review the licence. This happens automatically and follows its own timetable. Because a closure order means there has already been serious concern, these reviews need careful preparation.
Can I stop a review from going ahead?
Once the council accepts an application, it cannot usually be stopped by the licence holder. The applicant can withdraw it, which sometimes happens if concerns are resolved. More often, the outcome is shaped by agreeing proposals with the applicant and authorities, which can then be presented to the committee at the hearing.
How long does a review take?
There is a 28 day period for representations after the application is advertised. The hearing is then normally held within 20 working days of the end of that period. Summary reviews brought by the police are much quicker, with interim steps considered within 48 hours and a full hearing within 28 days.
Can I keep trading during a review?
Usually yes. On an ordinary review, the licence continues as normal until the decision and, in most cases, until any appeal period has passed. In a summary review, the council can impose interim steps, such as suspension, that take effect immediately. A solicitor can explain the position in your case.
What documents will I receive?
You should receive a copy of the review application from the applicant. Before the hearing, the council usually sends a report with the application, the representations and any supporting evidence. If you think documents are missing, your solicitor can ask the council or the applicant for them.
Who sits on the licensing sub-committee?
A sub-committee is usually made up of three councillors from the council's licensing committee, supported by a licensing officer and a legal adviser. The councillors make the decision. They are expected to keep an open mind and decide on the evidence and the licensing objectives.
Can new evidence be produced at the hearing?
Documentary evidence usually needs to be provided before the hearing day. New material on the day can be allowed only if all parties agree. This is one reason why careful preparation in advance is so important. Your solicitor will plan when and how to send in your evidence.
Do I have to attend the hearing myself?
You do not have to, but it often helps if the licence holder, the DPS or the person running the premises attends. The committee may want to ask them how the premises will be run in future. If you have a solicitor, they will usually speak for you and prepare you for any questions.
Can I agree an outcome with the police before the hearing?
Often yes. Many reviews are narrowed or settled by agreeing conditions, management changes or a reduction in hours with the police and other authorities. The agreed proposals are then put to the committee, which still makes the final decision but usually gives weight to agreement. Your solicitor can lead these discussions.
What is a summary review?
It is a fast track review that the police can apply for where a premises licensed to sell alcohol is associated with serious crime or serious disorder. A senior officer signs a certificate, and the council must consider interim steps within 48 hours. It is designed for urgent situations where the police believe immediate action is needed.
What interim steps can the council take?
The council can change the conditions, exclude the sale of alcohol, remove the DPS or suspend the licence while the full review is pending. These steps can take effect immediately. The council can make this decision without hearing from the licence holder first.
Can I challenge interim steps?
Yes. The licence holder can make representations against the interim steps, and the council must then hold a hearing within a short time to consider them. This can be an opportunity to propose alternative measures that address the police concerns while allowing some trading to continue.
What happens at the full summary review hearing?
The full hearing must take place within 28 days of the council receiving the police application. The committee can take any of the steps available on an ordinary review and must also decide what to do about the interim steps. Thorough preparation in a short period is essential.
Is a closure order the same as a summary review?
No. Closure notices and orders are powers under separate anti-social behaviour laws, used by the police or council to close premises quickly, often with a court hearing within days. A summary review is a licensing process before the council. They can happen together, and a closure order can itself trigger a review. Both need urgent help.
Can my licence be revoked?
Yes. Revocation is the most serious outcome and is available on any review. Committees must decide what is appropriate to promote the licensing objectives, and many reviews end with conditions or management changes rather than revocation. National guidance treats some criminal activity, such as drug dealing or knowingly employing illegal workers, particularly seriously.
How long can a licence be suspended?
A committee can suspend a licence for up to three months. During a suspension, the premises cannot carry on the licensable activities covered by the suspension. Suspension is sometimes used as a deterrent or to give the operator time to put changes in place.
Can the committee remove my DPS?
Yes. Removing the DPS is one of the options available on a review, often where the committee has concerns about how the premises has been managed. The licence holder would then need to apply to name a new DPS before alcohol sales can resume.
Can I appeal the decision?
Yes. The licence holder can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. The review applicant and those who made relevant representations can appeal too. On an ordinary review, the decision usually does not take effect while an appeal is pending. A solicitor will explain the merits and costs risks.
What does the court look at on appeal?
The magistrates' court rehears the case and can consider evidence that was not before the committee, including events since the hearing. It gives weight to the council's decision, and the appellant usually has to persuade the court that the decision was wrong. The court can confirm, change or overturn the decision.
Will a review affect the sale of my business?
It can. A buyer will want to know about any review, its outcome and any conditions added. A revoked licence would usually need a new application. Even a review that ends with no action may be raised in due diligence. A solicitor can explain how the review is likely to be seen by buyers or landlords.
Will conditions added on review be permanent?
Conditions added on a review are usually permanent unless the committee says they apply only for a set period, which can be up to three months. Permanent conditions can later be changed through a variation, but councils will want to know why they are no longer needed. Your solicitor can help make sure any conditions offered are workable.
How much does help with a review cost?
It depends on how complex and contested the review is. Some firms offer a fixed fee for the whole review, while others charge in stages or by the hour. Extra hearings in a summary review, experts and appeals all add to the cost. Ask the firm for a written estimate and how they will update you.
Can I be ordered to pay costs?
Costs are not usually awarded at a council review hearing. On an appeal to the magistrates' court, the court can order the losing side to pay costs, and councils often ask for theirs if an appeal fails. Your solicitor will explain this risk before you decide to appeal.

How quickly can a firm help?
The firm usually contacts you within one working day, and often sooner. Reviews have fixed timetables, so it helps to get in touch as soon as you receive the application. If you have a hearing date or interim steps in place, say so on the form so the firm knows it is urgent.
What should I have ready when the firm calls?
Have the review application, your current licence with its plan and conditions, any hearing date and any letters from the police or council. It also helps to know what has happened at the premises, what changes you have already made and what you would be prepared to change. The firm will ask for evidence later.
Do I need a personal licence to work behind a bar?
No. Staff do not each need a personal licence to serve alcohol. Every sale must be made or authorised by a personal licence holder, so a holder, often the manager, authorises other staff to sell. You would usually need a personal licence to be named as the designated premises supervisor.
Which council do I apply to?
You apply to the council for the area where you are ordinarily resident, which is usually where you live. If you live outside England and Wales, you can apply to any licensing authority in England and Wales. The licence then stays with that council even if you move later.
What qualification do I need?
You need an accredited licensing qualification, usually a short course covering licensing law and the role of a personal licence holder, with an exam at the end. Approved training providers run these courses. Some other qualifications are also accepted. Check with the training provider that the course is accredited for personal licence applications.
How long does a personal licence take?
If there is nothing to declare and the application is complete, the council will usually process it within a few weeks, although times vary between councils. If you declare a relevant conviction, the police have time to consider objecting, and a hearing may follow, which adds more time.
Does a personal licence expire?
No. Personal licences no longer need renewing and last indefinitely unless they are surrendered, forfeited, suspended or revoked. If you have an older licence with an expiry date printed on it, that date no longer applies. Your licence remains valid.
Can I use my licence anywhere?
Yes. A personal licence granted by any council in England and Wales can be used at licensed premises anywhere in England and Wales. It is not tied to a particular premises or employer, so you keep it if you change jobs.
Do I need the right to work in the UK?
Yes. Applicants must have the right to work in the UK, and the council will check this. A personal licence can lapse if the holder's right to work ends. If your immigration status is time limited, the licence may be linked to that. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Can I get a personal licence with a criminal record?
Often yes. Many people with past convictions hold personal licences. Spent convictions are generally not relevant. If you have an unspent relevant conviction, the police can object and the council may hold a hearing. The outcome depends on the offence, how long ago it was and your circumstances since.

More questions people ask

What is a relevant offence?
Relevant offences are listed in the Licensing Act 2003. They include certain offences involving alcohol, drink driving, violence, dishonesty, drugs, some sexual offences, certain immigration offences and offences under licensing law. Equivalent offences abroad can also count. A solicitor can check whether a particular conviction is relevant.
Do I have to declare a drink driving conviction?
Drink driving offences are relevant offences, so an unspent drink driving conviction must usually be declared on an application. Whether the conviction is spent depends on the sentence and the date. If it is spent, it generally does not need to be declared. A solicitor can check your position.
What if I do not declare a conviction?
Knowingly or recklessly making a false statement on an application is an offence. It can also lead to the licence being challenged later and damage your credibility. If you are unsure whether something needs to be declared, it is better to check with a solicitor before you apply.
Will the police always object?
No. The police consider each declared conviction and decide whether granting the licence would undermine the crime prevention objective. Many applications with older or minor convictions go through without objection. Where they do object, a hearing allows you to explain your circumstances.
What happens at a hearing about my application?
A licensing sub-committee hears from the police and from you or your representative. It considers the offence, its circumstances, your record since and your plans. You can provide references and evidence of your work. The committee then decides whether to grant or refuse, and must give reasons.
Can I appeal if my application is refused?
Yes. You can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. The court looks at the matter again. Appeals carry a risk of paying the council's costs if unsuccessful, so a solicitor will explain the merits before you decide.
Do motoring offences other than drink driving count?
Most ordinary motoring offences, such as speeding, are not relevant offences for a personal licence. Some driving offences involving alcohol or drugs are relevant. The list in the Licensing Act 2003 is specific, so a solicitor can check whether any particular offence counts before you apply or report it.
