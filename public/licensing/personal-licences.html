A solicitor can help with applications where there are convictions and represent you if the council considers action against your licence.

Personal licence in brief

A personal licence lets you authorise the sale of alcohol under a premises licence. Most applications are simple, but a relevant offence, such as drink driving, can lead to objections or a review.

A licensing solicitor can advise personal licence holders facing a review or objection.

Personal licences explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A personal licence is a licence held by an individual, not by a business or a building. It allows that person to authorise the sale of alcohol from premises that hold a premises licence. It is granted by the council for the area where the person lives under the Licensing Act 2003, and once granted it can be used anywhere in England and Wales.

Under the mandatory conditions that apply to every premises licence that permits alcohol, every sale must be made or authorised by a personal licence holder, and the premises must have a designated premises supervisor who holds one. That is why bar managers, shop managers, publicans, area managers and many business owners hold personal licences. Plenty of staff without a personal licence still serve alcohol, under the authority of a personal licence holder.

For most people, getting a personal licence is a routine step. They take an accredited course, apply to the council and receive the licence. Legal help is more often needed where the applicant has a criminal conviction, where the police object to an application or where a holder has been convicted of an offence and the council or a court is considering action against the licence.

Why the licence matters to the holder

For a pub manager or shop owner, a personal licence can be essential to their job. Losing it, even for a few months, can mean they cannot act as DPS, which may cost them their position. For an operator, a DPS losing their licence can stop alcohol sales at a site until someone else is named.

A personal licence also brings duties. The holder must keep certain details up to date, tell courts and the council about certain matters and produce the licence when asked. Many holders are not aware of these duties until something goes wrong.

Employers and personal licences

Employers have a strong interest in their managers' personal licences. Many ask staff to tell them straight away if they are charged with an offence, and some pay for legal help to protect a manager's licence because replacing a DPS at short notice can be disruptive. If you are an employer worried about a member of staff, a licensing solicitor can explain how the process works and what it might mean for your premises.

An application is made to the council for the area where the applicant is ordinarily resident. If you live outside England and Wales, you can apply to any licensing authority in England and Wales.

Who can apply

  • People aged 18 or over
  • People who hold an accredited licensing qualification
  • People who have not had a personal licence forfeited in the previous five years
  • People who have the right to work in the UK

The qualification

Applicants must hold an accredited qualification, usually a short course covering licensing law, the responsibilities of personal licence holders and the social impact of alcohol. Courses are run by approved training providers, often in a single day, with an exam at the end. Certain other qualifications are also accepted.

What goes in with the application

The application usually includes the form, the qualification certificate, passport style photographs, a basic criminal record certificate, a declaration about relevant convictions, foreign offences and immigration penalties, evidence of the right to work and the fee. The basic criminal record certificate must be recent, and councils will tell you how recent.

Where there is nothing to report

If the applicant meets the requirements and has no relevant unspent convictions, foreign offences or immigration penalties to disclose, the council must grant the licence. The licence is then posted to the holder, usually with a photocard and a paper part.

Licences no longer expire

Personal licences used to need renewing every ten years. That requirement was removed, and personal licences now last indefinitely unless they are surrendered, forfeited, suspended or revoked. Some holders still have older licences showing an expiry date. Those licences have not lapsed simply because of that date.

Councils sometimes return incomplete applications, for example where the photographs are not endorsed correctly, the criminal record certificate is too old or the right to work evidence is missing. Checking everything against the council's own checklist before sending it in saves time.

Many people with a criminal record go on to hold personal licences. A conviction does not automatically prevent someone from getting one, but certain convictions trigger a closer look at the application.

Relevant offences

The Licensing Act 2003 lists the offences that count as relevant offences. They include certain offences involving alcohol, drink driving, violence, dishonesty, drugs, some sexual offences, certain immigration offences and offences under licensing law itself. Equivalent offences committed abroad, called foreign offences, can also count. Some civil penalties for immigration matters are relevant as well.

Spent convictions

Convictions that are spent under the Rehabilitation of Offenders Act 1974 are generally not relevant to a personal licence application and do not need to be declared. Whether a particular conviction is spent depends on the sentence and when it was imposed, and the rules have changed over time. A solicitor can check whether a conviction is spent.

What happens when there is a relevant conviction

If an applicant declares an unspent relevant offence or foreign offence, the council must tell the police. If the matter involves immigration, it tells Home Office Immigration Enforcement. They then have a short period, usually 14 days, to object if they believe granting the licence would undermine the crime prevention objective, or for immigration enforcement the prevention of illegal working. If no objection is made, the council must grant the licence.

If there is an objection

If the police or Immigration Enforcement object, the council must hold a hearing, unless everyone agrees one is not needed. The committee then decides whether to grant or refuse. It will usually look at the nature of the offence, how long ago it was, the sentence, the person's record since and their reasons for wanting the licence.

Being honest

Failing to declare a relevant conviction on an application is itself an offence. It also tends to damage the applicant's credibility at any hearing. If you are unsure whether something needs to be declared, a solicitor can look at your record before you apply.

Preparation for a hearing about a conviction usually involves gathering references, evidence of employment and training, and a clear account of what happened and what has changed since. The committee will want to understand why the applicant can be trusted to authorise alcohol sales responsibly.

Holding a personal licence brings legal duties. Some of them apply only in particular situations, but failing to meet them can be an offence and can count against the holder later.

Keeping your details up to date

A holder must tell the council that issued the licence about any change of name or address as soon as reasonably practicable. The licence stays with the council that issued it, even if the holder moves to a different area. There is usually a small fee for updating the licence.

Telling a court you hold a licence

If a holder is charged with a relevant offence, they must tell the court that they hold a personal licence, usually at their first appearance and before any plea is entered. This lets the court consider whether to make an order about the licence if the person is convicted.

Telling the council about convictions

A holder who is convicted of a relevant offence or a foreign offence, or who is given a relevant immigration civil penalty, must tell the council that issued the licence without delay. The council may then consider whether to take action against the licence.

Producing the licence

When working at licensed premises, a holder can be asked by a police officer or an authorised council officer to produce their licence. They must be able to do so within a reasonable time.

If you are charged with an offence

Being charged with a relevant offence, such as drink driving or an assault, is a worrying time. As well as dealing with the criminal case, it is worth thinking early about the licence, because the outcome of the case may lead to court or council action. Some holders instruct one firm to deal with both the criminal case and the licence.

If you lose your licence

If a licence is lost, stolen, damaged or destroyed, the holder can apply to the council for a replacement. Some councils ask for a police crime reference if it was stolen.

A licensing solicitor can explain exactly what these duties mean in your situation, particularly if you are facing a charge or have recently been convicted.

A personal licence can be taken away in two main ways. A criminal court can order it to be forfeited or suspended when the holder is convicted of a relevant offence, and the council can suspend or revoke it in certain circumstances.

Court orders

When a holder is convicted of a relevant offence, the court can order that the licence be forfeited or suspended for up to six months. It does not have to make such an order, and it will consider the circumstances of the offence and the holder. The holder can appeal the order in the same way as the conviction or sentence, and the court can suspend its order pending an appeal.

Council powers

The council that issued the licence can also act if it becomes aware that the holder has been convicted of a relevant offence or foreign offence, or given a relevant immigration civil penalty, after the licence was granted. It can choose to suspend the licence for up to six months or revoke it. It may also decide to take no action.

The council's procedure

Before deciding, the council must give the holder written notice and invite them to make representations within a set period, usually 28 days. The council may also ask the police for their view, and the holder can respond to what the police say. The council then decides, taking into account the crime prevention objective and any representations.

Making representations

This is the holder's chance to explain the circumstances of the offence, what has happened since and why they should keep the licence. Representations might include character references, evidence of employment and responsibilities, and any steps taken to address the behaviour behind the offence. A solicitor can help prepare them.

Appeals

A holder can appeal against a council decision to suspend or revoke the licence to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. The decision does not usually take effect until the appeal period has ended or any appeal has been dealt with.

If the licence is suspended, the holder cannot authorise alcohol sales or act as DPS for the period of the suspension. If it is revoked or forfeited, they would need to make a new application in future, and a forfeited licence prevents a new application for five years.

Personal licence matters are usually among the smaller licensing instructions, but the right help at the right time can still make a real difference.

Council fees

The fee for a personal licence application is set nationally and is relatively modest. There is also a small fee for updating details or replacing a lost licence. These amounts change from time to time, so check the current figures with the council or on GOV.UK.

Training and checks

Applicants also pay for the accredited qualification course and the basic criminal record certificate. Training providers set their own prices, which vary.

Solicitor fees

Many people do not need a solicitor for a straightforward application. Where there is a relevant conviction, a police objection or action against an existing licence, firms often offer a fixed fee for preparing representations and a separate fee for attending a hearing. Some employers pay for this help for their managers.

If you are an employer paying for help for a member of staff, ask the firm to agree in writing who the client is and who will receive advice, as this affects confidentiality.

Personal licences questions and answers

Do convictions stop me getting a licence?
Not always. The police can object, and the council decides.
Can my licence be taken away?
It can be suspended or revoked in some circumstances.
Do I need to tell the council about a conviction?
Personal licence holders have duties to notify. Get advice.
Which areas do you cover?
Licensing matters in England and Wales.
Do you help individuals as well as businesses?
Yes, for example personal licence holders and taxi or private hire drivers.
Does it cost anything?
Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Do I need a personal licence to work behind a bar?
No. Staff do not each need a personal licence to serve alcohol. Every sale must be made or authorised by a personal licence holder, so a holder, often the manager, authorises other staff to sell. You would usually need a personal licence to be named as the designated premises supervisor.
Which council do I apply to?
You apply to the council for the area where you are ordinarily resident, which is usually where you live. If you live outside England and Wales, you can apply to any licensing authority in England and Wales. The licence then stays with that council even if you move later.
What qualification do I need?
You need an accredited licensing qualification, usually a short course covering licensing law and the role of a personal licence holder, with an exam at the end. Approved training providers run these courses. Some other qualifications are also accepted. Check with the training provider that the course is accredited for personal licence applications.
How long does a personal licence take?
If there is nothing to declare and the application is complete, the council will usually process it within a few weeks, although times vary between councils. If you declare a relevant conviction, the police have time to consider objecting, and a hearing may follow, which adds more time.
Does a personal licence expire?
No. Personal licences no longer need renewing and last indefinitely unless they are surrendered, forfeited, suspended or revoked. If you have an older licence with an expiry date printed on it, that date no longer applies. Your licence remains valid.
Can I use my licence anywhere?
Yes. A personal licence granted by any council in England and Wales can be used at licensed premises anywhere in England and Wales. It is not tied to a particular premises or employer, so you keep it if you change jobs.
Do I need the right to work in the UK?
Yes. Applicants must have the right to work in the UK, and the council will check this. A personal licence can lapse if the holder's right to work ends. If your immigration status is time limited, the licence may be linked to that. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Can I get a personal licence with a criminal record?
Often yes. Many people with past convictions hold personal licences. Spent convictions are generally not relevant. If you have an unspent relevant conviction, the police can object and the council may hold a hearing. The outcome depends on the offence, how long ago it was and your circumstances since.
What is a relevant offence?
Relevant offences are listed in the Licensing Act 2003. They include certain offences involving alcohol, drink driving, violence, dishonesty, drugs, some sexual offences, certain immigration offences and offences under licensing law. Equivalent offences abroad can also count. A solicitor can check whether a particular conviction is relevant.
Do I have to declare a drink driving conviction?
Drink driving offences are relevant offences, so an unspent drink driving conviction must usually be declared on an application. Whether the conviction is spent depends on the sentence and the date. If it is spent, it generally does not need to be declared. A solicitor can check your position.
What if I do not declare a conviction?
Knowingly or recklessly making a false statement on an application is an offence. It can also lead to the licence being challenged later and damage your credibility. If you are unsure whether something needs to be declared, it is better to check with a solicitor before you apply.
Will the police always object?
No. The police consider each declared conviction and decide whether granting the licence would undermine the crime prevention objective. Many applications with older or minor convictions go through without objection. Where they do object, a hearing allows you to explain your circumstances.
What happens at a hearing about my application?
A licensing sub-committee hears from the police and from you or your representative. It considers the offence, its circumstances, your record since and your plans. You can provide references and evidence of your work. The committee then decides whether to grant or refuse, and must give reasons.
Can I appeal if my application is refused?
Yes. You can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. The court looks at the matter again. Appeals carry a risk of paying the council's costs if unsuccessful, so a solicitor will explain the merits before you decide.
Do motoring offences other than drink driving count?
Most ordinary motoring offences, such as speeding, are not relevant offences for a personal licence. Some driving offences involving alcohol or drugs are relevant. The list in the Licensing Act 2003 is specific, so a solicitor can check whether any particular offence counts before you apply or report it.
What do I have to tell the council?
You must tell the council that issued your licence about any change of name or address as soon as reasonably practicable. You must also tell it without delay if you are convicted of a relevant offence or foreign offence, or given a relevant immigration civil penalty. Failing to notify can be an offence.
What do I have to tell a court?
If you are charged with a relevant offence, you must tell the court that you hold a personal licence, usually at your first appearance and before entering a plea. If you do not know at that point, you must tell the court at your next appearance. A solicitor representing you in the criminal case can help with this.
Do I need to carry my licence at work?
You must be able to produce your licence within a reasonable time if a police officer or authorised council officer asks while you are working at licensed premises. Many holders keep the photocard with them and the paper part safely at work or at home.
What if I move to a different area?
Your licence stays with the council that issued it. You must tell that council about your new address, and there is usually a small fee for updating the licence. You do not need to apply to the council in your new area.
Can I give temporary event notices?
Yes. Personal licence holders can give temporary event notices, and they can give more each year than people who do not hold a personal licence. Each notice is still subject to the usual limits on numbers, length and timing, and the police and environmental health can object.
Can I surrender my personal licence?
Yes. A holder can surrender a personal licence by giving notice to the council that issued it, with the licence or an explanation of why it cannot be returned. Once surrendered, it stops having effect. If you think you might need a licence again in future, it is worth thinking carefully before surrendering it.
Can a court take my licence away?
If you are convicted of a relevant offence, the court can order your licence to be forfeited or suspended for up to six months. It does not have to make an order. It will consider the circumstances of the offence and your situation. Any order can be appealed in the same way as the conviction or sentence.
Can the council suspend or revoke my licence?
Yes. If the council becomes aware that you have been convicted of a relevant offence or foreign offence, or given a relevant immigration civil penalty, after your licence was granted, it can suspend it for up to six months or revoke it. It may also decide to take no action.
Will I get a chance to respond?
Yes. The council must give you written notice and invite you to make representations within a set period, usually 28 days. If the police give their view, you can respond to that too. The council then decides. A solicitor can help you prepare representations.
What should my representations include?
Representations usually explain the circumstances of the offence, what has happened since and why keeping the licence would not undermine crime prevention. Character references, evidence of your work and any steps taken to address the behaviour can help. A solicitor can help make them clear and focused.
Can I keep working while I appeal?
If the council suspends or revokes your licence, the decision does not usually take effect until the 21 day appeal period ends or any appeal is dealt with. For court orders, the court can suspend its order pending an appeal. A solicitor can explain how this works in your case.
What happens if I am a DPS and lose my licence?
If your personal licence is suspended, revoked or forfeited, you cannot act as designated premises supervisor and alcohol sales at that premises must stop until a new DPS is named. Telling your employer early allows them to plan. A solicitor can help you and your employer understand the options.
How much does a personal licence cost?
There is a council application fee set nationally, plus the cost of the accredited course and a basic criminal record certificate. Training providers set their own prices. Council fees change from time to time, so check the current figures with the council or on GOV.UK.
Do I need a solicitor for my application?
Many people apply without one. Legal help is more often useful where you have an unspent relevant conviction, the police object, or the council or a court is considering action against your licence. A solicitor can explain your position and help with representations and hearings.
Does Leaflegal charge me anything?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The licensing firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that does not change what you pay them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.
What should I have ready when the firm calls?
Have your licence or application details, the council's name, details of any convictions with dates and sentences, and any letters from the council, police or court. If there is a deadline for representations or a hearing date, mention it on the form so the firm knows it is urgent.
What is the difference between a taxi and a private hire vehicle?
A hackney carriage, often called a taxi or black cab, can be hailed in the street and use ranks in the area where it is licensed. A private hire vehicle must be booked in advance through a licensed operator and cannot pick up people who flag it down. They are licensed under different laws and often have different requirements.
Who licenses taxis and private hire vehicles?
Outside London, the district, borough, city or unitary council licenses drivers, vehicles and operators for its area. In London, Transport for London is responsible. Each licensing authority sets its own policy, fees and requirements within the law, so they differ from one area to another.
What checks do I need for a driver licence?
Most councils require an enhanced criminal record check with barred lists, a medical, a DVLA driving record check, proof of the right to work and a knowledge test. Many also require safeguarding and disability awareness training, and some require an English or communication assessment. Your council's policy sets out the full list.
Can I get a licence with a criminal record?
It depends on the offence, how long ago it was and your record since. Most councils have a policy setting out how long a person should normally be free of certain types of offence. Serious violent or sexual offences usually lead to refusal. Each case is still considered individually. A solicitor can look at your history and the policy.
Can I drive for a private hire operator licensed in a different area?
For private hire, the driver, vehicle and operator generally all need to be licensed by the same council. Hackney carriages can be pre-booked to go to other areas. The rules on cross-border working are complex, and a solicitor or the council can explain how they apply to your work.
What does fit and proper mean?
It is the test councils use to decide whether someone can hold a driver or operator licence. There is no fixed definition, but the focus is on passenger safety and whether the person can be trusted. Councils look at convictions, complaints, driving record and conduct. They do not need a criminal conviction to decide someone is not fit and proper.
Do I need to renew my licence?
Yes. Driver, vehicle and operator licences are granted for set periods and must be renewed. Renewal is not automatic, and the council will look again at your record. Applying in good time avoids a gap during which you cannot work. Check your council's renewal deadlines.
Can I transfer my badge to a new council?
No. A badge issued by one council cannot simply be transferred to another. If you want to work in a different area, you usually need to apply to that council and meet its requirements, which may include its own knowledge test and checks. Your history with the first council will usually need to be declared.
