About licence variations

If you want to extend your hours, change the layout, add activities or remove conditions, you will usually need to vary your premises licence. Small changes can sometimes use a quicker minor variation.

Licence variations explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A premises licence describes the business as it was when the licence was granted. It sets the hours, the activities, the layout on the plan and the conditions. Businesses change over time, and when they do, the licence often has to change with them. The way to do that under the Licensing Act 2003 is usually a variation application to the council.

Common reasons for varying a licence include staying open later, adding live music or late night food, extending into a new room or outside space, changing from a wet led pub to a food led restaurant, or removing conditions that no longer fit the way the premises runs. Operators also vary licences after a refurbishment, because the plan attached to the licence must match the actual layout.

There are two main routes. A minor variation is a quicker, cheaper process for changes that could not adversely affect the licensing objectives. A full variation follows a process much like a new licence application, with advertising, a consultation and the possibility of a hearing. Choosing the wrong route can waste time and money, so this is often the first thing a licensing solicitor will look at.

Some changes cannot be made by any kind of variation. If the premises is being changed so much that it is effectively a different place, a new premises licence application may be needed. And a change of designated premises supervisor has its own separate application, which is covered on our page about transfers and DPS changes.

Checking the licence you already have

Before applying for anything, it is worth reading the existing licence carefully. Many operators are surprised to find conditions they did not know about, a plan that shows an old layout or hours that differ between activities. A licensing solicitor will often start by comparing the licence with how the premises actually trades, so that one well planned application can deal with everything that needs changing.

Why it matters to get it right

Trading outside the terms of the licence, such as serving alcohol after the permitted hour or using a room that is not on the plan, can be a criminal offence and can lead to a review. It can also cause problems when the business is sold, because a buyer's solicitor will compare the licence with how the premises actually trades. Keeping the licence up to date protects the value of the business.

Leaflegal does not advise on what changes your licence needs. We introduce you to a regulated licensing firm that can look at your current licence and your plans and explain your options.

The minor variation process was introduced to make small changes simpler. It is only available where the change could not have an adverse impact on any of the four licensing objectives. The council decides whether that test is met.

What a minor variation can usually cover

  • Small changes to the layout shown on the plan
  • Removing a licensable activity you no longer need
  • Adding certain types of regulated entertainment
  • Removing out of date, unclear or unenforceable conditions
  • Adding conditions you have agreed to offer
  • Reducing hours for a licensable activity

What it cannot be used for

A minor variation cannot be used to extend the period during which alcohol is sold or supplied, to add alcohol sales to a licence that does not already have them, to change alcohol sales between 11pm and 7am, to extend the period for which the licence has effect, to vary the premises substantially or to change the designated premises supervisor. These changes need a full variation or a different application.

How it works

The application is made to the council with a plan if the layout is changing, and a white notice is displayed at the premises for a set number of working days. The council consults whichever responsible authorities it thinks appropriate, and other people can make representations within a short period. There is no hearing. The council considers any representations and must decide within a fixed number of working days.

If it is refused

There is no right of appeal against a refused minor variation, but the applicant can apply again as a full variation. Your solicitor will consider at the outset whether the change is clear cut enough to go as a minor variation, or whether a full variation is more sensible from the start.

Because the council has a fair amount of judgement in deciding whether a change is truly minor, it can help to explain clearly in the application why the change will not affect the objectives. A short covering note setting out the reasons, with photographs or a marked up plan where useful, can make the council's job easier.

A full variation is needed for changes that could affect the licensing objectives, such as later hours, new alcohol sales or more substantial changes to the layout. The process is very similar to applying for a new licence.

What goes in

The application form sets out the changes being sought, with an updated operating schedule describing any new steps to promote the licensing objectives. If the layout is changing, a new plan is required. Copies go to the responsible authorities in line with the council's arrangements.

Advertising

A blue notice must be displayed at the premises for 28 consecutive days from the day after the application is made, and a notice must appear in a local newspaper within the set time. The council also publishes the application. Mistakes in the notices can invalidate the application, just as they can with a new licence.

Consultation

There is a 28 day period for representations. Only representations about the effect of the proposed changes are relevant, not complaints about the existing licence as a whole. That is an important difference from a new application, and your solicitor may raise it if objectors stray beyond the variation.

Talking to the authorities

Because a full variation often involves later hours or new activities, the police and environmental health are often closely involved. Many solicitors approach them before applying to understand any concerns and to offer conditions that may address them. This can avoid a hearing.

Keeping your current licence

While a variation is being considered, the existing licence continues in force. If the variation is refused, you keep trading on the licence you already have. If it is granted, the changes take effect in line with the council's decision.

If several changes are being made at once, such as later hours, a new outside area and some updated conditions, they can usually be combined into one full variation. That saves on fees and advertising, but it also means objectors can comment on everything together. Your solicitor may suggest separating out the less controversial changes so they are not held up.

Every variation is different, but some kinds come up again and again. Here is a short guide to the issues that often arise with each.

Extending hours

Later hours are one of the most common requests and one of the most likely to attract objections, especially in residential areas or places covered by a cumulative impact policy. Applicants often support the request with a dispersal policy, extra staffing, door supervision at busy times or a limit on the number of late nights each year. Some ask for later hours only on certain days or for special occasions.

Adding outside areas

Bringing a garden, terrace or roof space into the licensed area can raise concerns about noise and smoking. Applications often include limits on when the area is used, how many people can be there and whether music can be played outside. Pavement seating on the public highway usually needs a separate permission from the council.

Changing the layout

After a refit, the plan on the licence may no longer match the premises. Small changes can often go through as a minor variation. Bigger changes, such as moving exits or adding a new floor, may need a full variation or advice from the fire authority.

Removing or updating conditions

Many older licences carry conditions that were converted from the old system, are badly worded or no longer make sense. Removing or rewording these can reduce the risk of accidental breaches. Where a condition was added by a committee after a hearing or review, removing it may be harder and may need a full variation with a clear explanation of why it is no longer needed.

Adding activities

Operators sometimes want to add live music, films, indoor sport, late night refreshment or off sales such as deliveries. Some entertainment no longer needs a licence in certain circumstances. Your solicitor can check what actually needs adding before an application is made.

Changing the style of business

When a premises changes concept, for example from a late night bar to a family restaurant, the old licence may allow more than is needed while also carrying conditions that do not fit. Some operators reduce hours or remove activities at the same time as adding new ones. Showing that the overall impact will be lower can help an application that also asks for something new.

If relevant representations are made about a full variation and they are not resolved, the council holds a hearing before a licensing sub-committee. The procedure is the same as for a new licence, with the applicant, the authorities and objectors all able to speak.

What the committee can decide

  • Grant the variation as applied for
  • Grant it with modified or extra conditions
  • Grant part of it, such as later hours on some days but not others
  • Reject the variation in full

The committee can only deal with the changes applied for. It cannot use a variation hearing to take away rights the premises already has, although concerns raised at the hearing could later lead to a review.

Showing a track record

An established premises has an advantage that a new venue does not. It can show how it has traded, whether there have been complaints and how it has worked with the police. Evidence such as incident logs, a quiet record with the authorities and positive feedback from neighbours can be useful. Equally, a history of problems will be raised by objectors.

Hearings often turn on practical detail. The committee may want to know how staff will manage a queue at closing time, how noise from an outside area will be checked or how a delivery driver will verify age. Being ready to answer these questions clearly, with written policies to back them up, usually helps.

Appeals

An applicant can appeal a refusal, or conditions they are unhappy with, to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. People who made relevant representations can also appeal against a grant. Appeals involve costs risks, and your solicitor will explain these.

Trying a different approach

Sometimes a refused variation can be revisited later with a more modest request, better supporting evidence or a trial period through temporary event notices. Councils can refuse to consider a repeat application that is essentially the same as one recently refused, so changing the proposal in a meaningful way matters.

The overall cost of a variation depends on which route is used and whether there are objections.

Council fees

A minor variation has a set flat fee. A full variation fee usually depends on the rateable value band of the premises, in the same way as a new licence. Fees change from time to time, so check the current figures with the council or on GOV.UK.

Solicitor fees

Minor variations are often done for a modest fixed fee. Full variations may be quoted as a fixed fee for the application, with a separate fee for a hearing if one is needed. If the variation is part of a wider project, such as a refurbishment or a change of concept, some firms will quote for the licensing work as a package.

Other costs

  • A new plan if the layout is changing
  • The newspaper notice for a full variation
  • Acoustic or noise reports for later hours or outside music
  • Extra staffing or equipment you agree to as conditions

If you are not sure whether a variation is worth the cost, ask the firm for an early view on the likely route, the chance of objections and the overall budget before committing to the full application.

It is worth thinking about the cost of conditions as well as the cost of the application. Agreeing to door supervisors every night, for example, can cost far more over a year than the application itself.

Licence variations questions and answers

What is a minor variation?
A simpler process for changes that won’t affect the licensing objectives.
Can I extend my hours?
You can apply. Residents and authorities may object.
How long does it take?
It depends on the type of variation and whether there are objections.
Which areas do you cover?
Licensing matters in England and Wales.
Do you help individuals as well as businesses?
Yes, for example personal licence holders and taxi or private hire drivers.
Does it cost anything?
Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
What is the difference between a minor and a full variation?
A minor variation is a quicker process for small changes that could not adversely affect the licensing objectives. It has a shorter notice period, no hearing and a lower fee. A full variation is needed for bigger changes such as later alcohol hours. It involves a 28 day consultation, newspaper and blue notices and the possibility of a hearing.
Can I extend my alcohol hours with a minor variation?
No. A minor variation cannot be used to extend the hours during which alcohol is sold or supplied, or to change alcohol sales between 11pm and 7am. Those changes need a full variation. A minor variation can, however, be used to reduce hours if you no longer need them.
Can I add alcohol sales to my licence?
If your licence does not currently permit alcohol, adding it needs a full variation, not a minor one. You will also need a designated premises supervisor who holds a personal licence. A solicitor can explain whether a full variation or a new application is more suitable.
When do I need a new licence instead of a variation?
A variation cannot be used to vary the premises substantially. If a building is being completely rebuilt, extended into a different property or changed so much that it is effectively new premises, a fresh application may be required. Where the line falls is a matter of judgement, and a solicitor can advise on your project.
Can I change the DPS through a variation?
Changing the designated premises supervisor uses a separate, specific application, not a minor or full variation. It is usually quicker and can take immediate effect. Our page on transfers and DPS changes explains more about it.
Do I need to vary my licence after a refurbishment?
If the refurbishment changes the layout shown on your licence plan, such as moving the bar, changing exits or adding a new area, the plan will need updating. Small changes may be dealt with by a minor variation. Bigger changes may need a full variation. Trading on an out of date plan can cause problems.
How long does a minor variation take?
The council must make a decision within a set number of working days from receiving the application, and the white notice must be displayed for a shorter period than a full variation. In practice a minor variation is usually much quicker than a full variation. If the council does not decide in time, the application is treated as refused.
How long does a full variation take?
There is a 28 day consultation period. If there are no unresolved representations, the variation is usually granted shortly after. If a hearing is needed, it is normally held within 20 working days of the end of the consultation. Allow time beforehand for preparing the application and any plans.
What notice do I need to display?
For a minor variation, a white notice is displayed at the premises for a set period. For a full variation, a blue notice is displayed for 28 consecutive days, and a notice must also be published in a local newspaper. Each has set requirements for size, wording and position.
Can I trade under the new terms while I wait?
No. Until the variation is granted, you must trade in line with your existing licence. For a one off event, a temporary event notice may sometimes help, within the limits that apply. A solicitor can explain the options if you have a fixed date in mind.
Who is consulted on a minor variation?
The council decides which responsible authorities to consult, depending on what is being changed. Other people can also make representations during the notice period. The council considers what is said and decides whether the change could adversely affect the licensing objectives.
Do I need a new plan?
Only if the variation changes the layout or the areas used for licensable activities. If it does, the plan must meet the same requirements as for a new licence, including showing exits, fire safety equipment and the licensed area. Many operators use a specialist to redraw the plan.
What if the council does not decide a minor variation in time?
If the council does not make a decision within the time allowed, the application is treated as refused and the fee is not usually returned. In that situation the applicant can apply again, either as a minor variation or as a full variation. A solicitor can explain why the deadline was missed and what to do next.
Can I get later hours just for weekends or special days?
Yes, a variation can ask for different hours on different days, or for later hours on particular occasions such as bank holidays or New Year's Eve. These can be easier to justify than later hours every night. Temporary event notices are another way to cover occasional late nights, within the limits that apply.
Can I add a beer garden to my licence?
Usually yes, by including the outside area on the plan through a variation. Whether a minor or full variation is suitable depends on the possible impact. Conditions limiting the times the area is used, the number of people or the playing of music are common. Pavement seating on the highway usually needs separate permission.
Can I remove conditions that do not make sense?
Often yes. Unclear, out of date or unenforceable conditions can sometimes be removed by a minor variation. Conditions imposed after a hearing or review may need a full variation and a clear explanation. Your solicitor will look at the history of each condition before suggesting an approach.
Can I add deliveries or off sales?
If your licence only allows alcohol for consumption on the premises, adding sales for consumption off the premises, such as deliveries or takeaway drinks, usually needs a full variation. Conditions about age checks at the point of delivery are common. A solicitor can explain what the council will usually expect.
Do I need a variation to add live music?
Not always. Some live and recorded music in licensed premises has been deregulated within certain hours and audience sizes. Outside those limits, or for other types of entertainment, a variation may be needed. A solicitor can check whether your plans are already covered.
Can I change the times for late night refreshment only?
Yes. Late night refreshment is a separate licensable activity from alcohol, so the hours for hot food and drink can be varied on their own. Extending them still needs the right kind of variation and may attract objections about noise or people gathering. A solicitor can explain how your council usually treats these requests.
Can residents object to a variation?
Yes. Anyone can make a representation about a full variation, and people can also comment on a minor variation. For a full variation, representations must relate to the effect of the changes on the licensing objectives. Complaints about the existing licence as a whole are not normally relevant to a variation.
What happens if the police object?
Police representations are given significant weight because they are the main source of advice on crime and disorder. Many police objections are resolved by agreeing conditions or adjusting the hours requested. If not, the matter goes to a hearing. A solicitor can talk to the police licensing team on your behalf.
Can the council take away my existing hours at a variation hearing?
Generally the committee deals only with the changes applied for and cannot use a variation to take away existing rights. However, it can attach conditions to the varied part of the licence. Serious concerns raised at the hearing could lead someone to apply for a review separately.
Can I appeal a refused full variation?
Yes. An applicant can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. There is no right of appeal against a refused minor variation, but you can apply again as a full variation. Your solicitor will explain the merits and costs risks before you decide.
Can I reapply if my variation is refused?
You can apply again, but a council can treat a repeat application that is essentially the same as one recently refused as repetitive. Changing the proposal meaningfully, such as asking for shorter hours or offering stronger conditions, gives a better basis for a fresh application.
How much does a variation cost?
The council fee for a minor variation is a flat amount, while a full variation fee usually depends on the rateable value of the premises. Solicitor fees vary with the complexity of the change and whether there is a hearing. Check current council fees with the council or on GOV.UK, and ask the firm for a written quote.
Can a solicitor review my whole licence?
Yes. Many operators ask a solicitor to review the licence before a variation, checking the plan, hours and conditions against how the premises actually trades. This can identify other changes worth making at the same time and reduce the risk of accidental breaches.
Does Leaflegal charge me anything?
No. Leaflegal is free for businesses to use. The licensing firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that does not change what you pay them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.
What should I have ready when the firm calls?
A copy of your current licence with its plan and conditions is the most useful thing. It also helps to explain what you want to change and why, any deadline such as a reopening date, and any history of complaints or contact with the police or environmental health about the premises.
Can the firm help with several premises at once?
Many licensing firms act for operators with several sites and can prepare variations across different councils. Each council applies its own policy, so the approach may differ from one area to another. Ask the firm how they manage multi site work and whether they offer a combined fee for related applications.
When do I need to transfer a premises licence?
A transfer is usually needed when someone new is taking over the business and will be the licence holder, such as on the sale of a business and its assets, the assignment of a lease or a new tenancy of a pub. If you are buying the company that already holds the licence, the holder does not change and a transfer is not usually needed.
Who makes the transfer application?
The person or company who wants to become the new licence holder makes the application. It goes to the council that issued the licence, with copies to the police and usually Home Office Immigration Enforcement. A solicitor can prepare the application and make sure it goes to everyone who needs to receive it.
What if the current holder will not consent?
The application can still be made, but you must explain why consent has not been obtained and show you have taken all reasonable steps to get it. In that case the transfer cannot take immediate effect, so you cannot trade under the licence until it is granted. A solicitor can help where the previous holder cannot be found or will not cooperate.
Can I trade while the transfer is processed?
If the current holder has consented and the application asks for immediate effect, you are treated as the licence holder from the time the council receives the application. That lets you trade without a gap. If the police later object and the transfer is refused, the licence goes back to the previous holder.
Can the police object to a transfer?
Yes, but only in exceptional circumstances where they believe the transfer would undermine the crime prevention objective. Home Office Immigration Enforcement can object on grounds about illegal working. Objections must be made within 14 days. If one is made, the council holds a hearing.
Will there be a right to work check?
Yes, if the transfer applicant is an individual. The council must check that they have the right to work in the UK, and documents usually go with the application. A licence held by an individual can also lapse if they stop being entitled to work in the UK. Companies are treated differently.
Can a licence be transferred between companies in the same group?
Yes. If a group reorganises and a different company will run the premises, a transfer to that company is usually needed so that the licence holder matches the business operating the site. The process is the same as any other transfer. Your solicitor can check whether a transfer is needed or whether the existing holder can stay in place.
What does a designated premises supervisor do?
The DPS is the person named on the licence as having day to day responsibility for the premises. They must hold a personal licence. They are the main point of contact for the police and council, and they are expected to be closely involved in how alcohol sales are managed. There is no rule that they must be there at all times.
