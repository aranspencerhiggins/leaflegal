About transfers and dps changes

When a business is bought or the operator changes, the premises licence needs to be transferred. When a manager leaves, the designated premises supervisor needs to be changed. Both can usually take effect quickly if done correctly.

Designated premises supervisor in brief

When a business changes hands, the premises licence usually needs transferring to the new operator. When the person running the bar changes, the designated premises supervisor (DPS) must be updated.

Both can be done quickly with the right paperwork. A licensing solicitor can handle it for you.

Transfers and DPS changes explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Two people matter most on a premises licence that permits alcohol sales. The first is the premises licence holder, the person or company the licence belongs to. The second is the designated premises supervisor, usually called the DPS, who must hold a personal licence and is normally the manager in day to day charge. When either of them changes, the licence usually needs updating with the council.

A transfer changes the premises licence holder. It is most often needed when a business is sold, when a tenant takes over a pub from a pub company, when a lease is assigned or when a business is moved from one company to another within a group. A change of DPS is needed whenever the named supervisor leaves, moves to another site or is no longer suitable.

Both applications are fairly routine and are usually quick. Under the Licensing Act 2003 they can often be given immediate effect, which means trading can carry on while the council processes them. But the details matter. A missing consent, the wrong applicant or a transfer that is not completed on time can leave a business unable to sell alcohol, or in the worst cases can cause the licence to be lost altogether.

Why these changes are easy to overlook

Licensing is often a small part of a much bigger event, such as the sale of a business, a refinancing or a change of management. Busy owners and their advisers may focus on the property and the price and leave the licence until the last minute. Some of the most serious licensing problems arise because nobody took responsibility for the transfer or the DPS change at the right moment.

The same applies to operators with several sites. Managers move between premises, companies are restructured and licences are sometimes held in the name of a company that no longer trades. A periodic check of who holds each licence and who is named as DPS can catch problems before they cause a gap in trading.

A licensing solicitor can deal with the applications, coordinate with the business sale solicitors and make sure the timing works. They can also check the licence itself, so that the buyer or new manager knows exactly what they are taking on.

A transfer application is made by the person or company who wants to become the new premises licence holder. It is sent to the council that issued the licence, with a copy to the police and, in most cases, to Home Office Immigration Enforcement.

Who can apply

The new holder must be eligible to hold a premises licence, in the same way as an applicant for a new licence. An individual must be 18 or over and have the right to work in the UK. A company applies through its registered details. Your solicitor will check that the right person or entity is named, which can be important for tax, funding and future sale reasons.

Consent from the current holder

The application usually needs the written consent of the current licence holder. If consent cannot be obtained, for example because the previous holder has disappeared or refuses to cooperate, the applicant has to explain why and show they have taken all reasonable steps to obtain it. The council can still grant the transfer, but it cannot take immediate effect in that case.

Immediate effect

Where the current holder consents, the application can ask for the transfer to have immediate effect. The applicant is then treated as the licence holder from the moment the application is received, while the council considers it. This allows the new owner to trade from completion without a gap.

Police and immigration objections

The police can object within 14 days if they believe, in exceptional circumstances, that granting the transfer would undermine the crime prevention objective. Home Office Immigration Enforcement can object on grounds relating to the prevention of illegal working. Objections are uncommon but are taken seriously. If one is made, the council holds a hearing, and a solicitor can represent the applicant.

If there is no objection

If no objection is made within the time allowed, the council must grant the transfer. The council then issues an updated licence and summary showing the new holder, which must be kept at the premises.

If a transfer is refused after it was given immediate effect, the licence goes back to the previous holder. That can leave a buyer who has already completed unable to trade under it. This is one reason why buyers are sometimes advised to look into any likely police concerns before completion rather than after.

Every premises licence that authorises the sale of alcohol must name a DPS, unless it is community premises that has had the requirement removed. No alcohol can be sold when there is no DPS, or when the DPS does not hold a valid personal licence or has had it suspended. A gap can therefore stop alcohol sales completely.

Applying to change the DPS

The application is made by the premises licence holder, not by the new DPS. It includes a consent form signed by the new DPS. A copy goes to the police, and the outgoing DPS must be notified. The application can ask for the change to have immediate effect, so the new DPS takes over from the moment the council receives it.

Police objections

The police can object within 14 days if they believe, in exceptional circumstances, that the new DPS would undermine the crime prevention objective. This might happen if the proposed person has a history of problems at other premises. A hearing then follows. If the change was requested with immediate effect and the objection succeeds, the new DPS is removed.

When a DPS leaves

A DPS can remove themselves by giving notice to the council and to the licence holder. If a manager leaves suddenly, the licence holder should arrange a new DPS as quickly as possible, because alcohol sales cannot lawfully continue without one. Many operators keep a second personal licence holder on the team so that a replacement can be named straight away.

What the DPS role involves

There is no legal requirement for the DPS to be present at all times, but they are expected to be the main point of contact for the police and council and to take an active role in how the premises runs. Some operators name an area manager as DPS for several sites, although councils and police often prefer a DPS who is closely involved with the specific premises.

Community premises

Church halls, village halls and similar community premises can apply to remove the usual DPS requirement. Instead, a management committee takes responsibility for the supervision of alcohol sales. There are conditions for this, and the council and police can object in some circumstances.

Changing the DPS does not affect the premises licence holder, the conditions or the hours. It simply updates the name on the licence. But the police may take a close interest where a premises has had problems, and a new DPS with a good record can sometimes help to rebuild trust with the authorities.

Licensing should be looked at early in any purchase of a pub, bar, restaurant, shop or venue. The licence affects what the business can do and how much it is worth, and problems found after completion can be expensive to fix.

Checking the licence before you buy

  • Who holds the licence and whether they can sign a transfer consent
  • Whether the annual fee has been paid
  • Whether the hours, activities and plan match how the business trades
  • What conditions apply and whether they are being complied with
  • Whether there has been a review, warning or enforcement action
  • Whether the premises sits in a cumulative impact area
  • Who the DPS is and whether they are staying

Share sale or asset sale

If you are buying the company that holds the licence, the licence holder stays the same, so a transfer is not usually needed. You may still need to change the DPS, and you will be taking on the company's licensing history. If you are buying the business and its assets, the licence will need to be transferred to the buyer, timed to take effect on completion.

Timing on completion

A common approach is for the seller to sign the transfer consent in advance, with the buyer's solicitor holding it until completion. The transfer and, if needed, the DPS change are then submitted on the day of completion with a request for immediate effect. This allows the new owner to trade from the first day.

Shadow licences

Landlords and pub companies sometimes hold a second premises licence for the same building, often called a shadow licence. It protects them if the tenant's licence is lost, for example through revocation or lapse. A shadow licence can also be relevant to a buyer, as it may affect the value of the tenant's own licence. Your solicitor can explain how it fits into the deal.

Sellers can help a sale go smoothly too. Having the licence, plan and summary to hand, confirming the annual fee is up to date and being ready to sign a transfer consent can avoid last minute delays.

A premises licence can lapse if the holder dies, becomes mentally incapable, becomes insolvent, is dissolved as a company or, in some cases, stops being entitled to work in the UK. It can also end if the holder surrenders it. When a licence lapses, licensable activities must stop unless action is taken in time.

Interim authority notices

In certain situations, such as the death or insolvency of the holder, a person with a prescribed interest in the premises, or someone connected to the former holder, can give an interim authority notice. This revives the licence for a limited period, giving time for a transfer to be arranged. The notice must be given within a short window after the lapse, and the police can object.

Reinstatement by transfer

It is also possible to apply for a transfer within a short period after a lapse or surrender, asking for the licence to be reinstated. Strict time limits apply, so this needs to be dealt with quickly.

Landlords, lenders and insolvency practitioners often have the most to lose when a licence lapses, because the value of the building may depend on it. Many keep a close eye on the position of tenants and borrowers so that they can act within the time limits if something goes wrong.

Why speed matters

If the time limits pass without action, the licence may be lost permanently. A new premises licence would then be needed, with full advertising, consultation and the risk of objections. For a valuable late licence in a busy area, that could make a big difference to the business. If you think a licence has lapsed or is at risk, speak to a licensing solicitor straight away.

Other changes to report

Even when no transfer is needed, some changes must be notified to the council. These include a change in the name or address of the licence holder and a change in the name or address of the DPS. A company that holds licences should keep its details up to date.

Transfers and DPS changes are among the lower cost licensing applications, but it is still worth understanding all the parts.

Council fees

Fees for a transfer, a change of DPS and an interim authority notice are set nationally and are the same wherever the premises is. Notification of a change of name or address also has a small fee. These amounts change from time to time, so check the current figures with the council or on GOV.UK.

Solicitor fees

Many firms offer fixed fees for transfers and DPS changes, sometimes with a reduced fee when both are done together. On a business purchase, the licensing work may be part of a wider fee covering due diligence on the licence. If the police object and a hearing is needed, that work is usually charged separately.

Avoiding hidden costs

The biggest costs in this area tend to come from things going wrong, such as a lapsed licence that has to be replaced with a new application, or a period when alcohol cannot be sold because there is no DPS. Spending a little time getting the timing right usually costs far less.

Does a new DPS need a personal licence?
Yes.
Can the police object?
Yes, in exceptional circumstances. A solicitor can represent you.
Which areas do you cover?
Licensing matters in England and Wales.
Do you help individuals as well as businesses?
Yes, for example personal licence holders and taxi or private hire drivers.
Does it cost anything?

When do I need to transfer a premises licence?
A transfer is usually needed when someone new is taking over the business and will be the licence holder, such as on the sale of a business and its assets, the assignment of a lease or a new tenancy of a pub. If you are buying the company that already holds the licence, the holder does not change and a transfer is not usually needed.
Who makes the transfer application?
The person or company who wants to become the new licence holder makes the application. It goes to the council that issued the licence, with copies to the police and usually Home Office Immigration Enforcement. A solicitor can prepare the application and make sure it goes to everyone who needs to receive it.
What if the current holder will not consent?
The application can still be made, but you must explain why consent has not been obtained and show you have taken all reasonable steps to get it. In that case the transfer cannot take immediate effect, so you cannot trade under the licence until it is granted. A solicitor can help where the previous holder cannot be found or will not cooperate.
Can I trade while the transfer is processed?
If the current holder has consented and the application asks for immediate effect, you are treated as the licence holder from the time the council receives the application. That lets you trade without a gap. If the police later object and the transfer is refused, the licence goes back to the previous holder.
Can the police object to a transfer?
Yes, but only in exceptional circumstances where they believe the transfer would undermine the crime prevention objective. Home Office Immigration Enforcement can object on grounds about illegal working. Objections must be made within 14 days. If one is made, the council holds a hearing.
Will there be a right to work check?
Yes, if the transfer applicant is an individual. The council must check that they have the right to work in the UK, and documents usually go with the application. A licence held by an individual can also lapse if they stop being entitled to work in the UK. Companies are treated differently.
Can a licence be transferred between companies in the same group?
Yes. If a group reorganises and a different company will run the premises, a transfer to that company is usually needed so that the licence holder matches the business operating the site. The process is the same as any other transfer. Your solicitor can check whether a transfer is needed or whether the existing holder can stay in place.
What does a designated premises supervisor do?
The DPS is the person named on the licence as having day to day responsibility for the premises. They must hold a personal licence. They are the main point of contact for the police and council, and they are expected to be closely involved in how alcohol sales are managed. There is no rule that they must be there at all times.
Who applies to change the DPS?
The premises licence holder makes the application, with a consent form signed by the new DPS. A copy goes to the police and the outgoing DPS must be notified. The application can ask for immediate effect so that the new DPS takes over straight away.
What happens if my DPS leaves without notice?
If the DPS removes themselves, alcohol cannot lawfully be sold until a new DPS is in place. The licence holder should apply to name a replacement as quickly as possible, with immediate effect. Keeping a second personal licence holder on staff can help cover this kind of gap.
Can one person be DPS at more than one premises?
Yes, the law does not prevent it. However, the DPS is expected to be able to take responsibility for each site, and the police may have concerns if someone is named for many premises they rarely visit. Some councils address this in their policies. A solicitor can explain local expectations.
Can the police object to a new DPS?
Yes, in exceptional circumstances, if they believe the person would undermine the crime prevention objective. They must object within 14 days. A hearing then takes place. A solicitor can represent the licence holder and the proposed DPS at the hearing.
Can I remove the DPS requirement?
Only community premises, such as church halls and village halls, can apply to remove it. A management committee then takes responsibility for supervising alcohol sales. Other premises that sell alcohol must have a DPS. A solicitor can tell you whether your premises qualifies.
Does the DPS have to be employed at the premises?
There is no strict rule that the DPS must be employed at the premises, but they need to be able to take real responsibility for it. Councils and police generally expect the DPS to be closely involved in running the site. A solicitor can explain what your council and police usually expect from a DPS.
What happens if the DPS loses their personal licence?
If the DPS has their personal licence suspended, revoked or forfeited, they can no longer act as DPS and alcohol sales must stop until a new DPS is named. The licence holder should apply for a replacement as soon as possible. Keeping track of the DPS's position helps avoid an unexpected gap.
What should a buyer check about the licence?
A buyer will usually want to see the full licence with its plan and conditions, check the annual fee has been paid, confirm the licence matches how the business trades and find out about any reviews, warnings or complaints. Checking the DPS arrangements and any shadow licence is also common. A licensing solicitor can carry out these checks.
How is the transfer timed on a sale?
Often the seller signs the transfer consent before completion, and the buyer's solicitor holds it. On the day of completion, the transfer and DPS change are submitted with a request for immediate effect. This allows the buyer to trade from day one. The licensing solicitor coordinates with the business sale solicitors.
Does a share sale need a transfer?
Not usually. If you buy the company that holds the licence, the holder stays the same. You may still want to change the DPS and update any notified details. You will also inherit the company's licensing history, so it is worth checking the background before you buy.
What is a shadow licence?
A shadow licence is a second premises licence for the same building, usually held by the landlord or pub company. It protects their interest if the tenant's licence is lost. A buyer or tenant should know whether a shadow licence exists, as it can affect their position. A solicitor can explain how it works in your deal.
Should the licence match how the business trades?
Yes. If a seller has been trading later than the licence allows, using areas not on the plan or ignoring conditions, a buyer may be taking on a business that cannot lawfully trade in the same way. Finding this out before completion gives the buyer the chance to negotiate or ask for the problem to be fixed.
What happens if the licence holder dies?
The licence usually lapses on the death of the holder. In certain cases, someone connected to them or with an interest in the premises can give an interim authority notice within a short window, which keeps the licence alive for a limited period while a transfer is arranged. Time limits are strict, so act quickly.
What happens if the licence holder becomes insolvent?
A licence held by an individual who becomes bankrupt, or a company that goes into certain insolvency processes, can lapse. Insolvency practitioners and landlords often need to act quickly, using an interim authority notice or a transfer, to protect the licence. A solicitor can explain the options and deadlines.
Can a lapsed licence be saved?
In some cases, yes, if an interim authority notice or a reinstatement transfer is made within the strict time limits. After those limits pass, the licence is usually lost and a new application is needed. If you think a licence may have lapsed, speak to a licensing solicitor straight away.
Do I need to tell the council if my address changes?
Yes. A premises licence holder must notify the council of a change of name or address, and a DPS must notify changes to their own details. There is usually a small fee. Keeping these details up to date avoids problems with notices and correspondence going missing.
What if a licence is surrendered by mistake?
Sometimes a tenant surrenders a licence when leaving, not realising the landlord or a new tenant needs it. A transfer with reinstatement may be possible within a short period after the surrender, if the right person applies. Time limits are strict, so speak to a licensing solicitor immediately if this happens.
How much does a transfer or DPS change cost?
Council fees for transfers and DPS changes are set nationally and are the same wherever the premises is. They change from time to time, so check the current figures with the council or on GOV.UK. Solicitor fees are often fixed for routine applications, with extra charges if there is an objection and a hearing.
Can a solicitor do both applications together?
Yes. It is common to deal with a transfer and a DPS change at the same time, especially on a business sale. Many firms offer a combined fixed fee. Ask what is included, such as checking the licence, preparing the forms and liaising with the sale solicitors.

No. Leaflegal is free for businesses to use. The licensing firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that does not change what you pay them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.
How quickly can this be done?
Transfers and DPS changes can usually be submitted quickly once the forms and consents are ready, and immediate effect means trading can continue. If you have a completion date or a manager leaving soon, mention it on the form so the firm can plan the timing.
Who can apply for a review of my licence?
Any responsible authority, such as the police, environmental health, trading standards, the fire service or the licensing authority, can apply. So can any other person, including a local resident or business. The application must relate to at least one of the licensing objectives. The council can reject applications from other persons that are frivolous, vexatious or repetitive.
What kind of problems lead to reviews?
Common reasons include crime and disorder linked to the premises, noise and nuisance affecting neighbours, sales of alcohol to children, breaches of licence conditions, public safety failings, drug use or dealing and the employment of illegal workers. Many reviews follow a build up of complaints, while some follow a single serious incident.
Will I get warning before a review?
Often there is some warning, such as letters, meetings or action plans from the police or council. But there is no legal requirement for a warning before an application is made, and some reviews follow a single incident. If you are receiving complaints or warnings, a licensing solicitor can help you respond before matters escalate.
Can a resident apply for a review on their own?
Yes. Any person can apply, as long as the grounds relate to the licensing objectives. The council will consider whether the application is frivolous, vexatious or repetitive before accepting it. Reviews brought by residents often concern noise and nuisance, and the outcome depends on the evidence like any other review.
What is a review after a closure order?
If a magistrates' court makes certain closure orders in respect of licensed premises, the council is required to review the licence. This happens automatically and follows its own timetable. Because a closure order means there has already been serious concern, these reviews need careful preparation.
Can I stop a review from going ahead?
Once the council accepts an application, it cannot usually be stopped by the licence holder. The applicant can withdraw it, which sometimes happens if concerns are resolved. More often, the outcome is shaped by agreeing proposals with the applicant and authorities, which can then be presented to the committee at the hearing.
How long does a review take?
There is a 28 day period for representations after the application is advertised. The hearing is then normally held within 20 working days of the end of that period. Summary reviews brought by the police are much quicker, with interim steps considered within 48 hours and a full hearing within 28 days.
Can I keep trading during a review?
Usually yes. On an ordinary review, the licence continues as normal until the decision and, in most cases, until any appeal period has passed. In a summary review, the council can impose interim steps, such as suspension, that take effect immediately. A solicitor can explain the position in your case.
