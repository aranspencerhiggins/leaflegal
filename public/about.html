Legal problems tend to arrive at stressful moments. A house sale, a separation, a court letter or an unpaid invoice all come with deadlines, and the last thing anyone wants is to spend days working out which firm to call.

Legal problems rarely arrive at a convenient time. A house sale falls behind schedule. A relationship ends. A letter arrives from the court or the police. A customer stops paying. In each case there is often a deadline, and the person dealing with it is already under strain.

Finding the right lawyer in that moment can be surprisingly hard. Most people do not know many solicitors, and the ones they know may not do the right kind of work. Searching online brings up a long list of names with little to tell them apart. Calling round takes time, and it often means explaining the same story again and again to people who then say they cannot help.

What we set out to change

We wanted to remove that first round of searching. Instead of you finding the firm, you tell us about your matter once, and we introduce a firm that handles that kind of work. The firm calls you knowing the basics, so the conversation starts in the right place.

We also wanted to be honest about it. Many websites that offer to find you a lawyer are not clear about who runs them, how they are paid or how many firms receive your details. We think people deserve to know exactly who they are dealing with, so we explain all of that on our website in plain words.

Why seven areas

We chose to focus on a small number of areas where people often need a specialist and where a quick introduction can make a real difference. Keeping our focus narrow means we understand the questions to ask and the firms we work with handle that work every day. It also means we do not pretend to help with things we cannot.

What a good introduction looks like

For us, a good introduction is one where the firm calls promptly, already understands the outline of the matter, explains clearly what it can do and what it will cost, and leaves the decision with you. It is not one where you feel rushed or chased. We design the form, the checks and our arrangements with firms around that idea.

We also know that not every enquiry will lead to someone instructing a firm. Some people just want to understand where they stand. Some decide to handle things themselves, or find that the matter resolves on its own. That is fine. Speaking to a regulated firm early can still help people make better decisions, whatever they choose to do next.

Who we have in mind

We write for people who have never dealt with a solicitor before as much as for those who have. That is why our pages use plain words, short sentences and as little jargon as possible. When a legal term cannot be avoided, we try to explain it. Our guides also include a section of common legal terms in plain English, so you can follow what your lawyer is saying.

Our work happens mostly behind the scenes. Here is what we actually do when you use Leaflegal.

We write the form

The questions on the form are designed for each area of law. They are the questions a firm's intake team would usually ask first. That way the firm can see quickly what kind of matter it is, how urgent it is and where you are.

We check each enquiry

We check that each enquiry is complete, that it falls within the areas we cover and that the contact details work. We also check that you have given your consent to be introduced. This helps make sure firms only receive enquiries they can actually help with, and that people only hear from a firm when they have asked to.

We introduce one firm

We pass each enquiry to one regulated firm that handles that type of matter and covers your area. For immigration, it goes to a regulated immigration adviser. We send the firm your answers, your contact details and a record of your consent.

We publish plain guides

Our pages explain in general terms how different kinds of matters usually work in England and Wales, what tends to affect cost and time, and what to have ready. They are not legal advice, but they can help you feel more prepared when you speak to a lawyer.

We listen to feedback

If something goes wrong with an introduction, or if a firm does not treat someone well, we want to know. We use that feedback to improve the service and to decide which firms we continue to work with.

We keep records

We keep a record of each enquiry, the consent given and the firm it was introduced to. That lets us answer questions, deal with complaints and show that introductions were made properly. Our privacy policy explains how long we keep these records and how you can ask to see them.

We keep the website up to date

Legal procedures, court fees, government forms and official guidance change from time to time. We review our pages regularly and update them when something important changes. Where figures change often, such as fees and tax rates, we do not quote them and instead point you to GOV.UK or the firm, so you always get the current position from the right source.

Why we do not give advice

Giving legal advice is a skilled job, and in some areas, such as immigration, it is a criminal offence for anyone who is not regulated to give it. Our role is to make the introduction. The regulated firm we introduce you to is the right place for advice about your situation, because it can look at all the details and it is accountable to its regulator for what it tells you.

Who is responsible for what

Leaflegal is responsible for the introduction itself, for how we handle your information and for the information on our website. The firm is responsible for the advice it gives, the work it does, the fees it charges and how it treats you as a client. If you have a complaint about the introduction, it comes to us. If you have a complaint about the legal service, it goes to the firm first.

Information on our website

Our pages explain in general terms how things usually work in England and Wales, and for immigration, how working with a regulated adviser usually goes. We try hard to keep them accurate and up to date. Laws, fees and procedures do change, though, and general information can never take account of your own circumstances. Always check the position with a regulated firm before you act.

Not a substitute for the firm

We sometimes hear from people who want us to tell them whether they have a good case, whether a quote is fair or what will happen at a hearing. We understand why, but those are questions only a regulated lawyer who knows the facts can answer properly. We will always point you back to the firm, or to another regulated adviser, rather than guess.

We introduce people to independent firms that are regulated for the work they do. For most areas that means firms of solicitors regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. For conveyancing it can also mean firms regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. For immigration it means solicitors or advisers who are regulated to give immigration advice, such as those registered with the Immigration Advice Authority.

How firms join

Firms that want to receive introductions talk to our team first and agree in writing how the arrangement works. They choose the areas of law, types of matter and locations they handle. That helps us introduce each enquiry to a firm that does that kind of work.

  • To contact people promptly and politely
  • To explain their fees clearly before any work starts
  • To tell clients in writing about the fee they pay for the introduction

When things go wrong

If we hear that a firm has not treated people fairly, we take it seriously. We look into what happened and may stop introducing people to that firm. We cannot investigate a firm's legal work, which is a matter for the firm's own complaints procedure and its regulator, but we can and do decide who we work with.

Firms are independent

The firms we introduce are not part of Leaflegal and we are not part of them. Each firm has its own regulator, its own professional indemnity insurance and its own complaints procedure. That independence is important. It means the firm owes its professional duties to you as its client, not to us.

Where the firms are

The firms we work with are based in different parts of England and Wales, and for immigration across the UK. For many matters the firm does not need to be close to you, because most work is done by phone, email and post. Where local knowledge matters, such as a hearing at your local court or an application to your local council, we take that into account.

Leaflegal is free for people and businesses to use. We are paid by the firm we introduce you to. Each firm pays us an agreed fee for the introductions we send. That income covers the cost of running the website, our advertising and the checks we carry out on enquiries.

The fee the firm pays us does not change what the firm charges you. The firm sets its own prices and will explain them before any work starts. Solicitors must tell you in writing about any fee they pay for an introduction, and the fee must not affect the advice they give you.

Keeping things fair

We introduce each enquiry based on the type of matter and where you are, not on which firm pays us the most. We do not rank firms for you or claim that one firm is better than another. You are always free to speak to other firms and to choose who you instruct.

Why the firm pays rather than you

Firms spend money on finding new clients in many ways, such as advertising, directories and websites. Paying for an introduction is one of those ways. Because the firm pays, we can keep the service free for you, and you can find out whether a firm is right for you without spending anything.

More questions people ask

What is conveyancing?
Conveyancing is the legal work involved in transferring the ownership of property, or registering a mortgage against it. It includes checking the title, carrying out searches, preparing or reviewing the contract, handling the money and registering the change at HM Land Registry. It applies to buying, selling, remortgaging and changing who owns a home.
Do I legally have to use a conveyancer?
It is possible to do your own conveyancing in some situations, but it is rare and difficult. If you have a mortgage, your lender will almost always insist that a regulated firm acts for it. Most people use a professional because the legal and financial risks of getting things wrong are high and hard to put right later.
What is the difference between freehold and leasehold?
Freehold means you own the building and the land it stands on with no time limit. Leasehold means you own the right to occupy the property for a set number of years under a lease, with a landlord who owns the freehold. Leasehold property usually comes with ground rent, service charges and extra checks during conveyancing.
What is HM Land Registry?
HM Land Registry keeps the official record of who owns most land and property in England and Wales. After a purchase, remortgage or transfer, your conveyancer applies to update the register. Some older properties are still unregistered, and the first sale or mortgage usually triggers an application for first registration, which takes more work.
What are searches?
Searches are enquiries made to the local authority, water companies and other bodies to find out about things that affect a property but may not be obvious from viewing it. They can reveal planning history, road adoption, drainage, flood risk, environmental issues and mining in some areas. Lenders usually require them when you buy with a mortgage.
When does a sale or purchase become legally binding?
In England and Wales, a sale or purchase only becomes binding when contracts are exchanged. Before that, either side can withdraw without penalty, even late in the process. Once contracts are exchanged, pulling out usually means losing the deposit or facing a claim for losses, so the timing of exchange matters a great deal.
What is the difference between a survey and a valuation?
A mortgage valuation is carried out for the lender to check the property is worth enough to lend against. It is not a detailed inspection and may not tell you about defects. A survey is commissioned by the buyer and looks at the condition of the property in more depth. Your conveyancer deals with the legal side and does not inspect the building, so many buyers arrange a survey as well.
What is a chain?
A chain is a series of linked sales and purchases that depend on each other. If you are selling to someone who is also selling, and you are buying from someone who is buying elsewhere, you are in a chain. Everyone in it usually needs to exchange contracts and complete on the same day, so a delay anywhere can affect everyone else.
Should I use the firm my estate agent recommends?
You are free to choose any firm. Agents sometimes receive a referral fee for recommending a conveyancer, and they should tell you if they do. A recommended firm may be perfectly good, but it is sensible to compare what is on offer and to choose a firm you are comfortable with.
What is the Conveyancing Quality Scheme?
It is an accreditation run by the Law Society for solicitors' firms that meet certain standards in residential conveyancing. Many lenders require their panel firms to hold it. It is one useful sign of a firm's standards, but it is not the only one, and licensed conveyancers have their own regulatory framework.
Does it matter if the firm is local?
Much conveyancing is now done remotely, so a firm does not need to be near the property. A local firm may know common local issues, such as mining areas or flood zones. An online firm may offer longer opening hours and case tracking. Responsiveness and experience usually matter more than distance.
Can I change firms partway through?
Yes, you can move your file to another firm, but there may be charges for work already done, and the change can add delay while the new firm catches up. If you are unhappy, it is usually worth raising your concerns with the firm first and using its complaints process.
What if I am unhappy with my conveyancer?
Every regulated firm must have a complaints process and tell you about it at the start. If the firm does not resolve your complaint, you can usually take it to the Legal Ombudsman. Concerns about professional conduct can go to the Solicitors Regulation Authority or the Council for Licensed Conveyancers.
Will the same person deal with my file throughout?
It depends on the firm. Some assign a named conveyancer who handles the file from start to finish, while others use a team approach with several people involved. Ask how the firm works and how you can get updates, as this often matters more to clients than they expect.
How do I protect myself from email fraud?
Criminals sometimes target property transactions by sending fake emails that appear to come from a conveyancer, asking for money to be sent to a different account. Reputable firms warn clients about this at the start and will not change their bank details by email. Before sending any money, call your firm on a number you already know and confirm the account details.
