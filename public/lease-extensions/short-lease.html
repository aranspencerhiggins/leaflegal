About flats with a short lease

If your lease has fewer than 80 years left, it can be harder to sell or remortgage, and extending it may cost more. Many people only find out when a buyer’s surveyor or a lender raises it.

Short lease in brief

A short lease, often anything under about 80 to 90 years, can make a flat harder to mortgage and sell, and the cost of extending usually rises as the years run down. A lease extension solicitor can explain your options and start the process.

If you are selling a flat with a short lease, you can often serve the section 42 notice and assign the benefit to your buyer, so the extension carries on after the sale.

Flats with a short lease explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Every lease has an end date. When a flat is new, the lease might run for 99 or 125 years and the end seems a long way off. But leases count down every day, and many flats bought decades ago now have far fewer years left than their owners realise. A lease that is getting short can affect the value of the flat, whether a buyer can get a mortgage and how much it costs to put things right.

There is no single legal definition of a short lease. People often use the term for a lease with fewer than 80 or 90 years left, because that is when buyers, lenders and valuers start to pay close attention. A lease with 60 years or less is usually treated as a serious issue, and one with only a few decades left can make a flat very difficult to sell in the ordinary way.

Many owners only discover the problem at an awkward moment. A buyer's surveyor flags it, a lender declines a remortgage, or an executor finds it while sorting out a family member's estate. The good news is that most flat owners have legal options for dealing with a short lease, and a solicitor can explain them clearly.

Common situations

  • An owner planning to sell who has been told the lease is too short for some buyers
  • A remortgage held up because the lender wants more years on the lease
  • A flat inherited with only a few decades left on the lease
  • A buyer who has found a flat they like with a short lease
  • A landlord whose rental flat is losing value as the lease runs down
  • An owner who has received an offer from the freeholder and wants to understand it

The law is changing

The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 makes major changes to how leases are extended, including how the price is calculated. Many of these changes are being brought in over time and some depend on regulations that are still to come. This matters particularly for short leases, because the old rules make them much more expensive to extend. A solicitor will confirm which rules apply to you now.

A short lease affects a flat in several connected ways. Understanding them helps explain why buyers and lenders react the way they do.

Value

A lease is an asset that shrinks over time. As the end date gets closer, the flat is generally worth less than the same flat with a long lease, and the gap tends to widen as the years fall. This is because the owner has fewer years of use left and because of the cost of extending.

Mortgages

Mortgage lenders usually want a minimum number of years left on the lease, often measured from the end of the mortgage term. The exact requirement varies between lenders and changes over time. If a lease falls below a lender's minimum, buyers who need a mortgage from that lender cannot proceed, which reduces the number of possible buyers.

The cost of extending

Under the long standing valuation rules, once a lease has 80 years or fewer left, the freeholder is entitled to a share of the marriage value when the lease is extended. This can add significantly to the premium, and the effect grows as the lease gets shorter. The 2024 Act removes marriage value, but this is part of the reforms being brought in over time, so the 80 year point may still matter for now.

Selling

Buyers' solicitors will report a short lease to their clients, and some buyers will walk away or ask for a lower price. Cash buyers and investors may still be interested, but they usually factor in the cost of extending.

Letting the flat

Landlords with a short lease flat can usually keep letting it, but the asset behind the rent is shrinking. Buy to let lenders often have their own lease length requirements, which can make refinancing harder. Some landlords only notice when they try to sell or remortgage a property they have owned for many years.

When a lease actually ends

If a long residential lease runs out, the law may in some circumstances allow the occupier to stay on as a tenant, but usually paying a market rent and without owning anything they can sell. Very few owners let it get this far, but it shows why the remaining term matters.

There are several ways to deal with a short lease. Which one suits you depends on your plans, your finances, your freeholder and the law at the time. A solicitor will explain them for your own flat. These are the routes most people hear about.

A statutory lease extension

Most flat owners have a legal right to extend their lease. Under the long standing rules this adds 90 years and removes the ground rent, and the 2024 Act provides for a 990 year extension once the relevant changes are in force. The freeholder cannot refuse if you qualify, and the price can be decided by a tribunal if it cannot be agreed. See our statutory lease extension page for more detail.

An informal extension

The freeholder may be willing to agree terms by negotiation. This can be quicker, but the terms are not protected by law and may include a ground rent or other conditions. It is common to have an offer checked before accepting it.

Buying the freehold with neighbours

If enough owners in the building want to act together, they may be able to buy the freehold and then grant themselves long leases. This takes longer to organise but can help a building where several leases are getting short.

Starting an extension and selling

If you are selling, it is often possible to serve a statutory notice and pass the benefit of it to your buyer when the sale completes. The buyer then finishes the extension. This can reassure buyers and lenders without you having to wait for the new lease.

Selling without extending

Some owners sell the flat as it is, usually to a cash buyer or investor, accepting that the price will reflect the short lease. Your solicitor can explain what to expect in the sale process.

Thinking about timing

With a short lease, timing can make a real difference. Under the long standing rules, each year that passes usually makes an extension more expensive, while the reforms are being brought in over a period that is not yet complete. Your solicitor can explain how timing might affect the cost and your plans, but the decision on when to act is yours.

The choice between these routes is yours. Your solicitor will explain the likely costs, timescales and risks of each so that you can decide.

A short lease often comes to light during a sale or a remortgage. Here is how it tends to play out, and what a solicitor can do.

If you are selling

Telling your solicitor and estate agent about the lease length early helps avoid surprises. Your solicitor can obtain the lease and title, check the remaining term and, if appropriate, start the extension process so that a notice is in place when you find a buyer. Some buyers will want the extension completed before they buy, while others are happy to take over a notice.

Passing the notice to your buyer

Where a statutory notice has been served, the benefit of it can usually be transferred to the buyer at the same time as the flat. This has to be done at the right moment and in the right way. Your solicitor and the buyer's solicitor will agree the arrangements, including who pays which costs.

If you are remortgaging

Your new lender may set conditions if the lease is short, or decline to lend. Some lenders will proceed if an extension is under way. Your solicitor and mortgage broker can work together to understand what the lender needs.

If you are buying a flat with a short lease

A buyer can ask the seller to start an extension and pass the notice across, or negotiate a price that reflects the cost of extending. Because the two year ownership rule for the statutory right was removed by the 2024 Act from 31 January 2025, a new owner may be able to start an extension soon after buying, but your solicitor will confirm the position before you rely on it.

Executors and inherited flats

Personal representatives can often start a statutory extension on behalf of an estate within a set period after the grant of probate or letters of administration. This can help when a short lease flat needs to be sold to distribute the estate.

The cost of dealing with a short lease depends on how short it is, which route you choose and the rules in force at the time.

The premium

Under the long standing valuation rules, the premium for extending a short lease includes compensation for lost ground rent, the value of the freeholder's future right to the flat and, below 80 years, a share of marriage value. For very short leases the premium can be a large proportion of the flat's value. The 2024 Act changes the valuation method, but those changes are being brought in over time.

Professional fees

You pay your own solicitor and surveyor. Many firms offer fixed fees for straightforward extensions. A valuation is especially important with a short lease, as small changes in assumptions can make a big difference to the figure.

The freeholder's costs

Under the long standing statutory rules you pay certain reasonable costs of the freeholder. The 2024 Act moves towards each side paying its own costs, with some exceptions, as part of the reforms being introduced over time.

Other costs

Land Registry fees, lender fees and sometimes Stamp Duty Land Tax may apply. Rates and thresholds change, so your solicitor will check what is payable.

Ask for a written estimate that lists the premium range, your own fees, any contribution to the freeholder's costs and the other likely charges. Seeing everything together helps you plan, especially if you need to borrow or if the extension is being paid for from a sale.

Flats with a short lease questions and answers

Is 80 years important?
It has historically affected the cost of extending. The rules are changing, so your solicitor will explain what applies now.
Can I sell a flat with a short lease?
Yes, but some buyers can’t get a mortgage on it. Starting an extension before you sell can help.
Should I extend now or wait?
It depends on your plans and the current rules. A solicitor can explain the pros and cons for your situation.
Which areas do you cover?
Leasehold flats in England and Wales.
Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
What counts as a short lease?
There is no fixed legal definition. Many people treat a lease with fewer than 80 or 90 years left as short, because that is when lenders, buyers and valuers start to pay closer attention. Leases with 60 years or less are usually seen as a significant issue. A solicitor can tell you exactly how many years are left on yours and what that means in practice.
How do I check how long is left on my lease?
Your lease sets out the start date and the original term. Subtract the time that has passed since the start date to find what remains. Note that the start date in the lease is not always the date it was signed. If you do not have the lease, a copy can usually be obtained from HM Land Registry, and a solicitor can check it for you.
Why does 80 years matter?
Under the long standing valuation rules, once a lease has 80 years or fewer left, the freeholder is entitled to half of the marriage value when it is extended. This can make the extension noticeably more expensive. The 2024 Act removes marriage value, but this depends on regulations and is being brought in over time, so a solicitor will confirm whether the 80 year point still affects you.
What happens when a lease runs out completely?
In some circumstances the law allows the occupier of a flat to stay on as a tenant when a long lease ends, but usually paying a market rent and without owning a lease they can sell. The flat's capital value effectively goes back to the freeholder. This is rare, because most owners extend well before it happens.
I inherited a flat with a short lease. What are my options?
Personal representatives can often start a statutory lease extension on behalf of the estate within a set period after the grant of probate or letters of administration. They can also sell the flat as it is, or sell with the benefit of a notice. A solicitor can explain the options and the time limits for the estate.
Does a short lease affect my service charge?
No, the service charge is set by the terms of the lease and the costs of running the building, not by how many years are left. However, if you extend informally, the new lease could change some terms. Under the statutory route, only limited changes to the existing terms are allowed.
Does the ground rent make a short lease worse?
It can. Under the long standing valuation rules, part of the premium compensates the freeholder for losing its ground rent, so a higher or rising ground rent usually means a higher premium. Some short leases also have ground rent terms that worry lenders. A surveyor can explain how your ground rent affects the cost of extending.
Can I extend a lease with very few years left?
Usually, yes. The statutory right to extend does not depend on how many years remain, as long as the lease was originally granted for more than 21 years and you qualify. However, the shorter the lease, the higher the premium tends to be under the long standing rules. A surveyor can give you an idea of the likely cost.
Should I extend now or wait for the reforms?
That depends on your circumstances and is a decision for you. Waiting may mean the lease falls further and becomes more expensive under the current rules, and it may not fit plans to sell or remortgage. The timing and detail of the reforms can also change. A solicitor can explain the factors involved so you can make an informed choice.
My freeholder has offered an extension. Should I accept?
Leaflegal cannot tell you that. A solicitor can review the offer, compare it with the statutory route and point out any terms that might cause problems later, such as a ground rent that rises sharply. A surveyor can also tell you how the price compares. You can then decide whether to accept, negotiate or take another route.
Can my neighbours and I buy the freehold instead?
If the building and enough owners qualify, you may be able to buy the freehold together through collective enfranchisement. You could then grant yourselves long leases. This takes longer to organise and needs enough owners to take part, but it can suit buildings where several leases are getting short.
Can I extend if I have only just bought the flat?
The old requirement to have owned the flat for two years before using the statutory right was removed by the 2024 Act from 31 January 2025. This means a recent buyer may be able to start an extension straight away. Your solicitor will confirm this before serving a notice.
Is there an option to just remove the ground rent?
The 2024 Act includes provisions allowing qualifying leaseholders to pay to reduce their ground rent to a peppercorn without extending the term. This depends on commencement and supporting regulations. For a short lease, extending is often the main concern. Your solicitor will explain whether any ground rent option is available and relevant.
What if my freeholder cannot be found?
Some short lease flats have a freeholder that cannot be traced, for example because a company has been dissolved or an individual owner has died. There is a separate court process that can allow a lease extension to go ahead in that situation, with the price usually decided by the tribunal and paid into court. A solicitor can explain how long it might take.
Can I sell a flat with a short lease?
Yes, but it can be harder. Buyers who need a mortgage may not be able to get one, and others may offer less to reflect the cost of extending. Some owners start a statutory extension and pass the notice to the buyer, which can make the flat more attractive. A solicitor can explain how this would work for your sale.
How does passing a notice to a buyer work?
Once a statutory notice has been served, its benefit can usually be assigned to a buyer at the same time as the flat is transferred. The buyer then takes over the claim and completes the extension. The assignment must be done correctly and at the right time. Both solicitors will agree the details, including who pays which costs.
Why will my lender not remortgage my flat?
Lenders usually require a minimum number of years on the lease, often counted from the end of the mortgage. If your lease is below that minimum, the lender may decline or set conditions. Requirements vary between lenders and change over time. A mortgage broker can explain the lending side, and a solicitor can explain the extension options.
I am buying a flat with a short lease. What can I do?
You can ask the seller to start a statutory extension and assign the notice to you on completion, or negotiate a price that reflects the cost of extending later. Your own solicitor will explain the options, check the lease carefully and tell you whether you are likely to be able to extend once you own the flat.
Do cash buyers buy short lease flats?
Some do, including investors who plan to extend the lease themselves. They usually take the cost and time of extending into account when deciding what to offer. Your estate agent can talk to you about the market, and your solicitor can explain the legal side of selling without extending.
Will a short lease delay my sale?
It can. Buyers' solicitors will raise questions, lenders may set conditions, and some buyers may want the extension completed first. Dealing with the lease early, before or soon after you put the flat on the market, usually reduces delays.
Will a short lease affect a buy to let mortgage?
Buy to let lenders usually have their own requirements about the remaining term of a lease, and these vary between lenders. A short lease may limit the lenders willing to refinance a rental flat. A mortgage broker can explain the lending options, and a solicitor can explain how an extension might change the position.
How much does it cost to extend a short lease?
There is the premium paid to the freeholder, your own solicitor's and surveyor's fees, possibly some of the freeholder's costs and other fees such as Land Registry charges. For a short lease the premium is usually the largest part. A surveyor's valuation is the best way to get a realistic figure for your flat.
How long does an extension take?
Under the statutory route, the freeholder has at least two months to respond to the notice. If the premium is agreed, many extensions complete within a few months of the notice. If the case goes to the tribunal, it can take a year or longer. Your solicitor will give you an estimate based on your freeholder.
Is the premium fixed when the notice is served?
Under the statutory route, the valuation is generally carried out at the date the notice is served. This means the lease does not keep getting more expensive while the claim continues. It is one reason some owners with short leases choose to serve a notice sooner rather than later.
Does Leaflegal charge me?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us for the introduction, and that never changes what you pay them.
Can I borrow to pay for the extension?
Some owners pay from savings and others borrow, for example by arranging further lending from their current lender. With a short lease, some lenders may be cautious until the extension is complete, so it helps to speak to a mortgage broker early. Your solicitor will deal with the legal side of any borrowing linked to the new lease.
Will I have to pay my freeholder's costs?
Under the long standing statutory rules, a leaseholder usually pays certain reasonable legal and valuation costs of the freeholder, but not its tribunal costs. The 2024 Act moves towards each side paying its own costs, with exceptions, as part of the reforms being introduced over time. Your solicitor will confirm which rules apply to your claim.
Who should I speak to first?
Many people start with a solicitor who handles lease extensions, as they can check the lease, explain the options and recommend a specialist surveyor. If you are selling or remortgaging, your estate agent and mortgage broker will also need to know about the lease.
What will the solicitor need from me?
Your lease or the title number, freeholder and managing agent details, recent ground rent demands, any offer from the freeholder, your lender's details and your plans for the flat. Photo ID and proof of address are usually needed too.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm we introduce you to usually gets in touch within one working day, often sooner. If a sale or remortgage depends on the lease, mention your deadline in your enquiry.
Do I have to go ahead once I have spoken to the firm?
No. You can find out your options and what they would cost, and then decide. There is no obligation to instruct the firm.
What is a deed of variation?
It is a legal document that changes the terms of an existing lease. It is signed by the leaseholder and the freeholder, and sometimes by other parties such as a management company. Once completed, it is registered at HM Land Registry so that anyone looking at the title can see the lease and the changes made to it.
Why would my lease need changing?
Common reasons include a wrong or missing plan, unclear repair or insurance clauses, service charge shares that do not add up, missing rights to use shared parts and onerous ground rent terms. Often the problem only comes to light when a buyer's solicitor or a lender reviews the lease. A solicitor will read your lease and explain whether a variation would help.
Can a lease be changed after it has been signed?
Yes. A lease is a contract, and the parties can agree to change it at any time. In some cases a tribunal or court can also order a change. The change must be properly documented and registered to be effective against future buyers and lenders.
Who needs to agree to a variation?
Usually the leaseholder and the freeholder, plus any management company that is a party to the lease. Mortgage lenders on both sides may also need to consent. If the change affects other flats, such as service charge shares, other leaseholders may need to be involved.
Is a lease variation the same as a lease extension?
No. A variation changes the terms of the existing lease, while an extension gives a longer lease. They can be combined, as a new lease on an extension can sometimes fix defects at the same time. Your solicitor can explain whether doing both together would make sense for you.
Can Leaflegal tell me if my lease is defective?
No. Leaflegal does not give legal advice or review leases. We introduce you to a regulated firm, and the solicitor there can read your lease and explain any problems.
Is a licence different from a variation?
Yes. A licence is a one off permission from the freeholder, for example to carry out particular alterations, and it leaves the lease terms unchanged. A variation permanently changes what the lease says. If you only need permission for something specific, a licence may be simpler. Your solicitor will explain which is appropriate.
What is a doubling ground rent?
It is a ground rent that doubles at fixed intervals, such as every ten or fifteen years. Over a long lease this can lead to very high rents. Lenders and buyers are often cautious about these terms. Many owners with doubling ground rents look at varying the lease or extending it to deal with the problem.
