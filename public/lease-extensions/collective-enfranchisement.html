About collective enfranchisement

Collective enfranchisement is the right of qualifying flat owners in a building to buy the freehold together. Once they own it, they can grant themselves longer leases and manage the building themselves.

It needs enough flat owners to take part, usually through a company set up for the purpose, and a careful process with notices and deadlines. A solicitor coordinates the participants, the valuation and the negotiation.

Collective enfranchisement in brief

Collective enfranchisement lets qualifying leaseholders in a building join together to buy the freehold. Once you own the freehold, you can grant yourselves long leases and take control of how the building is managed.

Buying the freehold needs enough leaseholders to take part, a company to hold the freehold and a surveyor’s valuation. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm that handles collective enfranchisement claims.

Collective enfranchisement explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Collective enfranchisement is the legal right of qualifying flat owners in a building to join together and buy the freehold. The right comes mainly from the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993. If the building and enough of the flat owners qualify, the freeholder has to sell. The freeholder can argue about the price and some of the terms, but it cannot simply refuse.

Owning the freehold changes the relationship between the flat owners and their building. Instead of paying a landlord and dealing with a managing agent they did not choose, the owners decide how the building is run, who insures it and how repairs are planned. They can also grant themselves much longer leases, often without ground rent, which can make the flats easier to sell and mortgage.

It is a bigger project than an individual lease extension. Several owners need to agree, organise themselves, set up a company and pool their money. The legal process involves formal notices and strict deadlines, and the price is based on a specialist valuation of the whole building. Most groups use a solicitor to coordinate the claim and a surveyor to value it.

Groups usually start thinking about buying the freehold for one of a few reasons. Some are unhappy with the way the building is managed, the level of service charges or the cost of insurance. Others have several owners whose leases are getting short and want a better long term solution than each person extending on their own. In some buildings the freeholder has simply disappeared or stopped engaging, leaving the owners with nobody to deal with.

Buying the freehold or the Right to Manage

Some groups who mainly want control over management look at the Right to Manage instead. That lets qualifying owners take over the management of the building through their own company without buying the freehold or paying a premium. It does not give anyone a longer lease or remove ground rent. A solicitor can explain how the two options compare for your building.

Why owners choose to buy the freehold

  • To control how the building is managed and maintained
  • To choose their own managing agent or manage it themselves
  • To grant themselves long leases, often at no ground rent
  • To stop paying ground rent to an outside landlord
  • To have more say over insurance, service charges and major works
  • To make the flats more attractive to buyers and lenders

A changing area of law

The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 makes a number of changes to collective enfranchisement, including the rules on mixed use buildings, how the price is calculated and who pays costs. These changes are being brought into force over time, and some depend on regulations that are still to come. Which rules apply can depend on when the claim starts. A solicitor will confirm the position for your building.

Before any group spends time and money, a solicitor will check that the building and the flat owners meet the conditions for the statutory right. The main points are set out below in general terms. The detail matters, so the solicitor will look at your building carefully.

The building

The claim must relate to a self contained building or a self contained part of a building. In most cases this is a block of flats or a house converted into flats. A part of a building can qualify if it is divided vertically from the rest and could be run separately.

Qualifying flat owners

There must be at least two flats held by qualifying tenants, and qualifying tenants must hold at least two thirds of the flats in the building. A qualifying tenant is usually someone who owns a flat on a long lease, which generally means one originally granted for more than 21 years.

Participation

Enough qualifying tenants must take part. Under the current rules, the number taking part must be at least half of the total number of flats in the building. In a building with only two flats, both owners must take part.

Commercial parts

Under the long standing rules, a building does not qualify if more than 25 per cent of its internal floor area is used for non residential purposes, such as shops or offices. The 2024 Act raises this limit to 50 per cent, allowing more mixed use buildings to qualify. This is one of the reforms being brought in over time, so your solicitor will confirm which limit applies.

Resident landlords

Some small converted buildings are excluded where the freeholder, or a member of their family, lives in one of the flats as their main home and the building has only a small number of units. Your solicitor will check whether this exclusion could apply.

Buying the freehold is as much about people as it is about law. Getting the owners organised early, and agreeing how the group will work, can prevent problems later on.

Finding out who is interested

Most claims start with one or two owners talking to their neighbours. It helps to find out how many owners are interested, whether they live in their flats or let them out, and how they would feel about contributing to the cost. Some owners may be overseas or hard to contact, which takes time.

The nominee purchaser

The freehold is bought by a nominee purchaser chosen by the participating owners. In practice this is usually a company set up for the purpose, with the participating owners as members. Your solicitor will help set up the company and explain how decisions will be made.

A participation agreement

Groups usually sign an agreement setting out who is taking part, how much each person will contribute, how costs will be shared, what happens if someone drops out and how the freehold will be run afterwards. This is one of the most useful documents in the whole process, because it reduces the chance of disputes between neighbours.

Sharing the cost

The total cost has to be split between the participating owners. Contributions are often based on the relative value of each flat or on the share of the premium attributable to it, but the group can agree any fair method. Flats with shorter leases may account for more of the premium under the long standing rules.

Owners who do not take part

Owners who do not participate keep their existing leases. After the purchase, the company becomes their landlord. They can usually still extend their own lease later, and the company receives any premium they pay. Your solicitor will explain how non participating flats affect the price and the arrangements afterwards.

Keeping everyone informed

Claims run more smoothly when owners know what is happening. Regular short updates from the group's main contacts, agreed deadlines for providing information and money, and a clear way of making decisions all help. Your solicitor can suggest how other groups have organised themselves.

Each claim is different, but most follow similar stages. The solicitor coordinates the group, the surveyor and the freeholder throughout.

1. Investigation

The solicitor obtains the title to the building and copies of the leases, identifies the freeholder and any other landlords, and checks that the building and the owners qualify. They also look at any land that might be included, such as gardens, car parks or bin stores.

2. Valuation

A specialist surveyor values the freehold and advises on the price to propose. This includes looking at each flat, its lease and any commercial parts.

3. Setting up the group

The nominee company is formed, the participation agreement is signed and the group arranges its funding, so it is ready to pay a deposit if asked and the full price later.

4. The initial notice

The solicitor prepares and serves the formal notice, often called a section 13 notice, on the freeholder. It names the participating owners, describes the property to be bought, proposes a price and gives a date by which the freeholder must reply. That date must be at least two months away. The value is generally fixed at the date of the notice.

5. The counter notice and negotiation

The freeholder can ask for information, require a deposit and inspect the building. It then serves a counter notice accepting or disputing the claim, and may propose a different price or terms. The two sides negotiate, usually through their surveyors.

6. Tribunal, if needed

If terms cannot be agreed, an application can be made to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber). There is a strict window for this after the counter notice, and missing it can end the claim.

7. Completion

Once terms are agreed or decided, the transfer is completed, the price is paid and the nominee company is registered as the new freeholder at HM Land Registry.

Buying the freehold involves a number of costs, which are shared between the participating owners according to their agreement.

The price

Under the long standing rules, the price reflects the value of the freeholder's interest, including ground rents and the right to get the flats back when the leases end, a share of marriage value for participating flats with 80 years or fewer left on their leases, and sometimes compensation for other losses. Any commercial parts or extra land also affect the price. The 2024 Act changes the valuation method, and those changes are being introduced over time. Your surveyor will explain what applies.

Professional fees

The group pays its own solicitor and surveyor. Fees depend on the size of the building and the number of participants. Some firms quote fixed fees for set stages and hourly rates for contested work.

The freeholder's costs

Under the long standing rules, the group pays certain reasonable legal and valuation costs of the freeholder, but not its tribunal costs. The 2024 Act moves towards each side paying its own costs, with exceptions, as part of the reforms being brought in over time.

Company and other costs

There are usually costs for setting up the company, Land Registry fees, and possibly Stamp Duty Land Tax. After completion there are ongoing costs of running the company and the building. Rates and thresholds change, so your solicitor will check what applies.

Buying the freehold is the start of a new responsibility as well as the end of a project. The owners, through their company, now take on the landlord's role.

Granting new leases

Many groups grant each participating owner a new long lease, often 999 years at no ground rent, soon after completion. This usually needs to be done carefully with each owner's mortgage lender. Your solicitor can deal with this as part of the same instruction.

Running the company

The company needs directors, must file accounts and returns at Companies House and must keep proper records. Directors have legal duties, and it helps to have clear rules on how decisions are made.

Managing the building

The company becomes responsible for the landlord's obligations in the leases, such as insuring and repairing the building and collecting service charges. Some groups appoint a managing agent, while others manage the building themselves. Either way, the leases and the law on service charges still have to be followed.

Selling a flat in the future

When an owner sells, the buyer's solicitor will want to see the company documents and the share or membership will usually pass to the buyer. Keeping the company records in good order makes future sales smoother.

Insurance and major works

The company will usually need to arrange buildings insurance from completion and plan for future repairs. Many groups set up a reserve fund, if the leases allow it, so that large costs such as a new roof can be met without sudden bills. Major works still need to follow the consultation rules for service charges.

Timing

The fewer years left, the more an extension usually costs. Many owners start the process well before the lease falls to 80 years.

Costs

Collective enfranchisement questions and answers

How many flat owners need to take part?
There are minimum participation rules. Your solicitor will check them for your building.
What happens after we buy the freehold?
The participating owners control the freehold, can extend their own leases and decide how the building is managed.
Do all owners have to pay the same?
Not always. Contributions are usually agreed between participants, often based on each flat’s value.
Which areas do you cover?
Leasehold flats in England and Wales.
Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
How many flat owners need to take part?
Under the current rules, the number of participating qualifying tenants must be at least half of the total number of flats in the building. In a building with just two flats, both owners must take part. Your solicitor will count the flats and check who qualifies, because getting the numbers wrong can make the notice invalid.
Does our building have to be purpose built?
No. Converted houses and purpose built blocks can both qualify, as long as the building is self contained and meets the other conditions. Some small converted buildings with a resident landlord are excluded, so your solicitor will check whether that applies to your building.
We have shops on the ground floor. Can we still buy the freehold?
Possibly. Under the long standing rules, a building does not qualify if more than 25 per cent of its internal floor area is non residential. The 2024 Act raises this to 50 per cent, and that change is being brought in over time. Your solicitor and surveyor will measure the commercial parts and confirm which limit applies.
Do owners who let out their flats count?
Yes. Owners who let their flats to tenants can usually be qualifying tenants and can take part. What matters is that they hold a long lease of the flat. An owner who lives elsewhere, even abroad, can still participate.
Can we include the garden or car park?
Often, yes. Land and facilities that the flat owners are entitled to use under their leases, such as gardens, car parks and bin stores, can usually be included in the claim. Your solicitor will check the title plans and the leases to identify what can be bought.
What if one owner holds several flats?
Ownership of several flats can affect who counts as a qualifying tenant under the rules, and the position has been changed over time. Your solicitor will look at who owns each flat and how that affects the numbers.
What is a resident landlord exclusion?
Some small buildings are excluded from collective enfranchisement where the building is a conversion with only a few units and the freeholder, or an adult member of their family, has lived in one of the flats as their main home for a qualifying period. The rules are detailed, and your solicitor will check whether they apply before the group spends money on the claim.
Do all the owners have to agree?
No. Only the required number of qualifying owners need to take part. Owners who do not want to join can stay out, keep their current leases and have the company as their landlord afterwards. It is still worth involving as many as possible, as this spreads the cost.
How do we decide who pays what?
The group agrees this between themselves, usually in a participation agreement. Common methods include sharing according to the value each flat adds to the price or by floor area. Flats with shorter leases can account for a larger share under the long standing valuation rules. Your solicitor can explain the options but cannot choose for you.
What if someone drops out part way through?
This is one of the main risks in a group claim. A good participation agreement sets out what happens, for example whether a departing owner forfeits money already paid and whether someone else can take their place. If numbers fall below the minimum, the claim may be affected, so it is important to plan for this.
Do we need to set up a company?
The freehold is bought by a nominee purchaser. In practice this is nearly always a company owned by the participating owners, which makes ownership and future sales much simpler. Your solicitor will usually form the company as part of the work.
Can one solicitor act for all of us?
Usually, yes. One firm normally acts for the nominee company and the participating owners as a group. If the interests of some owners conflict, for example over how costs are shared, the firm may suggest that some take separate advice.
How do we get everyone's information together?
Your solicitor will usually send a questionnaire to each participant asking for their details, lease information and mortgage lender. Appointing one or two owners as the main contacts for the group can make communication much easier.
Should we look at the Right to Manage instead?
The Right to Manage lets qualifying owners take over management of the building without buying the freehold. It can suit groups whose main concern is how the building is run. Buying the freehold gives ownership and the ability to grant long leases, but costs more. Your solicitor can explain both options so the group can decide.
What is a section 13 notice?
It is the formal initial notice that starts a collective enfranchisement claim. It identifies the building and any extra land, names the participating owners, proposes a price and gives the freeholder a date to reply that is at least two months away. It has to be accurate and served correctly, so it is normally prepared by a solicitor.
What can the freeholder do after receiving our notice?
The freeholder can ask for information about the participants, require a deposit and arrange access so its surveyor can value the building. It then serves a counter notice accepting the claim, disputing it, or proposing a different price and terms. In some cases it can ask to lease back certain parts, such as commercial units.
What if we cannot agree the price?
Either side can apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to decide the terms. The application must be made within a fixed period after the counter notice. Many claims still settle before a hearing, but the application keeps the claim alive while negotiations continue.
How long does it take?
Organising the group and the funding can take several months before any notice is served. After that, if the price is agreed, completion often follows within several months. A contested claim that goes to the tribunal can take a year or more. Your solicitor will give you an estimate once they understand your building.
What if our freeholder cannot be found?
Where a freeholder cannot be traced, there is a separate court process that can allow the owners to buy the freehold without the freeholder's involvement. The price is usually set by the tribunal and paid into court. It takes longer, but it can resolve a building that has been left without a landlord.
What is a leaseback?
In some cases the freeholder can require the new owners to grant it a lease of certain parts, such as commercial units or flats not held by qualifying tenants. This can reduce the price. The 2024 Act also introduces new rules allowing leaseholders to require leasebacks, as part of the reforms being brought in over time.
Can we withdraw once the notice is served?
The group can usually withdraw, but under the long standing rules it may have to pay certain costs the freeholder has incurred up to that point, and there is normally a waiting period before a new notice can be served. The participation agreement should say how such costs would be shared. Your solicitor will explain the consequences before any decision is taken.
How much will it cost to buy our freehold?
The total cost includes the price paid to the freeholder, the group's own legal and valuation fees, possibly some of the freeholder's costs, company set up costs and Land Registry fees. It depends heavily on the building, the leases and the rules in force at the time. A surveyor's report is usually the first step to understanding the likely price.
Do flats with longer leases pay less?
Under the long standing valuation rules, flats with longer leases generally contribute less to the price, because there is less marriage value and the freeholder's interest in them is worth less. The 2024 Act removes marriage value as part of the reforms being introduced over time. How the cost is split between owners is for the group to agree.
Will we pay the freeholder's costs?
Under the long standing rules, the group pays certain reasonable legal and valuation costs of the freeholder, but not tribunal costs. The 2024 Act moves towards each side generally paying its own costs. Your solicitor will confirm which rules apply when the notice is served.
Can individual owners borrow to pay their share?
Some owners use savings and others borrow, for example by remortgaging. Each owner's lender may also need to be involved when new leases are granted. A mortgage broker can talk through the borrowing side, and your solicitor will deal with the legal requirements.
Does Leaflegal charge us?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, which does not change what you pay them. There is no obligation to instruct the firm.
Do we have to pay a deposit?
After the initial notice is served, the freeholder can ask for a deposit. The amount is set by the rules and is credited towards the price on completion. The group should have funds ready before the notice is served, so the deposit can be paid without delay if it is requested.
Can we extend our leases once we own the freehold?
Yes. Once the company owns the freehold, it can grant new long leases to the owners, often 999 years at no ground rent. This is usually done soon after completion with each owner's lender involved. Owners who did not participate can usually still extend their leases under the statutory route, with the company as their landlord.
Who manages the building after the purchase?
The company takes over the landlord's role. It can appoint a managing agent or manage the building through its own directors. Either way, it must follow the leases and the law on service charges, insurance and major works.
What responsibilities do the directors have?
Directors must run the company properly, file accounts and confirmation statements at Companies House, keep records and act in the interests of the company. They also oversee the landlord's obligations under the leases. Some groups take advice on company matters or appoint a company secretary to help.
What happens when an owner sells their flat?
The buyer usually takes over the seller's share or membership of the company along with the flat. The buyer's solicitor will ask for company documents and details of the management of the building. Good records make this straightforward.
Do we still have to follow service charge rules?
Yes. Once the company owns the freehold, it is the landlord and must follow the leases and the law on service charges. This includes consulting the flat owners before certain major works or long term contracts. Owning the freehold together does not remove these protections, which apply to participating and non participating owners alike.
What is a share of freehold?
People often describe a flat as having a share of freehold when the owner holds a long lease and is also a member or shareholder of the company that owns the freehold. It is still a leasehold flat, but the owners collectively control the freehold. Buyers and lenders are usually familiar with this arrangement.
What counts as a short lease?
There is no fixed legal definition. Many people treat a lease with fewer than 80 or 90 years left as short, because that is when lenders, buyers and valuers start to pay closer attention. Leases with 60 years or less are usually seen as a significant issue. A solicitor can tell you exactly how many years are left on yours and what that means in practice.
How do I check how long is left on my lease?
Your lease sets out the start date and the original term. Subtract the time that has passed since the start date to find what remains. Note that the start date in the lease is not always the date it was signed. If you do not have the lease, a copy can usually be obtained from HM Land Registry, and a solicitor can check it for you.
Why does 80 years matter?
Under the long standing valuation rules, once a lease has 80 years or fewer left, the freeholder is entitled to half of the marriage value when it is extended. This can make the extension noticeably more expensive. The 2024 Act removes marriage value, but this depends on regulations and is being brought in over time, so a solicitor will confirm whether the 80 year point still affects you.
What happens when a lease runs out completely?
In some circumstances the law allows the occupier of a flat to stay on as a tenant when a long lease ends, but usually paying a market rent and without owning a lease they can sell. The flat's capital value effectively goes back to the freeholder. This is rare, because most owners extend well before it happens.
I inherited a flat with a short lease. What are my options?
Personal representatives can often start a statutory lease extension on behalf of the estate within a set period after the grant of probate or letters of administration. They can also sell the flat as it is, or sell with the benefit of a notice. A solicitor can explain the options and the time limits for the estate.
Does a short lease affect my service charge?
No, the service charge is set by the terms of the lease and the costs of running the building, not by how many years are left. However, if you extend informally, the new lease could change some terms. Under the statutory route, only limited changes to the existing terms are allowed.
Does the ground rent make a short lease worse?
It can. Under the long standing valuation rules, part of the premium compensates the freeholder for losing its ground rent, so a higher or rising ground rent usually means a higher premium. Some short leases also have ground rent terms that worry lenders. A surveyor can explain how your ground rent affects the cost of extending.
Can I extend a lease with very few years left?
Usually, yes. The statutory right to extend does not depend on how many years remain, as long as the lease was originally granted for more than 21 years and you qualify. However, the shorter the lease, the higher the premium tends to be under the long standing rules. A surveyor can give you an idea of the likely cost.
