About collective enfranchisement

Collective enfranchisement is the right of qualifying flat owners in a building to buy the freehold together. Once they own it, they can grant themselves longer leases and manage the building themselves.

It needs enough flat owners to take part, usually through a company set up for the purpose, and a careful process with notices and deadlines. A solicitor coordinates the participants, the valuation and the negotiation.

A specialist leasehold solicitor checks your lease, explains your options and deals with the freeholder for you, working alongside a surveyor who values the premium. When we introduce you, the firm will explain the likely timescale and costs for your particular lease.

Fees and costs

A lease extension usually involves two professionals. A solicitor handles the legal process, and a surveyor values the premium you pay the freeholder. Each will quote separately, and many solicitors work with surveyors they can recommend.

At present, if you use the statutory route you usually also pay the freeholder’s reasonable legal and valuation costs. The law on leasehold is changing, so ask the firm which rules apply to your lease and how that affects the overall cost.

Leaflegal is free for you to use. The firm pays us a fee for the introduction, and they will tell you about it in writing.

Questions to ask the firm

  • Do you recommend the statutory route or an informal deal for my lease?
  • Can you recommend a surveyor, and how do your fees fit together?
  • What costs might I have to pay the freeholder?
  • How long is the process likely to take?
  • What happens if the freeholder can’t be found or doesn’t reply?

When to get help

  • When your lease drops towards 90 years
  • Before you put the flat on the market
  • Before you remortgage, as lenders look closely at lease length
  • If you have received a notice from your freeholder

Things to know

Timing

The fewer years left, the more an extension usually costs. Many owners start the process well before the lease falls to 80 years.

Costs

As well as legal fees, there is usually a premium to pay the freeholder and a surveyor’s valuation. The law in this area is changing, so ask the firm how recent reforms affect you.

Statutory or informal

A statutory extension follows a set legal process. An informal deal with the freeholder can be quicker but may come with less favourable terms.

Collective enfranchisement questions and answers

How many flat owners need to take part?
There are minimum participation rules. Your solicitor will check them for your building.
What happens after we buy the freehold?
The participating owners control the freehold, can extend their own leases and decide how the building is managed.
Do all owners have to pay the same?
Not always. Contributions are usually agreed between participants, often based on each flat’s value.
Which areas do you cover?
Leasehold flats in England and Wales.
Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
