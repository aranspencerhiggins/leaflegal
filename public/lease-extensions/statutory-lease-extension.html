About statutory lease extensions

Many flat owners have a legal right to extend their lease, using a formal process with set steps and deadlines. It starts with a notice to the freeholder, which the freeholder must respond to, and any disagreement about the premium can go to a tribunal.

The law on leasehold is changing, so the right process and the way the premium is worked out depend on when you start. A solicitor will check what applies to you, work with a surveyor on the valuation and serve the notices correctly.

A specialist leasehold solicitor checks your lease, explains your options and deals with the freeholder for you, working alongside a surveyor who values the premium. When we introduce you, the firm will explain the likely timescale and costs for your particular lease.

Statutory lease extension in brief

A statutory lease extension uses your legal right as a qualifying leaseholder to add 90 years to the lease, usually with ground rent reduced to zero. The process starts when your solicitor serves a section 42 notice on the freeholder, which sets out your proposed premium.

Strict deadlines follow the section 42 notice, so you need a lease extension solicitor and a surveyor working together. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm that handles statutory lease extensions regularly.

Statutory lease extensions explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A statutory lease extension uses a legal right that most flat owners in England and Wales have, rather than relying on the freeholder's goodwill. The right comes from the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993. If you qualify, the freeholder cannot refuse to grant you a new lease. They can only argue about the price and some of the terms, and if you cannot agree, an independent tribunal decides.

Under the long standing rules, a statutory extension adds 90 years to whatever is left on your current lease and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn, which in practice means you pay none. So a lease with 70 years left would become a lease with 160 years left and no ground rent. The rest of the lease usually stays much the same, with only limited changes allowed.

The process is formal. It starts with a notice that must contain specific information and be served on the right people. From that point, both sides have to follow a timetable set by law. Missing a deadline can end the claim, which is why the process is usually run by a solicitor with a specialist surveyor alongside.

For most people, the aim is to protect the value of their home. A longer lease generally makes a flat easier to sell and easier to mortgage, and removing ground rent can make it more attractive to buyers. Some people extend because they plan to sell soon, others because a lender has raised the lease length on a remortgage, and some simply want peace of mind before the lease gets any shorter.

Why people choose the statutory route

  • The freeholder cannot refuse if you qualify
  • The terms of the new lease are protected by law
  • Ground rent usually falls to nothing for the new lease
  • Disputes about price can go to an independent tribunal
  • The benefit of a notice can usually pass to a buyer if you sell

A changing area of law

The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 changes the statutory right in several ways, including a longer standard extension of 990 years, a new way of calculating the premium and new rules on who pays costs. These changes are being brought in over time, and some depend on regulations that are still to come. Which rules apply to your claim can depend on when it starts. A solicitor will confirm the position for you.

Most people who own a flat on a long lease qualify, but there are some conditions and exceptions. A solicitor will check these at the start, because serving a notice when you do not qualify wastes time and money.

A long lease

The lease usually needs to have been granted for a term of more than 21 years when it was first created. Most flat leases easily meet this, as they are typically granted for 99, 125 or 999 years. What matters is the original length, not how much is left now.

Ownership

Under the old rules, you had to have been the registered owner for at least two years before you could serve a notice. The 2024 Act removed this requirement from 31 January 2025. This means many new owners can start straight away, although your solicitor will confirm this before serving anything.

Who the landlord is

Some landlords are partly or wholly exempt. Examples can include certain charitable housing trusts, some properties owned by the National Trust and some Crown land. Leases granted by certain bodies or for business purposes can also be treated differently. If your freeholder is unusual, your solicitor will check whether this affects your right.

When the owner has died

If a leaseholder dies, their personal representatives can usually serve a notice on behalf of the estate within a set period after the grant of probate or letters of administration. This can be useful if the flat is going to be sold. A solicitor will check the time limits.

Flats over shops and mixed buildings

The right to extend a lease applies to flats, including flats in buildings with shops or offices. The rules for buying the freehold of a mixed use building are stricter, but they do not usually stop an individual flat owner from extending their own lease.

Each claim is different, but most statutory extensions follow the same broad stages. Your solicitor will tell you exactly what applies and what you need to do.

Before you start

It is worth thinking about your plans for the flat before any notice is served. If you expect to sell or remortgage in the next year or so, tell your solicitor, as it can affect the timetable and whether the notice should be passed to a buyer. It also helps to know how you will pay the premium, especially if you need to borrow.

1. First review

Your solicitor checks your lease, your title and the freeholder's details. They confirm that you qualify and identify everyone who needs to receive the notice, which can include an intermediate landlord or a management company named in the lease.

2. Valuation

A specialist surveyor values the lease extension and advises on the figure to put in the notice. Many leaseholders propose a figure towards the lower end of the reasonable range, as the starting point for negotiation, but it must be a realistic figure. Your solicitor and surveyor will discuss this with you.

3. Serving the notice

The solicitor prepares the notice, often called a section 42 notice, and serves it on the freeholder and any other landlord. The notice sets out your details, the flat, the premium you propose and the date by which the freeholder must reply. That date must be at least two months after the notice is given. The value of the extension is generally fixed at the date the notice is served.

4. The freeholder's response

After receiving the notice, the freeholder can ask for evidence that you own the flat and can require a deposit. They can also ask to inspect the flat so their own surveyor can value it. They then serve a counter notice, which either accepts your proposals or puts forward different ones.

5. Negotiation

Most claims are settled by negotiation between the surveyors, with the solicitors dealing with the wording of the new lease. If the premium or terms cannot be agreed, an application can be made to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber). This must be made within a strict window after the counter notice, and missing it can mean the claim is treated as withdrawn.

6. Completing the new lease

Once terms are agreed or decided, the new lease is prepared, signed and completed. You pay the premium and any costs due, and your solicitor registers the new lease at HM Land Registry. There are also deadlines at this stage, and your solicitor will make sure completion happens in time.

The premium is the price you pay the freeholder for your new lease. It is often the biggest cost, so it helps to understand what affects it. Your surveyor will explain the figures for your own flat.

What affects the premium under the long standing rules

  • The value of the flat with its current lease
  • The value it would have with a longer lease
  • How many years are left on the current lease
  • The ground rent you pay now, and how it rises
  • Whether marriage value applies
  • Market evidence from similar flats and settlements

Ground rent and the freeholder's future interest

Part of the premium compensates the freeholder for the ground rent they will no longer receive. Another part reflects the value of getting the flat back when the lease ends, which becomes more valuable as the end date gets closer. A ground rent that rises sharply can add noticeably to the premium.

Marriage value

Under the long standing rules, where a lease has 80 years or fewer remaining, the freeholder is also entitled to half of the marriage value. This is the increase in value created by extending the lease. It is the main reason extensions can become much more expensive once a lease falls below 80 years.

How the 2024 Act changes valuation

The 2024 Act provides for marriage value to be removed and for certain rates used in the calculation to be set by the government rather than argued over case by case. These valuation changes depend on regulations and are part of the reforms being brought in over time. Your surveyor and solicitor will confirm which method applies when you serve your notice.

Why the surveyor matters

Leasehold valuation is a specialist skill. A surveyor who regularly works on lease extensions will know the market evidence in your area and how tribunals have approached similar cases. Their report also helps your solicitor decide what to say in the notice and how far to negotiate.

A statutory extension usually involves four kinds of cost. Your solicitor will give you an estimate before you commit.

The premium

This is paid to the freeholder, and sometimes partly to an intermediate landlord. It depends on the valuation and the rules that apply at the time.

Your solicitor and surveyor

Many firms charge a fixed fee for the legal work on a straightforward extension, with extra charges if it becomes contested. Surveyors usually charge for their valuation report, with further charges for negotiation or tribunal work.

The freeholder's costs

Under the long standing rules, you pay the freeholder's reasonable legal and valuation costs of dealing with your claim, but not their costs of going to the tribunal. The 2024 Act changes this so that each side usually pays its own costs, with some exceptions. As with other reforms, this is being brought in over time, so your solicitor will tell you which rules apply.

Other costs

These can include Land Registry fees, a fee charged by your mortgage lender, and Stamp Duty Land Tax in some cases where the premium is large. Rates and thresholds change, so check with your solicitor.

It is a good idea to ask your solicitor for a written estimate that sets out each of these costs separately. That makes it easier to compare firms and to see what might change if the claim becomes contested.

Statutory extensions often go smoothly, but some issues come up again and again. Knowing about them helps explain why the paperwork matters.

Notices with mistakes

A notice that leaves out required information, names the wrong landlord or is served at the wrong address can be challenged. Some mistakes can be corrected, but others mean starting again. If a notice is withdrawn or treated as withdrawn, there is usually a wait of 12 months before another can be served.

Missed deadlines

Both sides have deadlines. If the freeholder does not serve a counter notice in time, the leaseholder may be able to apply to court for a new lease on the terms in their notice. If the leaseholder misses the tribunal or completion deadlines, the claim can be treated as withdrawn.

Freeholders who cannot be found

Where a freeholder cannot be traced, there is a separate court process to deal with the claim. It takes longer, but it can still lead to a new lease.

Intermediate landlords

Some buildings have a head lease between the freeholder and the flat owners. In that case the premium may be split between different landlords, and more parties are involved in the negotiation.

Proposed changes to lease terms

Freeholders sometimes try to add new terms to the extended lease. Only limited changes are allowed under the statutory route, and your solicitor will push back on terms that are not permitted.

Redevelopment

In rare cases, where a lease is close to its end and the freeholder intends to redevelop, the freeholder may be able to oppose the claim. Your solicitor will tell you if this could be relevant.

Funding the premium late

Some leaseholders reach the point of completion before they have arranged the money for the premium. Because there are deadlines for completing the new lease, a delay in funding can put the claim at risk. Speaking to your lender or broker early, if you need to borrow, avoids a last minute rush.

Statutory lease extensions questions and answers

What is a section 42 notice?
It is the formal notice a leaseholder serves on the freeholder to claim a statutory lease extension. It proposes a premium and starts a timetable for the freeholder’s response.
Why extend my lease?
As a lease gets shorter, the flat can become harder to sell or mortgage and more expensive to extend. Extending protects the value of your home.
How long does a statutory extension take?
Often several months, longer if the premium has to go to a tribunal. Starting before you plan to sell gives you time.
Do I pay the freeholder’s costs?
Usually you pay certain reasonable costs of the freeholder as well as your own. Your solicitor will explain the current rules.
Which areas do you cover?
Leasehold flats in England and Wales.
Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Do I need to have owned my flat for two years?
Not any more. The two year ownership requirement used to apply, but the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025. This means many people can start the process soon after buying. Your solicitor will confirm that the requirement does not apply to you before serving a notice.
Does it matter how long my lease has left?
You can usually extend however many years remain, as long as the lease was originally granted for more than 21 years. However, the number of years left affects the price under the long standing valuation rules. A lease with fewer than 80 years left can cost noticeably more to extend because marriage value applies.
Can I extend if I sublet my flat?
Letting your flat to a tenant does not usually stop you using the statutory right. You remain the leaseholder, and you can still serve a notice. Your solicitor may need to know about the tenancy, as the surveyor might need access to value the flat.
Can I extend if the flat is owned through a company?
In many cases, yes. The rules about company owners have changed over time and depend on the circumstances. Your solicitor will check whether a company owner qualifies before any notice is served.
My flat is above a shop. Can I still extend?
Usually, yes. The right to extend is available to qualifying flat owners whether or not the building contains commercial units. The stricter rules about commercial space mainly affect buying the freehold of the whole building rather than extending an individual lease.
Can I extend if I own the share of freehold?
If you already own a share of the freehold, you may be able to grant yourself a longer lease by agreement with the other freeholders, which is often simpler than the statutory route. A solicitor will look at how your freehold is held and explain the options.
What if there are two landlords?
Some flats have a head lease between the freeholder and the flat owner, so there are two or more landlords. The statutory process deals with this by involving a competent landlord who runs the claim, with the premium split between the different landlords. Your solicitor will identify everyone involved.
What is in a section 42 notice?
The notice includes details of the leaseholder and landlord, a description of the flat, details of the lease, the premium the leaseholder proposes and any other amounts, the terms proposed for the new lease, and the date by which the landlord must reply. The details must be accurate, and your solicitor will check them against the lease and the title before serving it.
How long does the freeholder have to respond?
The notice states a date by which the freeholder must serve a counter notice. That date must be at least two months after the notice is given. In the meantime, the freeholder can ask for proof of title, ask for a deposit and arrange to inspect the flat for valuation.
What if the freeholder does not reply?
If no counter notice is served in time, the leaseholder can usually apply to the county court for an order that the new lease is granted on the terms in their notice. There is a deadline for this application too. Your solicitor will act quickly if the freeholder misses its date.
What happens if we cannot agree the price?
Either side can apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) once two months have passed after the counter notice. The application must be made within a fixed window, or the claim is treated as withdrawn. Most cases still settle before a hearing, but the application protects the claim while negotiations continue.
Can I withdraw my notice?
Yes, a leaseholder can withdraw. However, you would usually have to pay certain of the freeholder's costs incurred up to that point under the rules that apply, and you normally cannot serve another notice for 12 months. Your solicitor will explain the consequences before you decide.
Is the premium fixed when I serve my notice?
The valuation is generally carried out as at the date the notice is served. This is one reason the timing of the notice matters, as later changes in the market or in the number of years left do not usually affect the figure. Your surveyor will explain how this applies to you.
Does the freeholder get to inspect my flat?
After receiving the notice, the freeholder is usually entitled to reasonable access so its surveyor can value the flat. Your solicitor will help arrange a suitable time. Refusing reasonable access can cause problems with the claim, so it is best to cooperate with a properly arranged visit.
Do I have to pay a deposit?
The freeholder can ask for a deposit after receiving your notice. The amount is set by the rules and relates to the premium you proposed. It is usually held by the freeholder's solicitor and credited towards the price on completion. Your solicitor will tell you if one is requested and how much it is.
How is the premium worked out?
Under the long standing rules, the premium reflects the loss of ground rent to the freeholder, the value of the freeholder's right to get the flat back when the lease ends and, for leases with 80 years or fewer, half of the marriage value. A specialist surveyor works out these figures using market evidence. The 2024 Act changes the method over time.
Why is 80 years such an important figure?
Under the long standing valuation rules, marriage value only applies once a lease has 80 years or fewer remaining. Crossing that line can add a large amount to the premium. The 2024 Act removes marriage value, but this is part of the reforms still being brought in. Your surveyor can explain whether 80 years matters for you.
Can I negotiate the premium?
Yes. Most statutory claims settle by negotiation between the two surveyors. Your surveyor will use market evidence and their experience to argue for a fair figure. If agreement is not possible, the tribunal can decide.
Does my ground rent affect the price?
Yes. The freeholder is giving up its future ground rent, so the higher it is, and the faster it rises, the more the freeholder can claim. Ground rents that double regularly can have a noticeable effect on the premium.
Will I pay my freeholder's costs?
Under the long standing rules, the leaseholder pays certain reasonable legal and valuation costs of the freeholder, but not tribunal costs. The 2024 Act moves towards each side paying its own costs, with exceptions. Your solicitor will confirm the position that applies on the date your claim starts.
Is the premium paid all at once?
Yes, the premium is normally paid in full on completion of the new lease, together with any costs payable. If you are borrowing to pay it, your lender will need to be involved early so the money is ready in time.
How does the 2024 Act change statutory extensions?
Among other things, it provides for a standard extension of 990 years for flats, removes marriage value, sets some valuation rates by regulation, removed the two year ownership rule from 31 January 2025 and generally makes each side pay its own costs. Some of these are already in force and others depend on further regulations. A solicitor will confirm what applies now.
Will I get 90 years or 990 years?
Under the long standing rules, a statutory extension adds 90 years. The 2024 Act provides for 990 years instead. Which applies to you depends on when the relevant part of the Act comes into force and on the transitional rules. Your solicitor will tell you which length applies when your notice is served.
Should I wait until all the reforms are in force?
Some people wait and others go ahead. Waiting means the lease gets shorter, which can affect the price under the current rules and may not fit plans to sell or remortgage. The timing of the reforms can also move. A solicitor can explain the factors to weigh up, but the decision is yours.
Can I extend more than once?
Yes. Under the statutory route you can usually extend again in future, although with a 990 year extension that is unlikely to be needed. Your solicitor can explain how a second extension would work if you already have a statutory lease.
Can I just get rid of my ground rent?
The 2024 Act includes provisions allowing qualifying leaseholders to pay to reduce their ground rent to a peppercorn without extending the term. Like many parts of the Act, this depends on commencement and supporting regulations. A solicitor will tell you whether this option is available yet and how it would compare with a full extension.
What if I started my claim under the old rules?
Claims that are already under way may continue under the rules that applied when the notice was served, depending on the transitional provisions. In some cases there may be a choice to make. If you have already served a notice, ask your solicitor how the reforms affect your claim before taking any further step.
Why use a solicitor rather than doing it myself?
The notices, deadlines and lease terms are technical, and mistakes can be expensive. A solicitor who does this regularly will make sure the notice is valid, the deadlines are met and the new lease is drafted and registered correctly. The firm will also coordinate with your surveyor and your lender.
What will the solicitor ask me for?
Expect to be asked for your lease or title number, details of the freeholder and any managing agent, recent ground rent and service charge demands, your lender's details and your plans for the flat. Photo ID and proof of address are also usually needed.
Will the solicitor find me a surveyor?
Many firms work with specialist leasehold surveyors and can suggest one, but you are free to choose your own. The surveyor should have experience of lease extension valuations in your area.
Does my mortgage lender need to know?
Usually, yes. Your lender has a charge over the lease, so it normally needs to be told about the new lease and may need to consent. Your solicitor will contact the lender, deal with any conditions and register the new lease with the lender's charge in place.
How long will the whole process take?
If the premium is agreed without a dispute, many extensions complete within a few months of the notice being served. If a tribunal application is needed, it can take a year or longer. Your solicitor will give you a realistic estimate once they know your freeholder.

What is an informal lease extension?
It is a new or longer lease agreed by negotiation between you and your freeholder, rather than through the statutory process set out in the 1993 Act. The terms are whatever you both agree. Once the deal is signed, the new lease is registered at HM Land Registry like any other lease.
Is an informal extension legally binding?
Once the new lease or deed of variation has been signed and completed, it is legally binding in the same way as any other lease. Before that point, either side can usually walk away. Heads of terms and letters agreeing the main points are not normally binding on their own.
Who can offer an informal extension?
Any freeholder can offer one, and any leaseholder can ask. Some large freeholders send out offers when leases reach a certain length. Smaller freeholders may agree one if asked. There is no obligation on the freeholder to offer or accept an informal deal.
Why would a freeholder offer one?
Freeholders have their own reasons. An informal deal can save them time and the uncertainty of a statutory claim, and it may allow them to keep a ground rent or add terms they prefer. Knowing this can help you understand why an offer looks the way it does.
Do I qualify for an informal extension?
There are no qualifying conditions in the same way as for the statutory right. If the freeholder is willing, an informal extension can usually be agreed. This is one reason it was sometimes used by owners who had not yet owned their flat for two years, a requirement that used to apply to the statutory route until the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025.
Is an informal extension the same as a deed of variation?
Sometimes. An informal extension can be done by granting a brand new lease or by varying the existing lease to add years. Your solicitor will explain which method is proposed and how it affects things like ground rent rules and registration.
Is an informal extension common?
Yes, many lease extensions are agreed informally, especially where the freeholder is a small landlord or a company owned by the flat owners. Larger freeholders also agree informal deals, sometimes on standard terms. Whether it is the right route for you depends on the offer and your plans, which is something a solicitor can talk through with you.
My freeholder has written to me with an offer. What now?
Many people ask a solicitor to review the offer before replying. They can explain what the terms mean, how they compare with the statutory route and whether anything might cause problems later. A surveyor can also give a view on value. You do not have to accept, and you do not have to reply straight away.
