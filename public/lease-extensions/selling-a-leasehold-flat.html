Selling a leasehold flat means providing the buyer with the lease, a management pack and answers about service charges and works. If the lease is short or has unusual terms, buyers and lenders will ask about it.

Selling a leasehold flat explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Selling a leasehold flat involves everything that goes into selling a house, plus an extra layer of paperwork about the lease and the building. Your buyer is not just buying four walls. They are taking over your lease, with all its rights and obligations, and becoming part of the arrangements for running the building. Their solicitor and their lender will want to understand all of that before they commit.

That is why leasehold sales often take longer than freehold ones. Much of the information the buyer needs does not come from you. It comes from the freeholder, the managing agent or a residents' management company, and it can take time to arrive. Buyers' solicitors also ask more questions about leasehold flats, especially about service charges, major works, building safety and the length of the lease.

The good news is that a lot of this can be prepared in advance. A solicitor who regularly handles leasehold sales will know what the buyer's side is likely to ask for and can start gathering it early, so it is ready when you accept an offer.

Who is involved

A typical leasehold sale involves more people than a freehold one. As well as you, your buyer and the two solicitors, there is usually a freeholder, often a managing agent, sometimes a residents' management company named in the lease, and the buyer's mortgage lender. Each of them can affect how quickly things move. Part of your solicitor's job is to keep all of these moving in the same direction.

What buyers' solicitors usually focus on

  • How many years are left on the lease
  • The ground rent and how it can change
  • The service charge, recent accounts and any arrears
  • Planned or ongoing major works and how they will be paid for
  • Building safety information, especially for taller buildings
  • Whether alterations have been made with the freeholder's consent
  • Any restrictions on letting, pets or use
  • Any disputes with the freeholder, managing agent or neighbours

The law is changing

The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 includes measures aimed at making leasehold sales smoother, including rules on the information freeholders and managing agents provide and what they can charge for it. These changes are being brought in over time and depend on further regulations. A solicitor will confirm which rules apply when you sell.

Your solicitor prepares a contract pack that is sent to the buyer's solicitor once you accept an offer. For a leasehold flat it usually includes the items below. Starting early helps, as some of them take time to obtain.

Title and lease

Your solicitor obtains official copies of your title and the lease from HM Land Registry, along with any deeds of variation, previous lease extensions or other documents that change the lease.

Property information forms

You complete the standard property information form and a leasehold information form, giving details about the flat, the building, the freeholder, the managing agent, service charges and any disputes or works. You also complete a fittings and contents form. Answer as fully and accurately as you can, as the buyer relies on what you say.

The management pack

This is information from the freeholder, managing agent or management company. It usually covers service charge budgets and accounts, the reserve fund, buildings insurance, planned major works, ground rent, any arrears and the process for registering the buyer as the new owner. There is usually a fee, and it can take some time to arrive.

Building safety information

For some buildings, particularly taller ones, buyers' solicitors will ask about fire safety, cladding and the information required under the building safety rules. This can include a leaseholder deed of certificate and the landlord's certificate. Your solicitor will tell you what applies to your building.

Consents and certificates

If you have made alterations, such as removing a wall or replacing windows, the buyer will want to see the freeholder's consent where the lease requires it, as well as building regulations approval. If you have let the flat, the buyer may want evidence that any consent needed for letting was obtained.

Letting and use

If the flat has been let, the buyer will want to know whether the tenancy will end before completion or whether they are buying with a tenant in place. Leases often contain rules about letting, short term holiday lets and running a business from the flat. Buyers' solicitors check that these rules have been followed.

If your lease is getting short, it is likely to affect your sale. Buyers who need a mortgage may struggle, and buyers' solicitors will raise it with their clients. Dealing with it early gives you more options.

Sellers are often surprised to learn how many years are actually left. Leases are usually described by their original length, such as 99 years, but what matters to a buyer is what remains today. Your solicitor can confirm the exact figure from the lease at the start of the sale.

How a short lease affects a sale

Lenders usually require a minimum number of years left on the lease, and the exact figure varies between lenders and over time. Below that level, buyers who need a mortgage may not be able to proceed. Buyers who can proceed may offer less to reflect the cost of extending.

Extending before you sell

Completing a lease extension before marketing the flat removes the issue, but it takes time. Under the statutory route, the freeholder has at least two months to respond to the notice, and agreeing the premium can take longer. Your solicitor will explain the likely timescale.

Starting an extension and passing it on

Many sellers serve a statutory notice and assign the benefit of it to their buyer on completion. The buyer then finishes the extension. This can reassure buyers and lenders, and the price can reflect the arrangement. It needs careful coordination between the two solicitors. Find out more on our short lease page.

Selling as it is

Some sellers choose to sell without extending, often to cash buyers or investors. Your estate agent can talk to you about the market and your solicitor can explain the legal side.

The 2024 Act is changing how extensions are valued and how long they last, as part of reforms being brought in over time. Your solicitor will explain how this affects your options. Which route to take is your decision.

Several payments relating to the lease are dealt with around completion. Your solicitor will explain each of them and include them in the completion statement.

Service charge and ground rent

Service charges and ground rent are usually paid in advance for a set period. On completion, they are normally split between you and the buyer up to the completion date, so you pay for your period of ownership and the buyer pays for theirs. This is called an apportionment.

Retentions

Service charge accounts are often finalised after the year ends, which can lead to a balancing charge or credit. Buyers' solicitors sometimes ask for part of the sale money to be held back to cover this, or to cover major works that have been agreed but not yet billed. Your solicitor will negotiate any retention.

Arrears

Any unpaid service charge or ground rent is usually paid from the sale proceeds on completion. If you dispute a charge, tell your solicitor early, as it can affect the sale.

Freeholder fees after completion

The buyer's solicitor usually has to give notice of the sale to the freeholder or managing agent, and some leases require a deed of covenant or a share transfer in a management company. There are often fees for these, and who pays them is agreed between the parties.

Paying off your mortgage

Your solicitor obtains a redemption figure from your lender and pays off your mortgage from the sale money on completion, then sends you the balance after deducting agreed costs.

Your final figures

Before completion, your solicitor sends you a completion statement showing the sale price, the mortgage redemption, the estate agent's fee, the legal fees and disbursements, any apportionments and any retention. Checking it carefully, and asking about anything you do not understand, avoids surprises on the day.

Leasehold sales can be held up by things outside the seller's control. Knowing the usual causes helps you plan.

Slow management information

The single most common delay is waiting for the management pack. Ordering it as soon as you accept an offer, or even earlier, helps. Your solicitor will chase the managing agent.

Major works

If major works are planned or under way, buyers want to know the likely cost and who will pay. Uncertainty about the bill can lead to long negotiations over price or a retention.

Building safety questions

Questions about cladding, fire safety or the building safety certificates can delay a sale while information is obtained. Your solicitor will tell you what is likely to be asked for your building.

Missing consents

Alterations made without the freeholder's consent can cause delay. Sometimes retrospective consent can be obtained, and sometimes indemnity insurance is offered instead.

Problems in the lease

A defect in the lease, such as a wrong plan or an onerous ground rent, may need a variation before some buyers or lenders will proceed.

The chain

As with any sale, delays elsewhere in the chain affect everyone. Your solicitor will keep you updated.

How to keep things moving

  • Instruct a solicitor early and order the management pack promptly
  • Find any guarantees, consents and building regulations certificates for work you have done
  • Complete the property forms fully and return them quickly
  • Tell your solicitor about any disputes, arrears or planned works straight away
  • Reply to questions from your solicitor as soon as you can

Selling a leasehold flat usually costs more in legal fees than selling a freehold house, because there is more work involved.

Legal fees

Many firms quote a fixed fee for a leasehold sale, often with a supplement compared with a freehold sale. Ask what is included and what might cost extra, such as dealing with a lease extension or a variation alongside the sale.

Management pack fees

The freeholder or managing agent usually charges for providing the management information. The 2024 Act includes provisions about these charges, which are being introduced over time. Your solicitor will tell you the fee once it is quoted.

Other costs

There may be Land Registry fees for copy documents, fees for any consents, indemnity insurance premiums, and your estate agent's fee. If you extend your lease, there are separate costs for that.

If your sale also involves a lease extension, a variation or retrospective consent for works, ask for those costs to be shown separately. This makes it clearer what each part of the work costs and helps when you are comparing quotes.

A lease extension usually involves two professionals. A solicitor handles the legal process, and a surveyor values the premium you pay the freeholder. Each will quote separately, and many solicitors work with surveyors they can recommend.

At present, if you use the statutory route you usually also pay the freeholder’s reasonable legal and valuation costs. The law on leasehold is changing, so ask the firm which rules apply to your lease and how that affects the overall cost.

Things to know

Timing

The fewer years left, the more an extension usually costs. Many owners start the process well before the lease falls to 80 years.

Costs

As well as legal fees, there is usually a premium to pay the freeholder and a surveyor’s valuation. The law in this area is changing, so ask the firm how recent reforms affect you.

Statutory or informal

A statutory extension follows a set legal process. An informal deal with the freeholder can be quicker but may come with less favourable terms.

Selling a leasehold flat questions and answers

Why do leasehold sales take longer?
The buyer needs information from the freeholder or managing agent, which can take time to arrive.
Should I extend before selling?
It can make the flat easier to sell. A solicitor can start the process and pass the benefit to your buyer.
Who pays for the management pack?
Usually the seller. Your solicitor will order it.
Which areas do you cover?
Leasehold flats in England and Wales.
Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
When should I instruct a solicitor to sell my flat?
Many sellers instruct a solicitor when they put the flat on the market, or even before. This allows the solicitor to obtain the title and lease, check the remaining term and order the management pack early. With a leasehold flat, an early start can save weeks once you accept an offer.
What is a management pack?
It is information from the freeholder, managing agent or management company about the building and your share of its costs. It usually includes service charge accounts and budgets, insurance details, planned works, ground rent and any arrears. The buyer's solicitor needs it to advise the buyer. There is usually a fee for it.
Who pays for the management pack?
In most sales the seller pays, as the information is needed to sell the flat. The 2024 Act includes provisions about what can be charged for leasehold information, which are being brought in over time. Your solicitor will order the pack and tell you the cost.
What forms will I need to fill in?
Usually the standard property information form, a leasehold information form and a fittings and contents form. These ask about the flat, the building, service charges, disputes and any changes you have made. Your solicitor will send them to you and help with any questions.
What if I cannot find my lease?
Most leases are registered at HM Land Registry, and your solicitor can obtain a copy. Older leases are not always held in full, and your solicitor will explain how to deal with that if it happens. If you have other documents, such as a previous deed of variation or a lease extension, these are useful too. Your solicitor will check that everything affecting the lease is included in the contract pack so the buyer's solicitor has a complete picture.
Do I need to tell the buyer about disputes with neighbours or the freeholder?
The property information forms ask about disputes, and you must answer honestly. Giving incorrect or incomplete answers can lead to claims after the sale. If you are unsure how to answer a question, ask your solicitor.
Can I sell a flat I have let to a tenant?
Yes. You can sell with the tenant in place to an investor, or arrange for the tenancy to end before completion if the buyer wants vacant possession. Ending a tenancy has to follow the correct legal process and can take time. Your solicitor will explain how the tenancy affects the sale, and a letting agent or housing specialist can help with the tenancy itself.
Should I extend my lease before selling?
That depends on how long is left, your timescale and the market. Some sellers extend first, some start the statutory process and pass the notice to the buyer, and some sell without extending. A solicitor can explain the pros and cons of each for your flat, and the decision is yours.
Can I pass a lease extension to my buyer?
Under the statutory route, once a notice has been served, its benefit can usually be assigned to the buyer at the same time as the sale completes. The buyer then takes over the claim. This needs to be done in the right order and form, so both solicitors will coordinate it.
My lease has a doubling ground rent. Will that affect my sale?
It can. Some lenders and buyers are cautious about ground rents that double at fixed intervals. Some freeholders have agreed to vary these terms. Your solicitor can explain whether a variation might be needed and how it could be arranged alongside the sale.
I made alterations without the freeholder's consent. What happens?
Tell your solicitor early. Depending on the lease and the work done, it may be possible to get retrospective consent from the freeholder, or indemnity insurance may be offered. Building regulations approval may also be needed. Leaving it until the buyer's solicitor asks can cause delays.
What if my flat has a share of the freehold?
The buyer will usually take over your share or membership of the freehold company. The buyer's solicitor will ask for company documents, such as share certificates, accounts and details of the directors. Having these ready can speed up the sale.
Does the length of my lease affect who can buy?
Yes. Buyers who need a mortgage are limited by their lender's minimum lease requirements, which vary between lenders. Cash buyers are not limited in the same way, but they usually take the lease length into account in their offer.
My lease has a restriction on selling. What does that mean?
Some leases require the freeholder's consent before the flat is sold, or require the buyer to sign a deed of covenant or join a management company. These are usually routine steps with a fee rather than real obstacles. Your solicitor will check the lease and tell the buyer's solicitor what will be needed.
How are service charges dealt with on completion?
Service charges paid in advance are usually split between seller and buyer up to the completion date. Your solicitor works out the figures and includes them on the completion statement. Arrears are normally paid from the sale proceeds. Because service charge years do not always match the date you sell, the figures can look complicated. Your solicitor will explain how they have been worked out and whether any amount is being held back until the accounts are finalised.
What is a service charge retention?
It is money held back from the sale proceeds on completion to cover a possible balancing charge for the current service charge year, or a known future cost. It is released once the final figures are known. The amount is negotiated between the solicitors.
There are major works planned. Can I still sell?
Yes, but buyers will want to know the likely cost and who will pay. If you have already been billed, the buyer may ask you to pay before completion. If not, they may ask for a price reduction or a retention. Your solicitor will negotiate on your behalf.
What is a section 20 notice?
It is a notice the landlord must give leaseholders when consulting on major works or certain long term contracts above a set level. Buyers' solicitors ask about any notices served, as they suggest future costs. Tell your solicitor if you have received one.
What building safety information will the buyer ask for?
For some buildings, especially taller ones, buyers' solicitors will ask about fire safety, cladding and the certificates and information required under the building safety rules. This can include a leaseholder deed of certificate. Your solicitor will tell you what applies and help you provide it.
What if I dispute a service charge?
Tell your solicitor as soon as possible. An unresolved dispute can affect the sale, as the buyer will want to know whether they could inherit a bill. Your solicitor will explain how it is likely to be handled during the sale.
What happens to the reserve fund when I sell?
Money paid into a reserve fund usually belongs to the fund for the building, not to individual owners, so it normally stays with the building when you sell. Buyers often see a healthy reserve fund as a positive sign. Your solicitor will check what the lease and the management pack say about it.
Why do leasehold sales take longer?
The buyer needs information from the freeholder and managing agent, which can take time to arrive, and buyers' solicitors ask more questions about leasehold flats. Issues such as a short lease, major works or building safety can add further time. Preparing early helps reduce delays.
How much will my solicitor charge for a leasehold sale?
Many firms charge a fixed fee, often with a leasehold supplement. Management pack fees, Land Registry fees and any consent or insurance costs are usually extra. Ask for a written quote that sets out everything that is included.
Who pays the freeholder's fees for registering the buyer?
Fees for giving notice of the sale, deeds of covenant and similar steps are usually dealt with by the buyer's solicitor, but who pays them can be agreed between buyer and seller. Your solicitor will explain the position in your sale. Some leases set out these fees, and the management pack often lists them, so they can usually be identified early in the sale.

No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us for the introduction, and that never changes what you pay them.
Will the 2024 Act change how leasehold sales work?
The Act includes measures about the information provided on leasehold sales and the fees that can be charged, among other things. These are being brought in over time and depend on regulations. Your solicitor will tell you which rules apply when you sell.
How long does a leasehold sale usually take?
It varies a great deal. A simple sale with a responsive managing agent and a short chain can move quickly. A sale involving a short lease, major works, building safety questions or a long chain can take months. Your solicitor will give you a realistic estimate once they have seen the lease and know who manages the building.
Can I reduce the cost of selling my flat?
Preparing early and providing information promptly can help avoid extra work that might be charged for. Getting several quotes and checking exactly what each includes also helps. Some costs, such as the management pack fee, are set by third parties, so your solicitor will tell you which items are fixed and which may vary.

How quickly will the firm contact me?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day, often sooner. If you already have a buyer or a deadline, mention it in your enquiry. The firm will want to understand your timescales, whether you already have a buyer and whether there are any lease issues it should know about from the start.
Do I have to instruct the firm?
No. Speaking to the firm does not commit you to anything. You are free to decide whether to go ahead. You can ask questions, get a quote and compare it with other options before deciding.
Is Leaflegal a law firm?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service. We do not give legal advice or handle sales ourselves. We introduce you to one regulated firm that deals with leasehold sales, and all advice comes from that firm.
Do I need to have owned my flat for two years?
Not any more. The two year ownership requirement used to apply, but the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025. This means many people can start the process soon after buying. Your solicitor will confirm that the requirement does not apply to you before serving a notice.
Does it matter how long my lease has left?
You can usually extend however many years remain, as long as the lease was originally granted for more than 21 years. However, the number of years left affects the price under the long standing valuation rules. A lease with fewer than 80 years left can cost noticeably more to extend because marriage value applies.
Can I extend if I sublet my flat?
Letting your flat to a tenant does not usually stop you using the statutory right. You remain the leaseholder, and you can still serve a notice. Your solicitor may need to know about the tenancy, as the surveyor might need access to value the flat.
Can I extend if the flat is owned through a company?
In many cases, yes. The rules about company owners have changed over time and depend on the circumstances. Your solicitor will check whether a company owner qualifies before any notice is served.
My flat is above a shop. Can I still extend?
Usually, yes. The right to extend is available to qualifying flat owners whether or not the building contains commercial units. The stricter rules about commercial space mainly affect buying the freehold of the whole building rather than extending an individual lease.
Can I extend if I own the share of freehold?
If you already own a share of the freehold, you may be able to grant yourself a longer lease by agreement with the other freeholders, which is often simpler than the statutory route. A solicitor will look at how your freehold is held and explain the options.
What if there are two landlords?
Some flats have a head lease between the freeholder and the flat owner, so there are two or more landlords. The statutory process deals with this by involving a competent landlord who runs the claim, with the premium split between the different landlords. Your solicitor will identify everyone involved.
What is in a section 42 notice?
The notice includes details of the leaseholder and landlord, a description of the flat, details of the lease, the premium the leaseholder proposes and any other amounts, the terms proposed for the new lease, and the date by which the landlord must reply. The details must be accurate, and your solicitor will check them against the lease and the title before serving it.
