Informal lease extensions explained

Pick a topic to read more.

An informal lease extension is a new or longer lease agreed directly between you and your freeholder, outside the formal statutory process. Instead of serving a legal notice and following a set timetable, the two sides simply negotiate. If they reach agreement, solicitors draw up the paperwork and the new lease is registered in the usual way.

Informal extensions are sometimes called voluntary or non statutory extensions. They can be started by either side. Some freeholders write to leaseholders offering an extension, often when a lease is getting shorter. Other times a leaseholder asks the freeholder what terms they would offer before deciding which route to take.

The big difference is that nothing in an informal deal is guaranteed by law. The length of the new lease, the ground rent, the price and any other changes to the lease are whatever you agree. That can work well when the freeholder is reasonable and the terms are fair. It can also lead to problems if the new lease contains terms that put off buyers and mortgage lenders later on.

Informal deals are common in buildings where the freeholder is a small landlord, a family company or a company owned by the flat owners themselves. They are also offered by some larger investment freeholders, who may have standard terms. In each case, the starting point is the same. Before you agree anything, it helps to understand exactly what you are being offered and what you would be giving up compared with the statutory route.

Why people consider the informal route

  • It can sometimes be quicker, as there are no statutory waiting periods
  • The freeholder may offer a price that is attractive compared with a formal claim
  • It can be combined with other changes the leaseholder wants, such as permission for alterations
  • It can suit buildings where the freeholder and leaseholders already have a good relationship
  • It can be an option where the statutory right is not available for some reason

A changing legal background

Leasehold law is being reformed. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 changes the statutory right to extend, including the length of a statutory extension and the way the premium is calculated, and these changes are being brought in over time. Because an informal offer is usually measured against what the statutory route would give you, the reforms can affect whether an offer looks good value. A solicitor will explain what applies at the time you are deciding.

It helps to see the informal route side by side with the statutory one. These are general differences. Your solicitor will explain how they apply to your lease.

Certainty

Under the statutory route, a qualifying leaseholder has a legal right to a new lease on terms set by law, and the freeholder cannot refuse. Under the informal route, the freeholder can say no, change its offer or withdraw at any point before the deal is signed.

Length of the new lease

A statutory extension under the long standing rules adds 90 years to the remaining term, and the 2024 Act provides for 990 years once the relevant changes are in force. An informal extension can be any length the two sides agree. Some offers extend the lease to a round figure such as 99 or 125 years from today, which may add fewer years than a statutory claim would.

Ground rent

A statutory extension reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn. An informal offer might keep a ground rent, increase it or include a review clause. Separate rules introduced in 2022 limit ground rent on many new residential leases, and they can affect the extra years added by a voluntary extension. Your solicitor will check how they apply to an offer.

Other lease terms

The statutory route only allows limited changes to the existing lease terms. An informal deal can change anything that both sides agree to change, which can be helpful or unhelpful depending on what is proposed.

Disputes

Under the statutory route, an independent tribunal can decide the premium if you cannot agree. There is no tribunal for an informal deal. If you cannot reach agreement, the negotiation simply ends, although you may still be able to use the statutory right.

Passing it to a buyer

A statutory notice can usually be passed to a buyer if you sell part way through. An informal offer is personal to the negotiation, and a buyer may not be able to rely on it.

Costs

Under the long standing statutory rules, the leaseholder pays certain reasonable costs of the freeholder, and the 2024 Act is moving towards each side paying its own. In an informal deal there are no set rules, so who pays what is simply part of the bargain. It is worth asking for any costs contribution to be agreed as a fixed sum at the outset.

If your freeholder has made an offer, it is common to have a solicitor look at it before replying. Offers can look simple in a letter but have significant effects once written into a lease. These are the kinds of things a solicitor is likely to check.

The number of years added

Work out how many years the new lease adds to what you already have, not just the total. An offer of a 125 year lease starting today may add less than it first seems.

The ground rent

Look at the starting ground rent and how it changes over time. Ground rents that double at regular intervals, or that rise in line with inflation without a cap, can cause problems with lenders and buyers. Your solicitor will compare the proposed rent with the rules that apply to new leases.

Changes to other terms

Some offers include new obligations, such as extra charges, restrictions on letting or keeping pets, or new costs for consents. Others take the chance to tidy up an old lease, which can be a benefit. Your solicitor will explain each change.

The premium

A specialist surveyor can advise on what the statutory route might cost, so you have something to compare the offer with. A lower premium may not be better value if it comes with a ground rent or a shorter extension.

Conditions and costs

Check whether the offer depends on paying the freeholder's legal fees, whether it has a deadline and whether it is subject to further approval. Freeholders sometimes ask for a contribution towards their costs, and the amount can often be negotiated.

How the offer fits your plans

An offer that suits someone planning to stay for decades may not suit someone selling next year, and the reverse is also true. Your solicitor will ask about your plans, such as a sale, a remortgage, letting the flat or passing it on in a will, so they can explain how the offer might affect each of these.

Your solicitor will give you their view of the offer and the alternatives. The decision whether to accept is yours.

There is no fixed timetable for an informal extension. Most follow a similar path, though, and your solicitor will guide you through each stage.

1. Getting or asking for an offer

Either the freeholder approaches you, or you or your solicitor ask the freeholder whether it would agree an extension and on what terms. Some larger freeholders have standard terms for informal deals.

2. Taking advice

Your solicitor reviews the offer and the existing lease. A surveyor may value the extension under the statutory rules so you can compare. You decide whether to negotiate, accept or consider the statutory route instead.

3. Agreeing heads of terms

Once the main points are agreed, they are usually written down as heads of terms. These set out the length, ground rent, premium, costs and any other changes. They are not usually binding but they guide the drafting.

4. Drafting the new lease

The freeholder's solicitor usually prepares the new lease or a deed of variation. Your solicitor checks it carefully against what was agreed and against the existing lease, and raises any concerns.

5. Lender consent

If you have a mortgage, your lender usually needs to agree to the new lease. Your solicitor contacts the lender and deals with any conditions it sets.

6. Completion and registration

The documents are signed, the premium and any agreed costs are paid, and your solicitor registers the new lease at HM Land Registry. Until registration is complete, a buyer or lender may still see the old lease on the title.

How long it takes

A straightforward informal extension with a responsive freeholder can sometimes be completed in a matter of weeks once terms are agreed. Negotiations that go back and forth, or freeholders that take a long time to reply, can stretch this to several months. Your solicitor will keep you updated and tell you if the statutory route might give more certainty on timing.

The costs of an informal extension depend on what is agreed. These are the main items to plan for.

The premium

This is whatever you and the freeholder agree. It may be more or less than a statutory premium would be, and it should be compared with the other terms on offer, not looked at in isolation.

Your own legal fees

Many firms charge a fixed fee to review an offer and complete an informal extension. Some charge separately for the initial review, which can be useful if you are not yet sure whether to go ahead.

Valuation advice

A surveyor's report is optional for an informal deal, but many people find it helpful to know what the statutory route might cost before they agree a price.

The freeholder's costs

There are no fixed rules on costs for an informal extension. The freeholder may ask you to pay its legal and valuation fees, and this forms part of the negotiation.

Other costs

There may be Land Registry fees, a fee from your mortgage lender and, in some cases, Stamp Duty Land Tax. Rates and thresholds change, so your solicitor will check what applies.

Ask your solicitor to set out each of these items in writing. Seeing the premium, the fees and any contribution to the freeholder's costs together gives a clearer picture of the total cost of the deal and makes it easier to compare with the statutory route.

Informal extensions can be a sensible choice, but some issues come up often enough that they are worth knowing about in advance.

Onerous ground rent

A new lease with a ground rent that doubles regularly, or rises steeply, can make a flat harder to sell or mortgage. Some leaseholders who accepted informal extensions in the past later needed to vary their lease again to deal with this.

Short extensions

An extension that only takes the lease to around 99 years may need to be extended again sooner than expected, and the cost of doing so later can be higher.

Pressure to decide

Some offers come with a short deadline. A deadline may be genuine, but it is still sensible to take time to understand what you are signing. Your solicitor can ask for more time if needed.

Losing the statutory protections

Agreeing informally means giving up the protections of the statutory process for that deal, such as fixed terms and the tribunal. It does not usually stop you using the statutory right in future, but you would be starting from the new lease you agreed.

Using both routes

Some leaseholders serve a statutory notice first and then negotiate. This keeps the legal protections in place while leaving room to agree terms. Your solicitor will explain whether this approach suits your situation.

Missing documents

Occasionally an informal extension is agreed by letter but the new lease is never properly completed or registered. Years later, a buyer's solicitor finds that the title still shows the old lease. Making sure the deal is completed and registered at HM Land Registry avoids this kind of problem.

Informal lease extensions questions and answers

Is an informal extension a good idea?
It can be, if the terms are fair. Your solicitor will tell you what to look out for, such as ground rent increases.
Can I switch to the statutory route?

Will my lender need to know?

What is an informal lease extension?
It is a new or longer lease agreed by negotiation between you and your freeholder, rather than through the statutory process set out in the 1993 Act. The terms are whatever you both agree. Once the deal is signed, the new lease is registered at HM Land Registry like any other lease.
Is an informal extension legally binding?
Once the new lease or deed of variation has been signed and completed, it is legally binding in the same way as any other lease. Before that point, either side can usually walk away. Heads of terms and letters agreeing the main points are not normally binding on their own.
Who can offer an informal extension?
Any freeholder can offer one, and any leaseholder can ask. Some large freeholders send out offers when leases reach a certain length. Smaller freeholders may agree one if asked. There is no obligation on the freeholder to offer or accept an informal deal.
Why would a freeholder offer one?
Freeholders have their own reasons. An informal deal can save them time and the uncertainty of a statutory claim, and it may allow them to keep a ground rent or add terms they prefer. Knowing this can help you understand why an offer looks the way it does.
Do I qualify for an informal extension?
There are no qualifying conditions in the same way as for the statutory right. If the freeholder is willing, an informal extension can usually be agreed. This is one reason it was sometimes used by owners who had not yet owned their flat for two years, a requirement that used to apply to the statutory route until the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025.
Is an informal extension the same as a deed of variation?
Sometimes. An informal extension can be done by granting a brand new lease or by varying the existing lease to add years. Your solicitor will explain which method is proposed and how it affects things like ground rent rules and registration.
Is an informal extension common?
Yes, many lease extensions are agreed informally, especially where the freeholder is a small landlord or a company owned by the flat owners. Larger freeholders also agree informal deals, sometimes on standard terms. Whether it is the right route for you depends on the offer and your plans, which is something a solicitor can talk through with you.
My freeholder has written to me with an offer. What now?
Many people ask a solicitor to review the offer before replying. They can explain what the terms mean, how they compare with the statutory route and whether anything might cause problems later. A surveyor can also give a view on value. You do not have to accept, and you do not have to reply straight away.
What ground rent terms should I look out for?
Rents that double at fixed intervals, rents linked to inflation without a cap, and rents that are high compared with the value of the flat can all cause concern for lenders and buyers. Rules introduced in 2022 also limit ground rent on many new leases. Your solicitor will check the proposed rent against these rules.
Can the freeholder add new terms to my lease?
In an informal deal, the freeholder can propose any changes, and you can accept, reject or negotiate them. This is different from the statutory route, where only limited changes are allowed. Your solicitor will point out any new terms and explain their effect.
Can I ask for changes too?
Yes. An informal negotiation works both ways. Leaseholders sometimes ask for defects in the lease to be fixed, for permission for alterations or for clearer service charge terms. Whether the freeholder agrees is a matter for negotiation.
How long should the new lease be?
That is for you and the freeholder to agree. It helps to compare the offer with what the statutory route would give, which is 90 years added under the long standing rules and 990 years under the 2024 Act once in force. Your solicitor can explain how the length could affect value and future plans.
Can my freeholder withdraw its offer?
Yes, until the deal is completed, the freeholder can usually withdraw or change its offer. If certainty matters, for example because of a sale, your solicitor may discuss whether serving a statutory notice would give more protection.
What is a rent review clause?
A rent review clause allows the ground rent to change during the lease, for example by doubling at fixed intervals or by rising with an inflation index. Some reviews are capped and others are not. Lenders and buyers look closely at these clauses, because they can make the rent much higher in future. Your solicitor will explain how any review clause in an offer would work over the life of the new lease.
What happens to my service charge?
An informal extension does not usually change how the service charge works unless the new lease says so. However, some offers update the service charge clauses or add new charges. Your solicitor will compare the old and new wording so that you know whether anything you pay towards the building would change as a result of the deal.
Is the informal route cheaper than the statutory route?
Sometimes, but not always. The premium may be lower, but other terms, such as a continuing ground rent or a shorter extension, may make the overall deal worse value. Comparing the two needs a valuation and a careful look at the terms. Your solicitor and surveyor can help you weigh them up.
Is the informal route quicker?
It can be, because there are no statutory waiting periods. In practice it depends on how quickly the freeholder responds and how long it takes to agree terms. A freeholder that is slow to reply can make an informal deal take longer than a statutory one.
Can I still use the statutory route if talks fail?
Usually, yes, as long as you qualify. Negotiating informally does not normally stop you serving a statutory notice later. Your solicitor will watch for anything in the correspondence that might affect this.
Can I serve a statutory notice and still negotiate?
Yes. Many leaseholders serve a notice and then agree terms by negotiation within the statutory framework. This keeps the protections of the statutory process in place while leaving room for agreement. Your solicitor will explain the pros and cons.
How do the 2024 reforms affect informal offers?
The reforms change the statutory route, including the length of the new lease and how the premium is worked out, and they are being brought in over time. Because an informal offer is usually compared with the statutory alternative, the reforms can change how an offer looks. A solicitor will explain the position at the time you are deciding.
Can I pass an informal offer to a buyer?
Usually not in the same way as a statutory notice, which can be assigned to a buyer on a sale. An informal offer depends on the freeholder's agreement. If you are selling, your solicitor will explain which route is more practical given your timescales.
Why do some people prefer the informal route?
Some leaseholders value a quicker, less formal process, especially where they get on well with the freeholder. Others are offered a price they consider attractive, or want to make other changes to the lease at the same time. The right choice depends on the terms and your circumstances, and a solicitor will help you understand the trade offs before you decide.
Will a buyer's lender accept an informal extension?
Lenders look at the lease as it stands, whatever route was used to extend it. What matters is usually the length, the ground rent and any unusual terms. A well drafted informal extension with a reasonable ground rent should not cause difficulty, but onerous terms can. Your solicitor can explain how lenders tend to view the terms in an offer.
What will an informal extension cost me?
There is the premium you agree, your own solicitor's fees, any valuation fees and possibly a contribution to the freeholder's costs. Land Registry fees, lender fees and sometimes Stamp Duty Land Tax may also apply. Your solicitor will give you an estimate once they have seen the offer.
Do I have to pay the freeholder's legal fees?
There is no fixed rule for informal deals. Freeholders often ask leaseholders to cover their legal and valuation costs, and this is part of the negotiation. Your solicitor can ask for an estimate of those costs and challenge figures that look high.
Do I need a surveyor for an informal extension?
It is not compulsory, but a surveyor's view can help you judge whether the price is fair compared with the statutory route. Many people find it worth the cost before agreeing a premium.
Will my lender need to approve the new lease?
Usually, yes. Your lender has security over the lease, so it normally needs to consent to a new lease or a change to the existing one. Your solicitor will contact the lender and deal with any conditions.
Does Leaflegal charge for this?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us for the introduction, and that never changes what you pay them.
Can I pay the premium in instalments?
Most informal extensions require the premium to be paid in full on completion. Some freeholders may be open to other arrangements, but this is unusual and would need to be agreed and properly documented. If you need to borrow to pay the premium, speak to your lender or a mortgage broker early so the money is ready when the deal completes.
What should I send the solicitor?
A copy of your lease or the title number, any letters or offers from the freeholder, details of the freeholder and managing agent, recent ground rent demands and your mortgage lender's details. Let them know about any plans to sell or remortgage.
How quickly will a firm get back to me?
The firm we introduce you to usually gets in touch within one working day, often sooner. If your freeholder's offer has a deadline, mention it in your enquiry.
What if my freeholder will not reply?
Some freeholders are slow to respond or do not reply at all. Your solicitor can chase them and, if you qualify, explain how the statutory route would work, as it puts the freeholder under deadlines to respond. If the freeholder genuinely cannot be found, there is a separate court process that can be used.
Do I have to instruct the firm?
No. You can speak to the firm, hear what it would charge and decide whether to go ahead. There is no obligation.
Will Leaflegal tell me whether to accept the offer?
No. Leaflegal does not give legal advice. The firm we introduce you to will look at your offer and explain the options, and the decision is yours.
How many flat owners need to take part?
Under the current rules, the number of participating qualifying tenants must be at least half of the total number of flats in the building. In a building with just two flats, both owners must take part. Your solicitor will count the flats and check who qualifies, because getting the numbers wrong can make the notice invalid.
Does our building have to be purpose built?
No. Converted houses and purpose built blocks can both qualify, as long as the building is self contained and meets the other conditions. Some small converted buildings with a resident landlord are excluded, so your solicitor will check whether that applies to your building.
We have shops on the ground floor. Can we still buy the freehold?
Possibly. Under the long standing rules, a building does not qualify if more than 25 per cent of its internal floor area is non residential. The 2024 Act raises this to 50 per cent, and that change is being brought in over time. Your solicitor and surveyor will measure the commercial parts and confirm which limit applies.
Do owners who let out their flats count?
Yes. Owners who let their flats to tenants can usually be qualifying tenants and can take part. What matters is that they hold a long lease of the flat. An owner who lives elsewhere, even abroad, can still participate.
Can we include the garden or car park?
Often, yes. Land and facilities that the flat owners are entitled to use under their leases, such as gardens, car parks and bin stores, can usually be included in the claim. Your solicitor will check the title plans and the leases to identify what can be bought.
What if one owner holds several flats?
Ownership of several flats can affect who counts as a qualifying tenant under the rules, and the position has been changed over time. Your solicitor will look at who owns each flat and how that affects the numbers.
What is a resident landlord exclusion?
Some small buildings are excluded from collective enfranchisement where the building is a conversion with only a few units and the freeholder, or an adult member of their family, has lived in one of the flats as their main home for a qualifying period. The rules are detailed, and your solicitor will check whether they apply before the group spends money on the claim.
Do all the owners have to agree?
No. Only the required number of qualifying owners need to take part. Owners who do not want to join can stay out, keep their current leases and have the company as their landlord afterwards. It is still worth involving as many as possible, as this spreads the cost.
