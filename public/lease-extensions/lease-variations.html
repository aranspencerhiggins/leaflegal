Lease variations explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A lease is a contract that can run for a hundred years or more. Many were drafted decades ago, some in a hurry, and a surprising number contain mistakes, gaps or terms that no longer make sense. A lease variation is a formal change to the wording of an existing lease, made so that it works properly for the leaseholder, the freeholder, future buyers and mortgage lenders.

Most variations are made by agreement, using a document called a deed of variation. The leaseholder and the freeholder both sign it, any mortgage lender usually consents, and the change is registered at HM Land Registry. Where the freeholder will not agree, some types of defect can be put right by applying to a tribunal or, for genuine mistakes, to a court.

People usually look at varying a lease because something has gone wrong or is about to. A buyer's solicitor has raised a problem with the lease, a lender has refused to lend, or the leaseholder has found that the lease does not allow something they want to do. Sometimes all the flat owners in a building realise their leases do not work together, for example because the service charge shares do not add up.

A variation is different from simply getting permission to do something. If a lease requires the freeholder's consent for an alteration, for example, the usual answer is a licence for that particular work rather than a permanent change to the lease. A variation is used where the lease itself needs to say something different, now and for every future owner.

Why a defective lease matters

  • Buyers' solicitors may advise their clients not to proceed until it is fixed
  • Mortgage lenders may decline to lend on a lease with certain defects
  • Unclear repair or insurance terms can lead to disputes over who pays
  • Gaps in service charge clauses can leave costs unrecoverable or unfairly shared
  • Onerous ground rent terms can affect value and saleability
  • Missing rights can leave a flat without proper access or services on paper

The law around leases is also changing through the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024, which is being brought in over time. Some reforms, such as new options for dealing with ground rent, may affect how a lease problem can be solved. A solicitor will confirm what applies now.

Every lease is different, but certain problems come up again and again. A solicitor will read your lease in full and explain whether any of these apply.

Wrong or missing plans

The lease plan may show the wrong flat, leave out a parking space, garden or loft that the owner has always used, or be too unclear to identify what is included. Lenders and buyers want to know exactly what is being bought.

Repair and maintenance gaps

Some leases do not say clearly who is responsible for repairing parts of the building, such as the roof, the structure, windows or pipes. If nobody is clearly responsible, repairs can be left undone or disputed.

Insurance problems

A lease should make clear who insures the building and how the cost is recovered. Leases that leave this unclear, or that do not require adequate cover, often concern lenders.

Service charge errors

The percentages paid by each flat may not add up to 100 per cent across the building, leaving a shortfall or an overpayment. Some leases do not allow the recovery of certain costs, such as a reserve fund or managing agent fees, even though everyone has been paying them.

Missing rights

A flat may have no written right to use a shared entrance, a staircase, a bin store or the pipes and cables that serve it. In practice the owner uses them, but on paper the right is missing.

Restrictive or outdated terms

Some leases contain restrictions that no longer suit modern life, such as complete bans on alterations or unusual rules about use. Others refer to bodies or arrangements that no longer exist.

Onerous ground rent

Ground rents that double at regular intervals, or rise steeply in other ways, can make a flat hard to sell or mortgage. This is one of the most common reasons for a variation in recent years.

Leases that do not match each other

In some buildings, each flat was sold at a different time on a slightly different lease. Over the years this can leave owners with different obligations, different service charge methods and different rules. Bringing the leases into line usually needs the cooperation of several owners and the freeholder.

There are several ways to change a lease. Which one is available depends on the problem, the freeholder and the other leaseholders. A solicitor will explain the options for your lease.

By agreement

The most common route is a deed of variation agreed between the leaseholder and the freeholder. It can change almost any term, as long as both sides agree. Freeholders sometimes ask for a payment, particularly where the change reduces their income, such as a lower ground rent. They usually also ask the leaseholder to pay their legal costs.

Through a tribunal

Part IV of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1987 allows a party to a long lease of a flat to apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to vary the lease if it fails to make satisfactory provision for certain matters. These include repair and maintenance, insurance, the provision of services and the way service charges are calculated or recovered. There are also procedures where several leases in a building need to be changed together. The tribunal does not cover every kind of problem, so your solicitor will check whether yours fits.

Through the courts

If a lease does not reflect what the parties actually agreed, because of a genuine mistake in drafting, a court may be able to rectify it. This needs evidence of the mistake and is usually a last resort.

As part of a lease extension

A new lease granted as part of an extension can sometimes fix defects at the same time. Under the statutory route, changes are limited to certain kinds of defect and updates. In an informal extension, any change can be agreed. Your solicitor can explain whether combining the two would help.

After buying the freehold

Where the flat owners own the freehold together, they can agree to vary their own leases through their company, which can make it easier to correct problems across the whole building.

A straightforward variation agreed with a cooperative freeholder usually follows a simple path. Contested variations take longer. Your solicitor will explain what to expect.

How long this takes depends mostly on the freeholder. A cooperative freeholder and a simple change can mean a few weeks. Several leases, a slow freeholder or a disagreement about payment can stretch it to months.

1. Identifying the problem

The solicitor reads the lease, the title and any reports from a buyer's solicitor or lender, and identifies exactly what needs to change. Sometimes more than one problem is found, and it is sensible to deal with them together.

2. Proposing the change

Your solicitor writes to the freeholder or its solicitor setting out the proposed changes and the reasons. If other leaseholders are affected, they may need to be involved too.

3. Negotiation

The freeholder may agree, ask for changes, request a payment or refuse. Your solicitor handles the correspondence and explains what each response means.

4. Lender consent

If you have a mortgage, your lender usually needs to consent to the variation. The freeholder's lender may also need to agree in some cases.

5. The deed of variation

Once terms are agreed, a deed of variation is prepared, checked and signed by both sides. It sets out the original lease and the changes made to it.

6. Registration

Your solicitor registers the deed at HM Land Registry, so that future buyers and lenders can see the change on the title.

7. If agreement is not possible

Your solicitor will explain whether a tribunal or court application is available, how long it might take, what it might cost and the risks involved. You can then decide whether to take that step.

Ground rent terms are one of the main reasons people ask about lease variations. This section gives general information about how they are usually dealt with.

Why ground rent terms cause problems

Some leases, particularly on newer flats, include ground rents that double every set number of years. Over the life of a lease this can lead to very large sums. Lenders and buyers are cautious about these terms, and some will not proceed without a change.

Ground rent and tenancy rules

In some cases a ground rent above certain levels can technically bring a lease within the rules on assured tenancies, which lenders treat as a risk. Your solicitor will check whether this could apply to your lease.

Changing the ground rent by agreement

Many freeholders have agreed to vary ground rent terms, for example by replacing a doubling rent with a fixed rent or one that rises more modestly. Some have done so for whole developments. The freeholder may ask for a payment, and your solicitor will explain the proposal.

Ground rent on new leases

Rules that took effect in 2022 limit ground rent on most new long residential leases to a peppercorn. These rules do not usually change existing leases, but they can affect a new lease granted on an extension.

Reforms still being introduced

The 2024 Act includes provisions that would allow qualifying leaseholders to pay to reduce their ground rent to a peppercorn without extending the term. This depends on commencement and supporting regulations. A solicitor will tell you whether this is available and how it compares with a variation or extension.

Whatever the route, your solicitor will explain the options for your own lease. Leaflegal cannot say which is right for you.

The cost of a lease variation depends on the problem, how many leases are involved and whether the freeholder agrees.

Your own legal fees

Many firms quote a fixed fee for a straightforward deed of variation agreed with the freeholder. Contested matters, tribunal applications and court claims are usually charged by the hour or in stages.

The freeholder's costs

Freeholders usually ask the leaseholder to pay their reasonable legal costs of agreeing a variation. There is no fixed rule for agreed variations, so the amount can be negotiated. In tribunal cases, the rules on costs are different and your solicitor will explain them.

Any payment to the freeholder

If the variation reduces the freeholder's income or gives the leaseholder something extra, such as additional space, the freeholder may ask for a payment. A surveyor can advise on whether a figure is reasonable.

Other costs

There may be Land Registry fees, lender fees and, in some cases, tax to consider. Some variations are treated as creating a new lease, which can have different consequences. Your solicitor will check this.

Where several owners in a building share the same problem, they sometimes instruct one firm together and share the cost. This can make the work more efficient, though each owner and lender may still need to sign. Ask your solicitor whether a joint approach would work for your building.

Lease variations questions and answers

What is a deed of variation?
A legal document that changes the terms of an existing lease, signed by the leaseholder and freeholder.
Can my freeholder refuse?
They can, but some defects can be put right through a tribunal. Your solicitor will explain.
Can this be done with a lease extension?
Often, yes. Changes can be agreed as part of a new lease.
Which areas do you cover?
Leasehold flats in England and Wales.
Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
What is a deed of variation?
It is a legal document that changes the terms of an existing lease. It is signed by the leaseholder and the freeholder, and sometimes by other parties such as a management company. Once completed, it is registered at HM Land Registry so that anyone looking at the title can see the lease and the changes made to it.
Why would my lease need changing?
Common reasons include a wrong or missing plan, unclear repair or insurance clauses, service charge shares that do not add up, missing rights to use shared parts and onerous ground rent terms. Often the problem only comes to light when a buyer's solicitor or a lender reviews the lease. A solicitor will read your lease and explain whether a variation would help.
Can a lease be changed after it has been signed?
Yes. A lease is a contract, and the parties can agree to change it at any time. In some cases a tribunal or court can also order a change. The change must be properly documented and registered to be effective against future buyers and lenders.
Who needs to agree to a variation?
Usually the leaseholder and the freeholder, plus any management company that is a party to the lease. Mortgage lenders on both sides may also need to consent. If the change affects other flats, such as service charge shares, other leaseholders may need to be involved.
Is a lease variation the same as a lease extension?
No. A variation changes the terms of the existing lease, while an extension gives a longer lease. They can be combined, as a new lease on an extension can sometimes fix defects at the same time. Your solicitor can explain whether doing both together would make sense for you.
Can Leaflegal tell me if my lease is defective?
No. Leaflegal does not give legal advice or review leases. We introduce you to a regulated firm, and the solicitor there can read your lease and explain any problems.
Is a licence different from a variation?
Yes. A licence is a one off permission from the freeholder, for example to carry out particular alterations, and it leaves the lease terms unchanged. A variation permanently changes what the lease says. If you only need permission for something specific, a licence may be simpler. Your solicitor will explain which is appropriate.
What is a doubling ground rent?
It is a ground rent that doubles at fixed intervals, such as every ten or fifteen years. Over a long lease this can lead to very high rents. Lenders and buyers are often cautious about these terms. Many owners with doubling ground rents look at varying the lease or extending it to deal with the problem.
My lease plan is wrong. Does that matter?
It can. Buyers and lenders want to be sure what is included in the lease, such as the flat itself, a parking space or a garden. A wrong or unclear plan can hold up a sale or remortgage. A solicitor can check the plan against the title and suggest how it might be corrected.
The service charge percentages do not add up. What happens?
If the shares across all the flats do not add up to 100 per cent, the freeholder may be unable to recover the full cost of running the building, or some owners may pay too much. This can usually only be fixed by varying several leases together, either by agreement or through a tribunal application.
My lease does not mention the reserve fund. Is that a problem?
If a lease does not allow the landlord to collect money for a reserve fund, it may not be possible to require owners to contribute, even if they have been doing so. This can make planning for major works difficult. A variation can add a reserve fund clause where the parties agree or the tribunal orders it.
Can I change a ban on pets or alterations?
Restrictions like these can be changed by agreement with the freeholder, who may ask for a payment. Sometimes a one off licence or consent is simpler than varying the lease. Tribunal applications under the 1987 Act do not usually cover this kind of term. Your solicitor will explain which approach fits.
My flat has no written right to use the shared entrance. What can be done?
A missing right of way or a missing right to use pipes and cables can often be added by a deed of variation. Depending on the facts, there may also be other legal arguments that the right exists. A solicitor will look at the title and the history of the building.
Can a ground rent cause problems with lenders even if it is low now?
Yes. Lenders look at how the ground rent could change over the life of the mortgage and the lease, not just what it is today. A low rent that doubles regularly can become a concern. Your solicitor will look at the whole ground rent clause and explain how a lender is likely to view it.
What if my freeholder agrees?
If the freeholder agrees, the solicitors draft a deed of variation, both sides sign it, lenders consent and it is registered at HM Land Registry. This is usually the quickest and cheapest route. The freeholder may still ask for its legal costs and, sometimes, a payment.
What if my freeholder refuses?
Your solicitor will consider whether the problem falls within the tribunal's powers under the Landlord and Tenant Act 1987, whether a court could rectify a mistake, or whether the issue could be dealt with through a lease extension. Each option has different costs and risks, which your solicitor will explain.
Do other leaseholders need to be involved?
If the change only affects your lease, usually not. If it affects how costs are shared across the building, or several leases share the same defect, the other leaseholders and their lenders may need to be involved. Dealing with all the leases together is sometimes the only practical solution.
Does my mortgage lender need to agree?
Usually, yes. Your lender has security over your lease and will want to know that a change does not reduce the value of its security. Your solicitor will ask for consent and deal with any conditions the lender sets.
Can I fix my lease while selling my flat?
Sometimes. If the freeholder cooperates, a variation can be agreed during the sale, but it adds another party and can cause delay. Starting early, or as soon as a buyer's solicitor raises the issue, gives the best chance of keeping the sale on track.
Can a defect be dealt with by insurance instead?
For some issues, such as a missing right that has been used without problems for many years, indemnity insurance may be offered as an alternative to varying the lease. Whether a buyer and lender will accept it depends on the issue. Your solicitor will explain whether it is a realistic option.
Could a variation be treated as a new lease?
Some changes, such as adding extra land or extending the length of the lease, can in law be treated as the old lease ending and a new one being granted. This can have consequences for tax, registration and the lease terms. Your solicitor will check how the proposed change is likely to be treated before it is signed.
What can the tribunal change?
Under Part IV of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1987, the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) can vary a long lease of a flat that fails to make satisfactory provision for certain matters. These include repair and maintenance, insurance, the provision of services, and the computation and recovery of service charges. It cannot change any term simply because a party would prefer it.
Can the tribunal change several leases at once?
Yes. There are procedures for varying leases across a building where the same problem affects several flats, provided enough parties support the application. This is often used to correct service charge shares or add consistent clauses across a block.
What is rectification?
Rectification is a court remedy that corrects a written document so it reflects what the parties actually agreed, where a mistake was made in writing it down. It needs clear evidence of the mistake and the parties' true intention. It is usually considered when agreement cannot be reached.
How long does a tribunal application take?
It depends on the complexity and the tribunal's workload. Simple applications can be dealt with on paper, while others need a hearing. It often takes several months. Your solicitor will give you an idea of timescales for your case.
Who pays the costs of a tribunal application?
The tribunal has its own rules on costs, which differ from the courts, and parties often bear their own legal costs. There may be an application fee and, in some cases, compensation payable to someone who loses out because of the variation. Your solicitor will explain the costs risks before you decide.
How much does a deed of variation cost?
For a straightforward variation agreed with the freeholder, many firms charge a fixed fee. You may also pay the freeholder's legal costs, any payment the freeholder asks for, lender fees and Land Registry fees. Your solicitor will give you an estimate once they understand the problem.
Will the freeholder want paying?
Often, if the change gives the leaseholder something of value or reduces the freeholder's income, such as a lower ground rent. Even when no payment is asked for, freeholders usually expect their legal costs to be covered. A surveyor can advise on whether a requested payment is reasonable.

No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us for the introduction, and that never changes what you pay them.
What should I send the solicitor?
A copy of the lease or the title number, any letters from a buyer's solicitor or lender about the problem, the freeholder's details and your mortgage lender's details. If other flats in the building are affected, it also helps to know whether their owners are aware.
How quickly will the firm contact me?
The firm we introduce you to usually gets in touch within one working day, often sooner. If a sale or remortgage is waiting on the variation, mention this in your enquiry so the firm knows it is urgent.
Can owners in the same building share the cost?
Yes, where several owners need the same change, they can often instruct one firm and share the legal costs. A single approach to the freeholder can also be more effective than several separate ones. Each owner and their lender may still need to sign, so it takes some coordination.
When should I instruct a solicitor to sell my flat?
Many sellers instruct a solicitor when they put the flat on the market, or even before. This allows the solicitor to obtain the title and lease, check the remaining term and order the management pack early. With a leasehold flat, an early start can save weeks once you accept an offer.
What is a management pack?
It is information from the freeholder, managing agent or management company about the building and your share of its costs. It usually includes service charge accounts and budgets, insurance details, planned works, ground rent and any arrears. The buyer's solicitor needs it to advise the buyer. There is usually a fee for it.
Who pays for the management pack?
In most sales the seller pays, as the information is needed to sell the flat. The 2024 Act includes provisions about what can be charged for leasehold information, which are being brought in over time. Your solicitor will order the pack and tell you the cost.
What forms will I need to fill in?
Usually the standard property information form, a leasehold information form and a fittings and contents form. These ask about the flat, the building, service charges, disputes and any changes you have made. Your solicitor will send them to you and help with any questions.
What if I cannot find my lease?
Most leases are registered at HM Land Registry, and your solicitor can obtain a copy. Older leases are not always held in full, and your solicitor will explain how to deal with that if it happens. If you have other documents, such as a previous deed of variation or a lease extension, these are useful too. Your solicitor will check that everything affecting the lease is included in the contract pack so the buyer's solicitor has a complete picture.
Do I need to tell the buyer about disputes with neighbours or the freeholder?
The property information forms ask about disputes, and you must answer honestly. Giving incorrect or incomplete answers can lead to claims after the sale. If you are unsure how to answer a question, ask your solicitor.
Can I sell a flat I have let to a tenant?
Yes. You can sell with the tenant in place to an investor, or arrange for the tenancy to end before completion if the buyer wants vacant possession. Ending a tenancy has to follow the correct legal process and can take time. Your solicitor will explain how the tenancy affects the sale, and a letting agent or housing specialist can help with the tenancy itself.
Should I extend my lease before selling?
That depends on how long is left, your timescale and the market. Some sellers extend first, some start the statutory process and pass the notice to the buyer, and some sell without extending. A solicitor can explain the pros and cons of each for your flat, and the decision is yours.
