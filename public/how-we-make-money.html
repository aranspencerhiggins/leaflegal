How we make money

Leaflegal is free for you. Here is exactly how we are paid, and what that means for you.

You never pay us

Using Leaflegal costs you nothing. There is no fee to complete the form, no fee to be introduced and no obligation to instruct the firm we introduce you to.

Law firms pay us

We are paid by the law firms we introduce people and businesses to. Each partner firm pays us an agreed fee for each enquiry we send them. That is how we cover the cost of running the website, our advertising and the checks we carry out on every enquiry.

What that means for you

  • Each enquiry goes to one firm, chosen because it handles your type of matter and covers your area.
  • We don’t recommend lawyers based on who pays us most, and we don’t give legal advice.
  • Solicitors must tell you in writing about any fee they pay for an introduction, and it must not affect the advice they give you.
  • The fee the firm pays us does not change what the firm charges you. The firm will explain its own fees before any work starts.

Who we are

Leaflegal is a trading name of Vertical Brands Ltd, a company registered in England and Wales with company number 14254961. We are an introduction service. We are not a law firm, we do not give legal advice or immigration advice, and we are not regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority.

The firms we introduce are independent and regulated for the work they do. They are separate businesses from Leaflegal, and they are responsible for the advice and service they give you.

Why this matters

When you look for a lawyer, you are often doing so at a difficult time. You need to be able to trust the people helping you take that first step. Being clear about money is a big part of that trust. If you understand who is paying whom and why, you can weigh up what you hear and make your own decisions with confidence.

It also helps to know what the introduction fee does not do. It does not buy the firm a guarantee that you will instruct it. It does not buy influence over the advice the firm gives. And it does not give anyone else access to the details of your matter.

Every business has to pay for itself somehow. For Leaflegal, the money comes from the law firms that receive introductions from us.

Law firms pay for introductions

Firms that want to receive enquiries from Leaflegal agree terms with us in writing. They choose which areas of law, types of matter and locations they handle. When we introduce an enquiry to a firm, the firm pays us an agreed fee for that introduction. The amount is agreed individually with each firm.

The fee is for the introduction itself. It is not linked to whether you go on to instruct the firm, how much the firm earns from your matter or the outcome of your case.

What the money pays for

The income from firms covers the cost of running Leaflegal. That includes building and maintaining the website and the form, our advertising, the checks we carry out on each enquiry, keeping records of consent, handling questions and complaints, and writing and updating the guides on our website.

Why firms are willing to pay

Firms spend money on finding new clients in many ways, such as advertising online, paying for directory listings, running their own websites and sponsoring local events. Paying for an introduction is another way of doing the same thing. For the firm, the advantage is that it hears from people who have already said what kind of help they need and have asked to be contacted.

What you pay

Nothing to us. If you decide to instruct the firm, you pay the firm for its legal work on the terms you agree with it. That is the same as if you had found the firm in any other way.

Pay per introduction

Firms pay for each introduction they receive, rather than paying for a listing or for a share of their fees. That keeps the arrangement simple. It also means our income depends on making introductions that firms find genuinely useful, which in turn depends on people being introduced to the right kind of firm for their matter.

Enquiries that cannot be helped

Sometimes an enquiry turns out to be something a firm cannot help with, for example because the details given were wrong or the matter is outside the area it covers. Our written agreements with firms deal with situations like this. None of that affects you, and you are never asked to pay anything.

Knowing that the firm pays us, it is natural to wonder whether that affects you. Here is how we keep the arrangement fair.

Your bill does not go up

The fee the firm pays us does not change what the firm charges you. The firm sets its own prices and will explain them to you on the first call. It will confirm them in writing before any work starts. You are free to compare those prices with other firms.

You are not passed around

Each enquiry goes to one firm only. We do not sell your enquiry to several firms or run an auction for it. That means you are not chased by lots of firms at once, and the firm that contacts you knows it is the only one introduced to you through us.

You stay in control

There is no obligation to instruct the firm we introduce. You can ask questions, take time to think, speak to other firms and choose whoever you prefer. Using Leaflegal does not commit you to anything.

The advice is the firm's

The firm's lawyers are responsible for the advice they give you, and they owe you professional duties as their client. Their regulators expect them to act in your best interests and not to let a referral arrangement affect their advice.

You can ask questions

If you want to know more about the introduction fee, you can ask the firm, and you can ask us. We will explain how the arrangement works. We do not publish the individual amounts agreed with firms because they are commercial arrangements, but we are always happy to explain the principles.

Comparing with other ways of finding a lawyer

Whatever route you take to find a lawyer, someone usually pays for the firm to reach you. Search engines are paid for adverts, directories are paid for listings and some websites are paid for each enquiry. The difference with Leaflegal is that we tell you about it openly, we only send your enquiry to one firm and the firm must tell you about the fee in writing.

Introductions from services like Leaflegal to law firms are allowed, but they are subject to rules. Those rules are there to protect you.

Telling you in writing

Solicitors regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority must tell clients in writing when a client has been introduced through an arrangement where the firm pays a fee. That information is usually included in the firm's client care letter or terms of business. It means the arrangement is never hidden from you.

Independent advice

The same rules say that a referral arrangement must not affect the firm's independence or the advice it gives. The firm must act in your best interests. If you feel that is not happening, you can raise it with the firm, and concerns about a solicitor's conduct can be reported to the SRA.

Areas where referral fees are banned

In some areas of law, referral fees are not allowed at all. The best known are personal injury and clinical negligence claims. Leaflegal does not cover those areas, and we do not take payments for introductions in them. We also do not cover housing disrepair or financial mis-selling claims.

Other regulators

Licensed conveyancers and regulated immigration advisers have their own regulators and their own rules on introductions and transparency. Whichever kind of firm you are introduced to, it should explain its fees clearly and be open with you about how it came to have your details.

Our own obligations

As a company we must follow UK law, including data protection and consumer protection law. That means being clear about who we are, only sharing your details with your consent and not misleading you about what we do. We take those obligations seriously and we explain them on our website.

Consent to be introduced

Before we pass your enquiry to a firm, you agree on the form to be introduced and contacted. We record the wording you agreed to and when you agreed, and we send that record with your enquiry. It means the firm can see that you asked to hear from it, and it means there is a clear record if anyone ever needs to check.

An arrangement where firms pay for introductions only works if people can trust it. These are the principles we follow.

Introductions based on your matter

We introduce each enquiry to a firm because it handles that type of matter and covers your area. We do not send your enquiry to whichever firm pays us most at that moment, and we do not rank firms by what they pay.

No claims about outcomes

We do not tell you that a firm will win your case, get you a particular result or finish by a certain date. Every matter depends on its own facts. We also do not publish testimonials or success rates, because they can give a misleading impression of what will happen in your case.

Regulated firms only

We only introduce firms that are regulated for the work they do. That gives you the protections that come with a regulated firm, such as professional indemnity insurance, a complaints procedure and access to the Legal Ombudsman for service complaints.

Listening when things go wrong

If a firm does not treat someone fairly, we want to know. We may stop working with firms that do not meet the standards we expect. Telling us helps protect the next person who uses Leaflegal.

Your information is not for sale

We share your enquiry with the one firm we introduce, and only because you agreed. We never share the details of your matter with advertisers or data brokers, and we do not sell your details to lists of firms.

Not just about money

We would rather introduce fewer people well than many people badly. If an enquiry is clearly outside the areas we cover, we do not pass it to a firm just because a fee would be paid. If we are unsure, we check first. Firms rely on us to send them people they can genuinely help, and people rely on us to send them to firms that do their kind of work.

Look for the section about how you were introduced. If anything is missing or unclear, ask the firm to explain or to put it in writing before you agree.

More questions people ask

Do you help with consumer debts?
No. Leaflegal only introduces businesses that are owed money by other businesses in England and Wales. If the person who owes you money bought something for their own personal use, that is a consumer debt and it is outside this service. Consumer debt collection has separate rules and regulators, and we do not introduce firms for it.
What counts as a business debtor?
A business debtor is any organisation or person who bought goods or services from you for the purposes of their own business. That includes limited companies, limited liability partnerships, partnerships and sole traders acting in the course of their trade. A solicitor will check the status of your debtor, because some routes, such as winding up, only apply to companies.
Does Leaflegal give legal advice?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service, not a law firm. We introduce your business to one regulated law firm that handles commercial debt recovery. Any advice about your debt, your contract or the steps you could take comes from the solicitor, who will look at the details of your own situation.
How does the introduction work?
You fill in a short form telling us about the debt, who owes it and roughly how much it is. We pass your enquiry to one regulated firm that handles this kind of work. The firm then contacts you, usually within one working day, to talk through the debt and explain how they can help and what it would cost.
Do I have to instruct the firm?
No. There is no obligation. After speaking to the firm you are free to decide whether to instruct them. If you do go ahead, the firm will send you a client care letter setting out the work, the fees and the terms before anything starts.
Can you help if the debtor is in Scotland or Northern Ireland?
This service covers debts pursued under the law of England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems and court procedures. If your debtor is based outside England and Wales, mention it on the form, and the firm will tell you whether they can help or whether you need a firm elsewhere.
When should I involve a solicitor?
Many businesses involve a solicitor once their own reminders have not worked and the debt is clearly overdue. Waiting too long can make recovery harder, especially if the debtor is struggling. A solicitor can explain the options early and help you decide whether a formal letter is the right next step.
Is my debt too small to chase?
There is no fixed minimum, but the cost of recovery has to make sense against the amount owed. For smaller debts, a fixed fee letter or a straightforward claim may be proportionate. A solicitor will give you an honest view of whether it is worth pursuing, taking into account interest and compensation that may be added.
What if the debtor says the work was faulty?
A genuine dispute about the quality of the goods or work changes things. Some routes, such as a statutory demand, are not suitable for disputed debts. A solicitor will look at the complaint, your contract and the evidence, and explain which options remain open and what the risks are.
What if I don't have a written contract?
A contract does not have to be written to be binding. Orders by email, purchase orders, quotes that were accepted and a history of dealings can all show what was agreed. A solicitor will look at whatever records you have and explain how they support your claim.
Can a solicitor help with several debts at once?
Yes. Many firms handle multiple debts for the same business, and some offer a service designed for regular debt recovery with online tracking. If you have several overdue accounts, mention this when you enquire so the firm can explain its options.
What if the debtor has stopped trading?
If the debtor has closed down, entered administration or gone into liquidation, the options change. You may need to submit a claim to the insolvency practitioner rather than sue. A solicitor can check the debtor's status and explain what is realistic.
What is a personal guarantee?
A personal guarantee is a promise, usually by a director, to pay the company's debts if the company does not. If your customer signed one when opening a credit account, it may give you a second person to pursue. A solicitor will check whether the guarantee was properly signed and what it covers before relying on it.
What is retention of title?
A retention of title clause in your terms says that goods remain yours until they are paid for. If it was properly included in the contract, it may let you recover unpaid goods that the debtor still holds, which can be valuable if the debtor becomes insolvent. A solicitor will check whether your clause is likely to work.
What is a letter before action?
It is a formal letter that sets out what is owed and why, and warns that court proceedings will follow if the debt is not paid by a deadline. Courts expect parties to try to resolve matters before issuing a claim, and many debts are paid once a solicitor's letter arrives.
How does a court claim work?
The solicitor issues a claim, usually online, setting out the debt, interest and costs. The debtor has a short period to respond. If they do not respond, judgment can usually be entered without a hearing. If they defend, the court sets a timetable and the case may go to a hearing.
What happens after judgment?
The debtor is ordered to pay, usually within a set time. If they do not, the creditor can use enforcement tools such as enforcement agents, charging orders, third party debt orders or an order requiring the debtor to give information about its finances. A solicitor will suggest the method most likely to work.
What is a statutory demand?
A statutory demand is a formal demand for payment of an undisputed debt, made under insolvency law. If a company owes more than £750 and does not pay or secure the debt within 21 days, the creditor may be able to petition to wind it up. It should only be used where the debt is clear.
What is a winding up petition?
It is an application to the court to put a company into compulsory liquidation because it cannot pay its debts. It is a serious step that affects all of the company's creditors. A solicitor will explain whether it is suitable for your debt and what it involves.
How long do I have to bring a claim?
In England and Wales, a claim for a contract debt must usually be started within six years of the date payment fell due. Written acknowledgement of the debt or a part payment can restart the clock. It is still best to act long before the deadline, while evidence and assets are available.
