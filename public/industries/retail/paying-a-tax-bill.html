The basics

Retailers collect VAT on every sale and hand most of it over a quarter later. After a strong autumn, that can be a large bill. The difficulty is that the cash taken in October and November is often already spent on January stock, rent and the staff who covered Christmas. The VAT is there on paper, but the money has moved on. Multi-site retailers have the added complication that stronger shops can mask weaker ones, so the group’s VAT bill may be larger than any single till suggests.

Corporation tax raises a similar issue. A profitable year can be tied up in unsold inventory, and the profit shown in the accounts is not all sitting in the bank. Short-term borrowing can spread the bill, though it is worth testing what the next two quarters of sales look like before taking it on. Before borrowing, many retailers check the stock position first, because selling slow lines at a small discount can release cash at lower cost than a loan.

What businesses use it for

VAT after the Christmas quarter

Pay the quarter that includes the busiest trading when stock for the new year has already been bought.

Corporation tax with cash tied in stock

Settle a bill on profits that are held in inventory rather than the bank.

PAYE for seasonal staff

Cover payroll taxes for temporary staff taken on for peak weeks, once sales have tapered.

A bill across several shops

Meet a single HMRC deadline for a small group when only some shops have the cash on the day.

How it fits

A VAT and tax loan for the fixed bill

A VAT and tax loan is a short-term loan repaid over a few months, with the lender paying HMRC or releasing the funds for you to do so. For example, a retailer with a £30,000 VAT bill could repay over six months. Lenders look at VAT returns, accounts and bank statements.

Revolving credit for stock and tax together

Retailers with repeating peaks often prefer a revolving credit facility. You draw when needed, repay as sales come in, and pay interest only on what you use. It suits a business that has to cover both stock buying and tax from the same cash.

Repayments that follow sales

If sales are strongly seasonal, test repayments against the quietest quarter. A broker can ask lenders whether any will shape repayments to the pattern, though many use fixed monthly instalments.

Things to consider

Check which VAT scheme applies

Retailers can use special VAT schemes, and the choice affects how the bill is calculated. Your accountant can say whether the one you use is the best for the business.

Stock is not cash

A balance sheet with lots of stock looks strong, but lenders will focus on cash flow and sales. Be ready to show recent sales by month.

Look at the cause

If the bill keeps arriving when cash is short, consider setting aside VAT weekly in a separate account. A loan helps once, a habit helps every quarter.

Questions and answers

Can a shop get a loan to pay VAT?
Yes. VAT and tax loans are designed for this. The lender either pays HMRC or releases funds to you, and you repay in instalments over several months. Expect to provide VAT returns, accounts and bank statements. Lenders will usually also ask for the HMRC demand and recent bank statements so they can see the bill and how your sales are performing.
Why does retail VAT feel hard to pay?
VAT on sales is collected with each sale, but the payment falls due after the quarter ends. If cash has gone on stock or rent in between, the money is no longer there. It is a timing issue more than a profit one. The gap between a sale and the VAT payment can run to several months, and in that time stock, rent and wages all draw on the same takings.
Is Time to Pay better than borrowing?
HMRC may agree a payment plan, and for some shops that costs less than borrowing. HMRC charges interest on what is outstanding. Compare the total cost and conditions of both before choosing. It is worth asking an adviser to work out the total interest under each route, as the cheaper monthly cost is not always the cheaper arrangement overall.
Can I borrow if my sales are seasonal?
Yes. Lenders see seasonal trading regularly in retail. They will look at the full year’s figures, not just recent months. A clear explanation of your peak and quiet periods helps. Strong seasonal peaks are not a problem in themselves, so long as the figures show that the business can meet repayments through the quieter months.
How soon should I start?
As early as you can. Lenders need time to run their checks and HMRC deadlines do not move. Starting a few weeks before the bill is due gives more choice than leaving it to the final days. A broker can also check which lenders are willing to proceed in the time you have, and which would be too slow for your deadline.
