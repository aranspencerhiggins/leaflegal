The basics

Ecommerce stock cycles are long and front-loaded. A brand sourcing from overseas may pay a deposit at order, the balance before shipment, and then wait weeks for sea freight, customs and delivery to a warehouse or fulfilment centre. Marketplace sellers may need to send inventory into a fulfilment network ahead of peak, then wait for payouts that arrive on a cycle. Advertising spend to sell the stock adds a further outlay.

Lenders can see online sales through payment providers, marketplaces and bank statements, so a revolving credit facility, an unsecured loan or a revenue-based advance repaid from sales are common routes. Import VAT and duty can also create a large short-term bill, for which VAT and tax loans are sometimes considered. That is long enough for a good seller to run short of cash. Weeks of cash tied up before launch are common.

What businesses use it for

Peak-season inventory

Place a large order in the summer for Black Friday and Christmas, paying suppliers before any of the sales come in.

Overseas supplier deposits

Pay deposits and balances to manufacturers abroad, covering the months before stock reaches the warehouse.

Marketplace fulfilment stock

Send a large inbound shipment to a fulfilment network ahead of a promotion, and carry the cost until payouts arrive.

Import VAT and duty

Cover the import VAT and duty due when goods clear customs, before the stock is sold.

How it fits

A revolving facility for repeat buying

Brands that reorder regularly can use a revolving credit facility, drawing for each order and repaying as sales come in. For example, a skincare brand might hold a £75,000 limit. Lenders look at monthly sales, margins and return rates.

Revenue-linked repayment

A merchant cash advance or revenue-based product is repaid as a share of sales, so repayments follow demand. It is usually costlier than a conventional loan, so compare the total repayable.

An unsecured loan for a defined order

For a single planned order, such as the next season’s range, an unsecured loan offers a fixed sum and a set term. Lenders review accounts and bank statements, and directors may be asked for a personal guarantee.

Things to consider

Long lead times tie up cash

Between paying a supplier and receiving payouts, money can be committed for months. Plan the borrowing term for the whole cycle, not just the sales period.

Return rates and dead stock

High returns reduce real revenue, and slow lines tie up cash. Be cautious about borrowing to buy more of a product that is not yet proven.

Platform dependence

Marketplace rules, fee changes or account suspensions can disrupt sales. Lenders know this, and a diversified customer base can help.

Questions and answers

Can I borrow to buy stock for my online shop?
Yes, if the company has traded for at least six months and has the revenue lenders look for. Revolving facilities, unsecured loans and revenue-based advances are common. A broker can compare these once they have your figures. Many sellers also ask lenders about options for further orders.
Do lenders accept marketplace sales as evidence?
Many do. They can look at payment provider and marketplace reports as well as bank statements. A broker can tell you which providers are comfortable with your sales channels. Your sales reports and payout histories help a broker understand your business.
Can I finance goods bought from overseas?
Sometimes, through general working capital or specialist trade finance that pays the supplier directly. Availability depends on the goods and your trading history. A broker can say what may be possible. Some providers will also look at your supplier history.
What if my stock does not sell?
You would still need to meet repayments, so a cautious order size helps. Consider testing a new line with a smaller batch, and keep a cash buffer. Test new lines on a small run first, and avoid ordering more until sell-through is clear.
Can finance cover import VAT?
Some businesses use a VAT or tax loan, or a working-capital facility, to cover import VAT before goods are sold. It is worth checking whether postponed VAT accounting is available to you, via your accountant. A broker can discuss how the timing would work for you.
