The basics

Ecommerce growth tends to arrive in steps. A seller outgrows a spare room and takes a small warehouse, then adds a pick-and-pack team, then a new product range or a second country. Each step needs cash for deposits, racking, staff and stock, and sales in the new market or range take months to build. Expansion into Europe, for instance, can involve a new VAT registration, a fulfilment partner, translation and local returns handling before the first order ships.

Online businesses have fewer hard assets than most, so lenders place weight on trading history, margins and customer data. Unsecured term loans are common, and asset finance covers racking, packing machinery and vehicles. Buying a smaller competitor or a brand is another route, which may involve a larger loan. Stock for the new range often needs a revolving facility, so the working capital can flex with the sales that follow.

What businesses use it for

A warehouse and fulfilment team

Take on a unit with racking, packing stations and staff, or fund a move to a larger one as order volumes rise.

A new product range

Pay for design, samples, the first production run and a launch campaign before the range has sold anything.

Selling in a new country

Cover translation, local VAT set-up, fulfilment and returns for a European or US launch.

Buying a smaller brand

Acquire a competitor’s store, customer list and stock to add revenue and customers faster than building from scratch.

How it fits

An unsecured term loan on trading history

Lenders often offer £25,000 to £250,000 over one to five years to online businesses with solid accounts, based on revenue, margin and growth. Without property or large assets, they rely on the company’s figures and a director’s guarantee. A strong repeat-customer rate helps the case.

Racking and equipment through asset finance

Racking, conveyor systems, label printers, forklifts and vans can be financed with the equipment as security. This keeps the unsecured borrowing for stock and marketing. Used equipment is often easier to finance than people expect, since lenders understand its resale value.

Acquisitions

Buying another online business involves valuing its profits, stock and customer base. Lenders will often lend against the target’s trading record as well as yours, sometimes with the seller leaving part of the price to be paid later. Acquisitions are specialist, so a broker with experience can help.

Things to consider

Growth that outpaces stock cash

More sales means more stock paid for in advance. Plan the working capital for the larger sales volume as well as the capital spend.

New markets carry hidden costs

Returns, VAT, customs and carrier costs can be higher abroad. Include them in the case you show a lender.

Concentration on one channel

If most sales come from one platform or one ad channel, lenders may see the business as more fragile. Diversifying helps.

Questions and answers

Will lenders finance an online business with no property?
Yes. They look at trading history, margins, repeat customers and the owners rather than assets. Unsecured lending is common, sometimes with a director’s guarantee. Equipment such as racking or vehicles can be financed separately as assets. A broker can explain which lenders understand ecommerce.
How much do lenders expect to see in sales history?
At least six months of trading is needed, and many prefer 12 months or more to see a full seasonal cycle, including peak periods. Monthly sales data, margins and return rates are useful. A broker can help you prepare the figures in the way lenders expect.
Can I borrow to buy another online business?
Yes, though it is a more specialist request. Lenders examine the target’s profits, stock and customer base, and may want valuation support. Terms might include a deposit from you and sometimes a deferred payment to the seller. A broker with acquisition experience can help.
Should I finance a warehouse move through a loan or asset finance?
Probably both. The racking, packing machinery and vehicles can be financed as assets, while a loan or facility covers the deposit, moving costs and extra staff. Splitting them often costs less and keeps the repayments aligned with the life of each item.
What if the new market does not sell as well as planned?
Repayments continue regardless. This is why many brokers suggest testing a new market before borrowing heavily, and keeping the term long enough that repayments stay manageable if sales are slow. Lenders may ask how the business would cope if the launch took longer than forecast.
