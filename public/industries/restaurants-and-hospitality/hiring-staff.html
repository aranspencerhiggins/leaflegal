The basics

A hospitality business often has to hire ahead of demand. A new head chef may need two or three weeks of paid prep, a second site needs a full front-of-house team before its first booking, and a summer season needs temporary staff trained and on the rota before the first busy weekend. Employer National Insurance, holiday pay and uniform costs add to the wage bill, and rises in the minimum wage can lift costs for the whole team at once.

Wages are the largest regular outgoing for most venues, so lenders look closely at how comfortably takings cover them. Unsecured loans, merchant cash advances repaid from card sales and revolving credit facilities are the usual options. The choice depends on whether the hire is permanent, seasonal or tied to a new opening. Lenders will also look at how much of takings already goes on payroll before deciding what extra they are comfortable with.

What businesses use it for

Staffing a new opening

Pay a chef team, bar staff and managers during the weeks of training and soft launch before the new site is trading at full volume.

Seasonal summer or Christmas team

Take on and train extra waiting and kitchen staff for a peak, covering the wages until the busier takings arrive.

Hiring a head chef or manager

Bring in an experienced hire on a salary the venue cannot yet carry from its own margins, as part of a menu or format change.

Training and certification

Cover food hygiene, licensing, allergen and first-aid training for new starters so they can work a full shift safely.

How it fits

Matching the loan to the peak

For a seasonal team, a short unsecured loan or merchant cash advance can line up repayments with the busy months. For example, a hotel hiring eight extra staff for six months might borrow £30,000 and repay it over a similar period.

Revolving cover for uneven weeks

A revolving credit facility suits venues whose wages are steady while takings swing, because the business draws in a quiet week and repays after a busy one. Lenders check consistency of card revenue and how much headroom already exists.

What lenders review

Expect questions on payroll as a share of takings, how long the venue has traded and what the hire is expected to add. Recent accounts, bank statements and director details are usual, and an unsecured lender may ask for a personal guarantee.

Things to consider

Wages outlast the loan

The loan covers the early months, but the salary goes on. Check that takings will carry the new role once repayments end, and be cautious on a hire that only pays back at full capacity.

High turnover in the sector

Hospitality staff move on more often than in many industries, so training money can walk out of the door. Budget for replacing people, not just recruiting once.

Rising baseline costs

A minimum wage increase lifts every rate on the rota, not just the new hire’s. Build that into your numbers, and avoid borrowing to the limit for a hire that leaves no margin.

Questions and answers

Can I borrow to pay wages while a new restaurant opens?
Many operators do, usually with an unsecured loan or a facility repaid from takings. Lenders will want to see the plan, the other costs and how the business will cover wages once open. A broker can explain what a lender is likely to ask for.
What finance suits temporary summer staff?
Short-term options often fit best, such as a merchant cash advance repaid from card sales or a short unsecured loan. Because the staff are only needed for the season, a long-term commitment could outlast the benefit. A broker can compare the choices.
Will lenders count my existing wage bill?
Yes. They look at payroll as a share of revenue, and whether profit after wages can cover repayments. Venues with tight margins may find lenders more cautious, so keep recent payroll figures and accounts ready. Private income is also considered, and so is how steady each source is. A broker can advise how your own mix is likely to be viewed.
Can I finance recruitment agency fees?
Some businesses include them in a general working-capital loan. Agency fees can be a meaningful lump sum, so it may be worth asking the agency about staged payment first. A broker can say whether a loan is likely to be sensible for the amount.
Do I need a personal guarantee?
Unsecured lenders commonly ask directors for one, particularly for smaller limited companies. It means you could be personally liable if the business cannot repay. Read the terms carefully, and take advice from your solicitor or accountant before signing. It is worth checking exactly what is being guaranteed.
