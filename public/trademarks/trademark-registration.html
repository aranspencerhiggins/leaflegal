About trademark registration

A registered trademark gives you the exclusive right to use your brand for the goods and services it covers, across the whole of the UK. Applications are made to the UK Intellectual Property Office, which examines them, publishes them for two months so others can oppose, and then registers the mark for ten years at a time.

A trademark solicitor or chartered trade mark attorney checks whether your mark is likely to be accepted, searches for earlier marks that could block it, chooses the right classes and writes the list of goods and services so it protects how you actually trade. They can also extend protection abroad, for example through the Madrid system.

A trademark solicitor or chartered trade mark attorney checks whether your brand is free to use and register, files and prosecutes your application at the UK IPO, and acts for you if someone copies your brand or objects to your mark. Trademark registration in brief

UK trademark registration gives you the exclusive right to use your name, logo or slogan for the goods and services you list, across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. You register a trademark with the UK Intellectual Property Office, which examines it, publishes it for opposition and then registers it for ten years.

A trademark solicitor or attorney checks the mark is distinctive, searches for earlier rights, picks the right classes and drafts a specification that matches how you trade. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm for your trademark registration.

Trademark registration explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Registering a trademark gives you a legal monopoly over your brand for the goods and services you list. Once registered, you can stop others using the same or a confusingly similar sign for similar goods or services anywhere in the UK, without having to prove that you built up a reputation first. That makes a registration one of the most valuable assets a growing business can own.

Applications are made to the UK Intellectual Property Office. Anyone can apply online, and some businesses do. Many choose to use a trademark solicitor or a chartered trade mark attorney because the decisions made at the start, such as what exactly to register and how to describe your goods and services, decide how useful the registration is for years to come. Those decisions cannot be widened once the application is filed.

What you can register

A registration is also useful evidence. Online marketplaces, social media platforms and domain name dispute services all act more readily when you can show a registered mark. Investors and buyers often ask about trademarks during due diligence, and a clean registration in the right name can make a deal simpler.

Most applications are for a word mark, which protects a name in any style, or a figurative mark, which protects a logo or stylised name. Slogans, product names, series of related marks and some unusual signs such as shapes, colours and sounds can also be registered if they are distinctive and can be shown clearly on the register.

Why people use a professional

  • To check whether the mark is likely to be accepted before paying the official fee
  • To search for earlier marks that could lead to an opposition or a dispute
  • To choose the classes and write a specification that matches the business
  • To deal with any objection from the examiner
  • To plan protection in other countries if the business will trade abroad

A UK application follows a set path. Knowing the stages helps you understand what the firm is doing and when you might hear from them.

1. Checking the mark

The firm first looks at whether the mark is distinctive for your goods and services. Marks that describe the product, praise it or name a place are often refused. They also consider whether the mark is likely to clash with earlier rights, usually by running a clearance search.

2. Preparing the application

The application names the owner, shows the mark and lists the goods and services in the classes you choose. The owner should normally be the business that will use the brand. Getting the owner right at the start avoids problems with investors, buyers and lenders later.

3. Examination

After filing, an examiner at the UK IPO checks the application. They look at whether the mark is acceptable in itself and whether the specification is clear. The UK IPO also searches for similar earlier marks and may notify their owners that your application has been filed. If the examiner objects, they send a report setting a deadline to respond.

4. Publication and opposition

Once accepted, the application is published in the Trade Marks Journal. For two months, which can be extended to three, anyone with earlier rights can oppose it. Many applications pass through this stage without any challenge.

5. Registration

If there is no opposition, or an opposition is resolved in your favour, the mark is registered and a certificate is issued. Your rights date back to the filing date, and the registration lasts ten years from that date.

Common mistakes

Firms often help people who have run into problems with an application they filed themselves. The same issues come up again and again.

  • Filing a mark that describes the goods, which the examiner then refuses
  • Leaving out a class the business really needed, which cannot be added later
  • Claiming whole classes of goods the business will never sell
  • Filing in the wrong owner's name, such as a director instead of the company
  • Filing a logo when the name was what needed protecting
  • Missing the deadline to reply to an examination report
  • Paying an unofficial invoice that arrived after filing

None of these is unusual, and most can be avoided by taking advice before filing. If you have already filed and something has gone wrong, a firm can often still help, for example by narrowing the specification, filing a fresh application or recording a change of owner.

The list of goods and services is called the specification, and it is the heart of the application. It sets out exactly what your registration protects. Too narrow, and a competitor may be able to use a similar brand for related goods. Too wide, and you may face objections, oppositions or a non use challenge later.

The 45 classes

Goods and services are grouped into 45 classes under an international system. Classes 1 to 34 cover goods and classes 35 to 45 cover services. A clothing brand might need class 25 for clothing, class 18 for bags and class 35 for online retail. A software business might need classes 9, 42 and perhaps 41 for training.

Writing the specification

The UK IPO publishes pre approved terms that can be selected, and many applications use them. A professional will often combine standard terms with wording tailored to the business, so the registration covers what you actually sell and what you realistically plan to sell next.

  • Think about products and services you plan to launch in the next few years
  • Avoid claiming everything in a class if you will never use it
  • Remember that each extra class increases the official fee
  • Remember that the specification cannot be widened after filing, only narrowed

Series marks

If you use a few versions of the same mark that differ only in small ways, such as colour, it may be possible to file them as a series in one application. The firm can tell you whether that suits your marks.

The cost of registering a trademark is made up of the UK IPO's official fee and the firm's professional fee. The official fee depends mainly on the number of classes. Official fees change from time to time, so check GOV.UK for the current figures or ask the firm.

Professional fees vary between firms and with the amount of work. Many firms offer a fixed fee for preparing and filing a straightforward UK application, sometimes with a search included. Replying to an examiner's objection is often a separate fixed fee. Oppositions are usually charged separately and are harder to estimate.

What affects the cost

It can help to think about cost against the value of the brand. Rebranding after a dispute means new signage, packaging, websites and marketing, and the loss of recognition you have built. Compared with that, the cost of a search and a properly prepared application is usually modest. The firm can help you decide how far to go now and what can wait.

  • How many classes you need
  • Whether a clearance search is included and how wide it is
  • Whether the mark is likely to face objections
  • Whether you file in the UK only or also abroad
  • Whether you want a series of marks or several separate applications

Ask the firm what its fee includes, whether VAT is added and what happens to the fee if an objection or opposition arises. A clear written estimate helps you budget.

A straightforward UK application with no objections and no opposition usually moves from filing to registration in around four months. Each stage has its own timing.

  • Search and advice before filing, often a few days to two weeks
  • Examination by the UK IPO, usually within a few weeks of filing
  • Publication for opposition, two months, extendable to three
  • Registration shortly after the opposition period ends if nobody opposes

If the examiner objects, you have a deadline to respond, and the reply can add weeks or months. If someone opposes, the timetable is set by the UK IPO tribunal and a contested opposition can take many months.

Can it be faster

The UK IPO offers a fast track examination for an extra fee in some cases, which speeds up the first stage. It does not shorten the opposition period. If you need protection quickly, for example before an investment round or a launch, tell the firm early so they can plan.

While the application is pending you can use the ™ symbol and can tell people you have applied. You can't sue for infringement until the mark is registered, but once it is, your rights are treated as starting from the filing date.

Registration is the start rather than the end. To keep your mark strong, use it, watch for copies and keep the register up to date.

Use it consistently

A registered mark that is not genuinely used for five years can be revoked. Use the mark as registered, on the goods and services listed, and keep dated evidence such as invoices, packaging and advertising.

Watch for conflicts

The UK IPO does not stop similar marks being registered unless someone opposes. A watch service alerts you to new applications that could conflict, so you can oppose in time.

Keep details up to date

Record changes of address, a change of owner after a sale or restructure, and licences you grant. Diary the renewal date, which falls every ten years from the filing date.

Use the symbol

Once registered you can use the ® symbol. Using it before registration is an offence, so use ™ until then.

Protecting the brand abroad

A UK registration only protects your brand in the UK. If you sell online to customers abroad, use overseas manufacturers or plan to expand, it is worth thinking about protection in other countries early, because many systems work on a first to file basis.

The Madrid system

The UK is a member of the Madrid system, run by the World Intellectual Property Organization. It lets the owner of a UK application or registration file one international application that designates other member countries, such as the European Union, the United States, China or Australia. Each country then examines the mark under its own rules.

Using Madrid can be simpler and cheaper than filing separately in each country, and renewals can be managed centrally. For the first five years the international registration depends on the UK application or registration it is based on, so if the UK mark is refused or cancelled in that time, the international protection can fall too.

Priority

If you file in another country within six months of your UK filing date, you can usually claim the UK date as your priority date. That means your foreign application is treated as filed on the same day as your UK one, which helps if someone else files in the meantime.

Choosing countries

  • Countries where you sell now or will soon
  • Countries where your products are made
  • Countries where copies are a known problem
  • Large online markets your customers come from

The firm can explain the costs and the options. Many businesses start with the UK and one or two key markets, then add more as they grow.

Trademark registration questions and answers

How much does it cost to register a trademark in the UK?
The UK IPO charges an official fee for the first class and a further fee for each extra class. Professional fees for searching and filing are usually quoted as a fixed fee on top.
How long does UK trademark registration take?
Around four months if there are no objections or oppositions, and longer if there are.
Can I register a trademark myself?
Yes. Anyone can apply online at the UK IPO. Many businesses use a professional because a badly drafted specification or the wrong classes can leave a brand poorly protected, and mistakes can’t be fixed once the application is filed.
How long does registration take?
If there are no objections or oppositions, a UK application usually takes around four months from filing to registration. Objections or an opposition can add several months.
Does registering my company name protect it as a trademark?
No. Registering a company at Companies House does not give you trademark rights. A trademark registration is a separate application to the UK IPO.
Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Do I need to search before I apply?
It is not compulsory, but it is usually wise. The UK IPO does not refuse an application just because a similar earlier mark exists. Instead, the owner of that mark may oppose. A search helps you avoid paying for an application that is likely to be challenged, or a brand that you may later have to change.
Can I apply for a name I haven't started using yet?
Yes. You can apply for a mark you intend to use. You need a genuine intention to use it for the goods and services listed, and if you don't use it within five years of registration it becomes vulnerable to being revoked.
Who should own the trademark?
Usually the business that will use the brand, such as your limited company. Some owners file in their own name and license the company to use it, which can have tax or planning reasons. The firm can explain the options. Getting this right early saves work during investment or a sale.
What makes a mark distinctive?
A mark is distinctive if customers would see it as a sign of where the goods come from rather than a description of them. Invented words and words unrelated to the goods are strongest. Descriptive words, laudatory terms like best or premium and common shapes are weakest. The firm can tell you where your mark sits.
Should I file a word mark or a logo first?
If budget allows only one, many firms suggest the word mark because it protects the name whatever the styling. If the name itself is weak but the logo is distinctive, a logo mark may be easier to register. The firm will explain the trade off for your brand.
Can I trademark my own name?
Often yes, if it is distinctive for your goods or services and nobody has earlier rights in the same or a similar name. Common surnames can be harder. Others with the same name may still be able to use it honestly in some circumstances.
How long before launch should I file?
As early as you can once you are confident in the name. Your rights run from the filing date, so filing before launch reduces the risk that someone else files first or starts using something similar. It also gives you time to deal with any objection before your marketing starts.
Is it worth registering if I am a small business?
Many small businesses find it worthwhile, because a brand is often one of their most valuable assets and copying can hurt a small business more than a large one. A registration also makes it much easier to deal with copies on online marketplaces. The firm can help you decide what level of protection makes sense for your budget.
How do I choose the right classes?
Start from what you sell and plan to sell. The firm will map that to the right classes and draft wording that covers your business properly. Many businesses need two or three classes. Some need more. Each extra class adds to the official fee, so it is worth focusing on the most important ones.
Can I change my application after filing?
You can narrow the goods and services or correct minor errors, but you cannot add new goods or services or make significant changes to the mark. That is why the specification and the representation of the mark are checked carefully before filing.
What is a series application?
A series lets you register up to six marks that are very similar, differing only in small details such as colour or the presence of a word that doesn't change the overall identity, in one application. It is not suitable for every set of marks, and the examiner decides whether they qualify.
Can I file in my business name before the company is formed?
The applicant must be a person or an existing legal entity. If the company isn't formed yet, the mark can be filed in your own name and assigned to the company later. The assignment should be recorded at the UK IPO.
Is my application public?
Yes. Once filed, application details including the owner's name and address appear on the public register. Firms often act as the address for service so that official letters go to them. Be wary of unsolicited invoices that look official after you file.
What if I already have an EU trademark?
EU trademarks registered before the end of the Brexit transition period were cloned into comparable UK marks automatically. Applications filed after that date need a separate UK application to protect the brand in the UK. A firm can check what you already hold.
Can a firm act for me if I am outside the UK?
Yes. A UK address for service is needed for UK trademark matters, and a UK firm can provide it. Overseas businesses often use a UK solicitor or attorney to file and look after their UK marks.
What happens if the examiner objects?
The UK IPO sends an examination report explaining the objection and setting a deadline to reply. The firm can argue that the mark is acceptable, provide evidence that it has become distinctive through use, narrow the specification or suggest changes. Many objections are resolved this way.
What is a notice of threatened opposition?
During the first two months after publication, someone considering an opposition can file a notice of threatened opposition, which extends their deadline to three months. It is often a sign that the other side wants to negotiate. The firm can contact them to understand their concerns.
Can an opposition be settled?
Yes, and many are. Common outcomes include narrowing the goods and services, agreeing how each side will use its mark, or a coexistence agreement. A cooling off period can sometimes be agreed to give time for talks before formal steps are taken.
What if I lose an opposition?
The application may be refused in whole or in part, and you may have to pay a contribution to the other side's costs, which at the UK IPO is usually set on a published scale. You may still be able to use the brand in some circumstances, but the firm should advise on the risk first.
Does the UK IPO tell me about similar marks?
The UK IPO searches its records and may send you a report listing earlier marks that could be similar. You can then decide whether to go ahead. If you do, the owners of those earlier marks may be notified and have the chance to oppose.
How long do I have to reply to an examination report?
The report sets a deadline, usually a couple of months. It can sometimes be extended if there is a good reason. If you don't reply in time, the application may be refused, so pass the report to the firm as soon as you receive it.
Can I use evidence that my brand is already well known?
Yes. If the examiner objects that a mark is not distinctive, evidence that customers have come to recognise it through your use can sometimes overcome the objection. The evidence needs to be detailed, such as sales figures, advertising spend and examples of use over time. The firm will explain what is needed.
How long does a trademark last?
A UK registration lasts ten years from the filing date and can be renewed every ten years indefinitely, as long as the renewal fees are paid. There is a six month grace period after expiry with a late fee.
Do I have to use my registered trademark?
Yes, to keep it secure. If a registered mark is not genuinely used in the UK for the registered goods or services for a continuous five year period, anyone can apply to revoke it. Keep dated evidence of use.
Can I add new products to my registration?
No. To cover new goods or services you need a new application. Many businesses review their portfolio when they launch a new line and file again for the new classes.
Can I sell or license my registered mark?
Yes. A registered mark can be assigned, licensed or used as security, like other property. Assignments and licences should be in writing and recorded at the UK IPO so the register reflects who owns and uses the mark.
Does registration stop all similar names?
Not automatically. Registration gives you the right to act, but you need to watch for conflicts and take action. Others may also have earlier rights or be able to use similar signs in ways that don't cause confusion.
What should I do with my registration certificate?
Keep it safely with your business records, along with the filing receipt and any correspondence. Note the renewal date in your diary. Investors, buyers and online marketplaces may ask for your registration number, which you can also find on the public register.
Can I register a trademark for a brand I sell online only?
Yes. Online sellers can and often do register their marks, and many marketplaces offer extra brand protection tools to owners of registered trademarks. Make sure the specification covers both the goods you sell and, where relevant, your online retail services.

Using Leaflegal is free. The firm we introduce you to pays us for the introduction and will tell you about it in writing. Their fees and the official UK IPO fees are explained before you agree to anything.
Will the firm file the application for me?
If you instruct them, yes. They will usually advise on the mark first, perhaps run a search, agree the specification with you and then file and look after the application through to registration.
Can I get help if I have already filed myself?
Yes. Many people ask a firm for help after receiving an examination report or an opposition. Mention in the form that you have already filed so the firm can prepare.
What should I mention in the form?
Tell us what you want to protect, what you sell, where you trade and whether you have already filed or received any letters. If there is a launch date or a deadline, mention it. This helps the firm prepare for the first call and give you useful advice straight away.
Is the free UK IPO search enough?
It is a useful first check and costs nothing. It is good at finding identical marks on the UK register but is less reliable at finding marks that sound or look similar, and it doesn't cover company names, unregistered use or most international marks. For a launch or a filing, most businesses use a professional search as well.
What is a clearance search?
A clearance search checks whether a mark is clear to use and register, by looking for earlier marks and other rights that could cause a problem. It usually covers trademark registers, company names and use in the market. A professional then reviews the results and gives you a view on the risk.
What is the difference between a search and an opinion?
The search gathers the results. The opinion explains what they mean for you, which earlier marks matter, how serious the risk is and what you could do about it. Raw results without an opinion can be hard to interpret, so most businesses ask for both.
Can a search guarantee my brand is safe?
No search can guarantee that nobody will object. Registers change daily, unregistered rights are hard to find and judging similarity involves opinion. A good search greatly reduces the risk and helps you make an informed decision, but it is not a promise.
Does a search cover logos as well as names?
It can, but you need to ask for it. Logo searches use different tools and classification codes, so they are usually a separate part of the search. If your logo is central to the brand, ask the firm to include it.
How far back does a search go?
A register search covers all live marks, including old registrations that are still in force. It may also pick up recently expired marks that could be restored. Unregistered use is harder to date, which is one reason the firm's judgement matters.
Should I search company names too?
Yes, especially in the UK. A company trading under a similar name may have unregistered rights even if it never filed a trademark. Professional searches usually include Companies House and look at whether those companies are actually trading.
Can I search for a brand name on social media and marketplaces myself?
Yes, and it is a sensible early step. Searching the main marketplaces, app stores and social platforms can show whether others trade under a similar name. It won't tell you about legal rights, so it works best alongside a register search and an opinion from a professional.
