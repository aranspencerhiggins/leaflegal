About trademark searches

Before you invest in a new name, logo or product range, it pays to know whether someone else already has rights in something similar. The UK IPO’s free search tool shows identical marks, but it won’t reliably find marks that are similar in sound, look or meaning, unregistered rights or company names that could cause a problem.

A trademark professional runs a clearance search across the UK register and, where needed, international registers, company names and the wider market. They then give you a written view on the risks of using and registering your brand, so you can decide before you print or launch.

A trademark solicitor or chartered trade mark attorney checks whether your brand is free to use and register, files and prosecutes your application at the UK IPO, and acts for you if someone copies your brand or objects to your mark. When we introduce you, the firm will explain the process, the official fees and their own charges.

Trademark search in brief

A UK trademark search shows whether someone already has rights in a name or logo like yours. The free UK IPO search finds identical marks, but a professional trademark clearance search also looks for marks that sound, look or mean the same, plus company names and unregistered use.

A trademark attorney or solicitor turns the results into a clear view of the risk of using and registering your brand. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm to run your trademark search.

Trademark searches explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A trademark search tells you whether anyone else already has rights in a name or logo that is the same as, or similar to, the one you want to use. It is usually the first step before launching a brand, renaming a business, entering a new market or filing a trademark application. Finding a conflict early is far cheaper than discovering it after you have printed packaging, built a website and spent money on marketing.

The UK IPO offers a free online search of the UK register, and it is a useful first check. It is good at finding identical marks, but it is not designed to find every mark that sounds similar, looks similar or means the same thing. It also won't show unregistered rights, company names or businesses that trade under a name without registering it. A professional clearance search is designed to fill those gaps.

What a professional search looks for

  • Identical and similar marks on the UK register, including comparable marks that came from EU registrations
  • International registrations that cover the UK
  • Marks that sound alike, look alike or share a meaning, including translations
  • Company names at Companies House
  • Domain names and use on websites, marketplaces and social media
  • Pending applications that are not yet registered

The firm then reviews the results and gives you a view on the risk, rather than simply sending you a long list. That opinion is the most valuable part, because deciding whether two marks are likely to be confused involves judgement about the goods, the market and the way customers buy.

Why searching matters more than it used to

Starting a business and selling online is easier than ever, which means more brands, more applications and more chance that a name you like is already in use. Online marketplaces also act quickly on complaints from owners of registered marks, so a clash can lead to listings being removed with little warning. A search helps you avoid building a business on a name that someone else can take away.

Investors, buyers and retail partners increasingly ask whether a brand has been cleared and registered. Being able to show a search and a registration in the right name gives them confidence and can make negotiations smoother.

Not every situation needs the same depth of search. Firms usually offer a few levels, and the right one depends on how much you are about to invest and how crowded your market is.

Knock out search

A quick, low cost check for identical and very close marks on the relevant registers. It is useful for screening a shortlist of names and ruling out obvious problems before you spend more. It won't catch everything.

Full clearance search

A wider search of registers, company names and the market, looking for similar marks as well as identical ones. The firm reviews each relevant result and gives you a written opinion on the risk of using and registering the mark. This is the search most businesses commission before a launch or a major rebrand.

International searches

If you plan to trade abroad, the firm can search the registers in the countries that matter to you, often using local agents. Rules differ between countries, so a name that is clear in the UK may not be clear elsewhere.

Logo and image searches

Searching for logos is harder than searching for words, because registers classify images by their features, such as animals, stars or shapes. Firms use specialist tools and codes to look for similar devices. If the logo matters more than the name, tell the firm.

Watching after the search

A search is a snapshot on the day it is run. A watch service then monitors new applications and alerts you to marks that could conflict, so you can oppose them in time.

Most firms will recommend a level of search based on a short conversation about your plans. If you are only testing ideas, a screening search may be enough for now. If you are about to spend significant money, a full search is usually the safer choice.

Search results can look alarming, because almost every search finds something. What matters is whether the earlier marks are close enough, for similar enough goods or services, to create a real risk. The firm's opinion is designed to answer that question.

What the firm considers

  • How similar the marks look, sound and mean, judged as a whole
  • How similar the goods and services are, and how they are sold
  • How distinctive the earlier mark is, and whether it has a reputation
  • Whether the earlier mark is actually being used, or could be vulnerable to non use
  • Who owns the earlier mark and how they tend to protect it

The answer is rarely a simple yes or no. A firm might say the mark is clear, that it carries a low or moderate risk, or that it is likely to meet opposition. They may suggest ways to reduce the risk, such as narrowing what you sell under the brand, adding a distinctive element or approaching the owner of an earlier mark for consent.

If the search finds a problem

You might decide to change the name, adjust the mark, limit the goods or services, seek consent or a coexistence agreement, or challenge an earlier registration that has not been used. Each option has costs and risks, and the firm will explain them so you can choose.

Similarity in practice

Two marks don't need to be identical to clash. Marks can be similar because they sound alike when spoken, because they look alike on a label, or because they share an idea, such as a lion and a tiger for the same goods. A short mark with one different letter can be closer than it looks, while a long mark that shares a common word may be further away. The goods matter just as much, because similar marks for unrelated goods may happily coexist.

Customers also matter. Goods bought quickly and cheaply, such as snacks, are judged differently from goods bought carefully after research, such as specialist machinery. The firm will think about how your customers actually buy.

Once you have the opinion, take time to read it and ask questions. A good firm will be happy to talk through any result you don't understand, explain why they have rated a risk as they have and suggest practical next steps. The aim is a decision you are comfortable with, made with the full picture in front of you.

The cost of a search depends on how wide it is, how many classes it covers, how many countries are involved and how much analysis you want in the report. Knock out searches are usually the cheapest. Full clearance searches with a written opinion cost more, and international searches more again.

Many firms offer fixed fees for searches, and some include a basic search in a filing package. Ask exactly what the search covers, whether it includes unregistered use and company names, and whether the fee includes a written opinion or just the raw results.

Is a search worth it?

For a business about to invest in a brand, a search is usually a small cost compared with the risk of a forced rebrand. Rebranding can mean replacing stock, packaging, signage and websites, and losing the recognition you have built. A search also reduces the chance that your application will be opposed, which can be far more expensive than the search itself.

Getting good value

Give the firm a clear brief so the search is focused. Tell them exactly what you will sell, the countries that matter and whether the logo is important. A tightly scoped search costs less and is easier to act on than a broad one with hundreds of loosely relevant results. If you are choosing between several names, a screening search on all of them followed by a full search on one is often the most cost effective route.

The best time to search is before you commit money to a name. That usually means while you still have a shortlist, before you order packaging, register domains, build a website or sign a lease on a shop with your name above the door.

  • Before choosing a name for a new business or product
  • Before a rebrand or a new logo
  • Before filing a trademark application
  • Before expanding into a new country or a new range of goods
  • Before buying a business or a brand, as part of due diligence
  • When you receive a letter suggesting your name conflicts with someone else's

If you have already been trading for a while, a search is still useful. It can show whether your existing name is at risk and help you decide whether to file an application, approach another business or plan a change.

How long it takes

Knock out searches can often be done within a few days. A full clearance search with an opinion usually takes one to two weeks. International searches can take longer because local agents may be involved. If you have a launch date, tell the firm so they can plan.

Searching as part of a purchase

If you are buying a business, a product range or a brand, a search helps confirm that the seller really owns the marks they say they own, that the registrations are in force and in the right name, and that nobody else has a competing claim. The firm can review the trademarks as part of wider due diligence.

Trademark searches questions and answers

How do I do a UK trademark search?
You can search the UK IPO register online for free. For a launch or a filing, a professional clearance search is more thorough because it looks for similar marks as well as identical ones.
Isn’t the free UK IPO search enough?
It is a good first check, but it mainly finds identical or near identical marks. A professional search also looks for similar marks and judges how likely they are to cause a problem.
When should I search?
Before you spend money on branding, packaging or a website, and before you file a trademark application.
What is a trademark watch?
A service that alerts you when someone applies for a mark similar to yours, so you can oppose it in time.
Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Is the free UK IPO search enough?
It is a useful first check and costs nothing. It is good at finding identical marks on the UK register but is less reliable at finding marks that sound or look similar, and it doesn't cover company names, unregistered use or most international marks. For a launch or a filing, most businesses use a professional search as well.
What is a clearance search?
A clearance search checks whether a mark is clear to use and register, by looking for earlier marks and other rights that could cause a problem. It usually covers trademark registers, company names and use in the market. A professional then reviews the results and gives you a view on the risk.
What is the difference between a search and an opinion?
The search gathers the results. The opinion explains what they mean for you, which earlier marks matter, how serious the risk is and what you could do about it. Raw results without an opinion can be hard to interpret, so most businesses ask for both.
Can a search guarantee my brand is safe?
No search can guarantee that nobody will object. Registers change daily, unregistered rights are hard to find and judging similarity involves opinion. A good search greatly reduces the risk and helps you make an informed decision, but it is not a promise.
Does a search cover logos as well as names?
It can, but you need to ask for it. Logo searches use different tools and classification codes, so they are usually a separate part of the search. If your logo is central to the brand, ask the firm to include it.
How far back does a search go?
A register search covers all live marks, including old registrations that are still in force. It may also pick up recently expired marks that could be restored. Unregistered use is harder to date, which is one reason the firm's judgement matters.
Should I search company names too?
Yes, especially in the UK. A company trading under a similar name may have unregistered rights even if it never filed a trademark. Professional searches usually include Companies House and look at whether those companies are actually trading.
Can I search for a brand name on social media and marketplaces myself?
Yes, and it is a sensible early step. Searching the main marketplaces, app stores and social platforms can show whether others trade under a similar name. It won't tell you about legal rights, so it works best alongside a register search and an opinion from a professional.
Why do search results include marks in other classes?
Because goods in different classes can still be similar. Clothing and bags sit in different classes but are often sold together, and so are software and online services. A good search looks across related classes as well as the ones you will file in.
What if the search finds an identical mark?
It depends on the goods and services. An identical mark for completely different goods may not be a problem. An identical mark for the same goods usually is. The firm will look at the details, including whether the earlier mark is in use, and explain your options.
What does moderate risk mean?
It usually means there are earlier marks close enough to cause concern, but there are arguments either way. You might go ahead, adapt the mark or narrow your goods. The firm will explain what drives the risk and what would reduce it, so you can decide.
Can I use a name if the earlier mark isn't being used?
If a registered mark hasn't been genuinely used for five years, it may be vulnerable to revocation. That can open up options, but it needs care, because owners can sometimes restart use and evidence of use isn't always visible online. The firm can investigate.
Can I ask the owner of an earlier mark for permission?
Yes. Sometimes the owner will give consent or agree a coexistence arrangement, especially if your goods or markets are different. Approaching them alerts them to your plans, so the firm will usually advise on how and when to do it.
What if my search shows a company with the same name?
The firm will check whether the company is trading, what it does and where. A dormant company may pose little risk. An active business in the same field could have rights through use. The firm will explain how that affects your plans.
Will the search tell me if my mark is too descriptive?
A good opinion will comment on whether the mark is likely to be accepted by the examiner, as well as whether earlier marks could object. Both matter, because a mark can be clear of conflicts but still be refused for being descriptive.
What if an earlier mark belongs to a big brand?
Well known brands often enjoy wider protection, sometimes even against use on unrelated goods, and they tend to enforce their rights actively. A name that echoes a famous mark usually carries extra risk. The firm will flag this in the opinion and explain whether any use is likely to be safe.
Can two businesses use the same name in different places?
Within the UK, a registered mark covers the whole country, so local separation is not usually enough. Two businesses can sometimes coexist where their goods are different or they have agreed terms, and older local rights can survive in some cases. The firm will look at the facts.
What happens if I ignore a risky search result?
You might never hear anything, or you might receive a letter, an opposition or a marketplace complaint later, when changing name is more expensive. The firm can help you weigh the risk honestly, so any decision to go ahead is an informed one.
How long does a trademark search take?
A quick knock out search can take a day or two. A full clearance search with a written opinion usually takes one to two weeks. International searches may take longer. If you have a deadline, mention it in the form so the firm can plan.
How much does a trademark search cost?
It depends on the depth, the classes and the countries. Many firms quote a fixed fee for each level. Ask what is included, especially whether the fee covers a written opinion and checks on company names and unregistered use. The firm will confirm costs before starting.
Can I search several names at once?
Yes. Many businesses start with a quick screening search on a shortlist, then run a full search on the favourite. This keeps costs down and avoids paying for detailed searches on names that are ruled out quickly.
Should I search before or after choosing a designer?
Ideally before the final design is approved, and certainly before printing. A name can be checked early, and the logo can be checked once it is close to final. It is cheaper to adjust a design at concept stage.
Do I need to search again later?
If you expand into new goods or new countries, a fresh search for those areas is sensible. A watch service can also alert you to new marks that could conflict with yours over time.
Is a search included when I file a trademark?
Some firms include a basic search in their filing fee and others charge for it separately. Ask what is included. A search included in a filing package may be narrower than a full clearance search with a written opinion.
Can I get a search done urgently?
Many firms can turn around a screening search quickly if there is a real deadline, such as a printer's date or an investor meeting. Full clearance searches take longer because of the analysis involved. Mention any deadline in the form.
What should I do if the search is clear?
Many businesses file a trademark application straight away, so the filing date is secured. The firm can prepare the application using what they learned from the search. Searches go out of date as new applications are filed, so it is best not to wait too long.
What is a trademark watch?
A watch service monitors new trademark applications and alerts you when someone applies for a mark similar to yours. That gives you time to oppose before the mark is registered. Firms usually charge a yearly fee for watching.
Can I rely on a search done by my designer or branding agency?
Agencies sometimes run basic checks, which can be helpful. They are not usually a legal clearance search with an opinion from a regulated professional. If you are investing heavily, it is worth asking a trademark firm to review the position.
What if I am already trading and the search finds a problem?
You still have options. The firm may look at who used their name first, whether the earlier mark is used, whether the businesses really overlap and whether an agreement is possible. A search now is better than a letter from the other side later.
Should I keep a copy of the search report?
Yes. Keep it with your brand records. It shows that you took care before choosing the name, which can help if a dispute arises, and it is useful background if you later expand or sell the business.
Can the same firm handle my application and any dispute?
Usually yes. Many firms handle searching, filing and oppositions. If a dispute needs to go to court, a solicitor can act, and attorney firms often work with solicitors for that stage.

Yes, most trademark firms can arrange searches abroad, often through agents in other countries. Tell the firm where you plan to trade.

What is an examination report?
It is a letter from the UK IPO explaining why the examiner thinks your application cannot be accepted as filed. It sets out the objection and gives a deadline to respond. Many objections can be overcome by argument, evidence or changes to the list of goods and services, so it is worth getting advice before giving up.
How long do I have to respond to an objection?
The deadline is shown on the report and is usually around two months. Extensions may be possible in some circumstances. If you don't respond in time, the application may be refused. Passing the report to a firm promptly gives them the most time to prepare a good response.
Can I change my mark to overcome an objection?
Only in limited ways. You can't make a significant change to the mark after filing. Sometimes a minor amendment is allowed, but often the answer is to argue the point, narrow the goods or file a fresh application for a stronger version of the mark. The firm will explain the options.
What does it mean if my mark is descriptive?
It means the examiner thinks customers would see the mark as describing the goods or services, for example their type, quality or purpose, rather than as a brand. Descriptive marks are usually refused for the goods they describe. They may be acceptable for other goods where the meaning doesn't apply.
Can evidence of use overcome an objection?
Yes, sometimes. If a mark lacks distinctiveness on its face but customers have come to recognise it as your brand through use, detailed evidence can overcome the objection. It usually needs to show substantial use across the UK over time, so it is not always possible for newer brands.
What if the examiner refuses my application?
You may be able to ask for a hearing, appeal the decision or file a new application for a different version of the mark. The right step depends on the reasons for refusal and how important the mark is to you. The firm will explain the choices and their costs.
Can I talk to the examiner?
Yes, in many cases. Firms often call the examiner to understand the objection and test a possible solution before filing a written response. A short conversation can sometimes resolve a point that would otherwise take several letters.
Does an objection mean my brand is unsafe to use?
Not necessarily. An objection is about whether the mark can be registered. A mark refused as descriptive may still be usable, but you may find it hard to stop others using similar words. The firm can explain what the objection means for your business in practice.
