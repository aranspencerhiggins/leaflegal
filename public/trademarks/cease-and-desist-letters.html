A cease and desist letter is a formal demand that someone stops doing something, such as using your brand, copying your work or selling lookalike products. In the UK it is usually the first step in an IP dispute, and many disputes end with the letter.

Cease and desist letters explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A cease and desist letter is a formal written demand asking someone to stop doing something. In intellectual property disputes it is usually the first formal step. The letter explains what rights the sender has, what the recipient is doing that the sender objects to, what the sender wants them to do about it and by when.

In the UK, cease and desist letters are used for all kinds of brand and creative disputes. A business might send one to a competitor using a similar name, to a seller offering lookalike products online, to a website that has copied its photographs or text, or to a former partner who is still using its brand. Many disputes end with the letter, because the recipient agrees to stop, change or negotiate.

A cease and desist letter is not a court order and is not legally binding in itself. It is a demand backed by a threat of further action if the recipient doesn't comply. That is why it matters what the letter says, who it is sent to and whether the claims in it are justified.

Why the wording matters

UK law, now set out in the Intellectual Property (Unjustified Threats) Act 2017, allows a person who is threatened with infringement proceedings for trademarks, registered and unregistered designs or patents to sue the person making the threat in some circumstances, if the threat turns out to be unjustified. A letter that threatens the wrong person, such as a retailer rather than a manufacturer, or that goes beyond what the law permits, can lead to a claim against the sender.

There are safe ways to write these letters. The law allows certain permitted communications, for example giving notice that a right exists, or asking for information to find out whether a right is being infringed and by whom. Solicitors and trademark attorneys who handle these letters regularly know how to make the point firmly while staying within the rules.

Who can help

They will look at your rights and the other side's position and explain your options and fees before you decide anything.

Letters and the wider dispute

A cease and desist letter is rarely the whole story. It sits within a wider dispute that may also involve marketplace complaints, an opposition at the UK IPO, a domain name dispute or, occasionally, court proceedings. The letter often sets the tone for everything that follows, so it pays to get it right. A measured first letter can keep the door open to a sensible settlement, while an overblown one can harden positions and raise costs on both sides.

A good cease and desist letter is clear, accurate and proportionate. It should make the recipient take the matter seriously without overstating the case or creating unnecessary risk for the sender.

What a letter usually includes

  • Who the sender is and the rights they rely on, such as a registered trademark number
  • What the recipient is doing and why it infringes or causes a problem
  • What the sender wants, for example stopping use, removing listings or changing a name
  • Any undertakings the recipient is asked to sign
  • A deadline for a reply
  • What may happen if there is no satisfactory response

Choosing the tone

Not every situation calls for a strongly worded letter. If the other business is small and probably unaware of your rights, a firm but friendly approach may get a quicker result and avoid an expensive fight. If the copying is deliberate or the other side has ignored earlier contact, a more formal letter may be needed. The firm will help you decide.

Checking your own position first

Before sending, the firm will check that your rights are valid and cover what the other side is doing, that you own the rights in the right name, and that the other side cannot point to earlier rights of their own. A letter that provokes a counterclaim against a weak registration can leave you worse off.

Who to send it to

The unjustified threats rules treat manufacturers and importers differently from retailers and end users. Threats sent to primary infringers, such as the business making or importing the goods, are generally safer than threats sent to their customers. The firm will make sure the letter goes to the right party.

Preparing for the reply

Think in advance about how you will respond to the likely replies. The other side may agree, ask for time, deny infringement, point to their own earlier rights or simply ignore you. Having a plan for each makes it easier to act quickly and keeps the pressure on where that is needed.

Getting a cease and desist letter can be unsettling, particularly if you chose your name or created your content in good faith. The key is to respond calmly, within the deadline and with advice.

First steps

  • Note the deadline and diary it
  • Don't ignore the letter
  • Don't reply in anger or admit anything before getting advice
  • Keep the letter and any envelope or email it came in
  • Gather your own evidence, such as when you started using the name or created the work
  • Avoid deleting anything relevant, as it may be needed later

Assessing the claim

The firm will check whether the sender really has the rights they claim, whether those rights cover what you do, whether there is a real likelihood of confusion or genuine copying, and whether you have any defence or earlier rights of your own. Some letters are strong, some are weak and some are sent in the hope that the recipient will give in.

Your options

Depending on the assessment, you might deny the claim and explain why, propose changes, agree to stop by a reasonable date, negotiate a coexistence agreement, or challenge the sender's rights. If the letter makes an unjustified threat, you may even have a claim against the sender. The reply will be tailored to your situation.

Asking for more time

Deadlines in cease and desist letters are often short. If you need more time to take advice or gather information, it is usually reasonable to ask for an extension. A short, polite holding reply can be sent while the firm looks at the detail.

Insurance

Some business insurance policies include legal expenses cover for intellectual property disputes. If you receive a letter, check your policies and tell your insurer early, because late notice can affect cover. The firm can help you with the notification.

Remember that the letter is the other side's opening position. It will usually ask for everything they could hope to get. The reply is your chance to set out your side clearly, and many disputes move a long way from the original demands once both positions are on the table.

Many cease and desist letters ask the recipient to sign undertakings, which are formal written promises. Typical undertakings include agreeing to stop using a name or logo, to remove listings, to hand over or destroy stock, to transfer a domain name and to pay costs.

Undertakings are serious. They can be enforced like a contract, and an undertaking given to a court can be enforced as if it were a court order. Signing a broadly worded undertaking without advice can restrict your business more than necessary, for example by banning any use of a common word rather than just the specific mark in dispute.

Negotiating the terms

Undertakings can usually be negotiated. The firm might narrow the scope to the specific use in question, agree a sell off period for existing stock, limit the territory, remove any admission of liability or reduce any costs demanded. A well negotiated undertaking can settle a dispute while protecting your business.

If you are the sender

Asking for undertakings gives you a clear, enforceable outcome without going to court. The firm will draft undertakings that are wide enough to protect your brand but not so wide that the other side refuses to sign.

After undertakings are signed

Once undertakings are given, both sides should keep a copy and diary any dates, such as the end of a sell off period. The recipient should make sure staff, designers and suppliers know about the changes, because a breach by mistake is still a breach. The sender should check from time to time that the undertakings are being kept, and take advice if they are not.

What does cease and desist mean?
It means stop and do not start again. A cease and desist letter asks the other side to stop a particular activity, and usually to confirm in writing that they will.
Is a cease and desist letter legally binding?
No. It is a formal demand, not a court order. Ignoring a well founded letter can still lead to a court claim and costs.
Can I write my own cease and desist letter?
You can, but in IP disputes the rules on unjustified threats mean a poorly worded letter could expose you to a claim. Many people use a solicitor for this reason.
What should I do if I receive one?
Don’t ignore it and don’t reply in anger. Note the deadline and get advice on whether the claim is valid before you respond.

Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?

What does cease and desist mean?
It means stop and do not start again. A cease and desist letter asks the recipient to stop a particular activity, such as using a name or copying content, and usually to confirm in writing that they won't do it again.
Is a cease and desist letter legally binding?
No. It is a formal demand, not a court order. The recipient doesn't have to comply, but ignoring a well founded letter can lead to a court claim, and a court may take into account how each side behaved when deciding costs.
Do I need a solicitor to send a cease and desist letter?
You can write your own, but in IP disputes the unjustified threats rules mean a badly worded letter can expose you to a claim. A letter from a solicitor or trademark attorney also tends to be taken more seriously. Many businesses use a professional for this reason.
What is an unjustified threat?
It is a threat to sue someone for infringing a trademark, design or patent where the threat turns out not to be justified. In some circumstances the person threatened can sue for a declaration, an injunction and damages. Certain communications are permitted, and professionals know how to stay within the rules.
Do the threats rules apply to copyright?
The statutory unjustified threats rules apply to trademarks, designs and patents, not copyright. Copyright letters still need care, because exaggerated or inaccurate claims can damage your position and your reputation, but the specific threats regime does not apply.
Can I send a cease and desist letter by email?
Yes. Many letters are sent by email as well as post. The method matters less than the content, the recipient and keeping a record of when it was sent and received.
How long should the deadline be?
It should be reasonable in the circumstances. Around fourteen days is common. A shorter deadline may be justified where harm is ongoing or urgent. Unreasonably short deadlines can look aggressive and may count against the sender later.
What is a letter before claim?
It is a letter sent before court proceedings begin, setting out the claim in detail and inviting a response. Court rules expect parties to exchange information before suing. A cease and desist letter can serve as the first step, and a fuller letter before claim may follow if the dispute continues.
What should my cease and desist letter say?
It should identify your rights, describe the other side's activity, explain why it infringes or causes a problem, set out what you want them to do and give a deadline. It should be accurate and proportionate. The firm will tailor it to the situation.
Who should I send the letter to?
Usually the business responsible for the infringing activity, such as the manufacturer, importer or the business trading under the name. Sending threats to retailers or customers carries more risk under the unjustified threats rules. The firm will advise on the right recipient.
What if they ignore my letter?
You can send a follow up, take other steps such as platform takedowns or an opposition, or consider court proceedings. The right next step depends on the strength of your case and how much the issue matters to your business. The firm will explain your options.
Can I ask for compensation in the letter?
Yes, you can ask for damages or costs, but it is often better to focus on stopping the activity first. Demanding large sums early can make settlement harder. The firm will suggest what is realistic in your case.
Should I send a letter if my trademark isn't registered?
You may still have rights in passing off or copyright. The letter needs to be carefully framed around the rights you actually have, and you will need evidence of goodwill. The firm will advise on whether a letter is sensible and how to word it.
Can a cease and desist letter make things worse?
It can, if it is inaccurate, threatens the wrong person or provokes the other side to challenge your rights. That is why it helps to check your own position first. A carefully judged letter is usually the most effective first step.
Can I send a letter to a seller on a marketplace?
Yes, but it is often more effective to use the marketplace's reporting tools first, particularly if you have a registered trademark. If the seller keeps relisting, a letter to the business behind the listings may be the next step. The firm will advise on the order.
Should I mention court in the letter?
Letters usually say that further action may follow if the recipient doesn't comply. How this is phrased matters, especially for trademarks and designs, because of the threats rules. The firm will word it so it is clear but within what the law allows.
What should I do if I receive a cease and desist letter?
Don't ignore it and don't reply in anger. Note the deadline, keep the letter and get advice. The firm will assess the claim and help you respond in a way that protects your position. Many disputes are resolved by correspondence.
Do I have to stop what I'm doing straight away?
Not necessarily. It depends on how strong the claim is and the risk of continuing. The firm may advise stopping, making changes or carrying on while the claim is examined. Each option has different risks, which they will explain.
Can I ask for more time to reply?
Yes. If you need time to take advice or gather information, a polite request for an extension is usually reasonable. A short holding reply can be sent while the firm reviews the letter.
What if the letter is wrong?
If the claim is unfounded, the firm can reply explaining why, and in some trademark or design cases the letter may itself amount to an unjustified threat. Replying firmly and accurately often ends a weak claim.
Should I sign the undertakings they've sent?
Not without advice. Undertakings are binding promises and are often drafted widely. They can usually be negotiated to cover only what is reasonable, with time to sell off stock and without admissions of liability.
Will I have to pay their legal costs?
Letters often ask for costs, but you don't automatically have to pay them. Whether costs are payable depends on the strength of the claim and how the dispute is resolved. The firm can negotiate this as part of any settlement.
Should I tell the sender I've passed the letter to a solicitor?
Often yes. A short note that you have instructed a firm and will reply in due course can buy time and shows you are taking the matter seriously. The firm can send this for you.
Can I challenge the sender's trademark?
Possibly. If their mark hasn't been used for five years or should never have been registered, it may be revoked or declared invalid. A credible challenge can change the balance of negotiations significantly.
Is it a good idea to ring the sender?
It is usually better to let the firm handle contact, at least at first. Informal calls can lead to admissions or misunderstandings. Once the firm has assessed the claim, a call between the professionals on each side is often a quick way to explore a settlement.
I used a photo from the internet and received a demand. What now?
Image claims are common. Check whether the image was used, who owns it and whether the amount claimed is reasonable. Don't ignore it and don't pay before checking. The firm can review the claim and negotiate or reply.
A marketplace removed my listings after a complaint. Is that a cease and desist?
Not exactly, but it often comes from a similar complaint by a rights holder to the platform. You may be able to file a counter notice. The firm can look at the complaint and advise on getting listings restored or resolving the dispute.
Can I send a letter about a business name?
Yes. Letters about trading names, company names and domain names are common. Depending on the facts, the remedy might involve changing the name, using the Company Names Tribunal or a domain dispute procedure.
What if the other side is a much bigger business?
Larger businesses often send firm letters, but that doesn't mean their claim is right. The firm will assess it on its merits. A well reasoned reply can level the playing field, and sometimes a negotiated outcome suits both sides.
What if the sender is a former business partner?
Disputes between former partners, distributors or franchisees often turn on the agreements between them and who owns the brand. Bring any contracts to the first call. The firm will look at both the IP and the contractual position.
Can a letter be about copied website content?
Yes. Letters about copied text, images and designs on websites are common. They usually rely on copyright and ask for the content to be removed. Website hosts also have procedures for removing copied content, which can be used alongside or instead of a letter.

This service is for brand, copyright and design disputes. For unpaid business invoices, Leaflegal can introduce you to a debt recovery firm instead. Choose the right area in the form.
Will the firm send the letter in their own name?
Usually yes. Letters sent on a law firm's or attorney's letterhead tend to be taken more seriously, and replies go to the firm rather than to you directly, which can take some of the stress out of the dispute.
When do I need to renew my trademark?
Every ten years from the filing date of the application. You can renew up to six months before the expiry date, and there is a six month late period after expiry in which you can still renew by paying an extra fee.
Will the UK IPO remind me?
The UK IPO usually sends a reminder to the address on the register. It is still your responsibility to renew. If your address has changed, or the reminder goes to a former adviser, you may never see it. Many owners ask a firm to track dates for them.
Can I renew only some classes?
Yes. You can choose to renew only the classes or goods and services you still need. This can save money. The firm can help you weigh the saving against the value of keeping wider protection.
What happens if I renew late?
If you renew within six months after expiry, you pay a late fee and the registration continues as if it had been renewed on time. After that, the mark is removed from the register and you would need to ask for restoration or file again.
Can I renew a trademark myself?
Yes. Renewals can be done online through the UK IPO. Many owners use a firm when they have several marks, when the specification needs reviewing or when they want someone else to keep track of dates.
Does renewing change my rights?
No. Renewal simply continues the registration for another ten years. It doesn't widen the goods and services or change the mark. If you need wider protection, you need a new application.
Should I renew a mark I don't use any more?
It depends. Keeping an unused mark costs renewal fees and it becomes vulnerable to revocation after five years without use. Sometimes it is still worth keeping for defensive or future reasons. The firm can help you decide.
Can I renew early to get it out of the way?
You can renew from six months before the expiry date, but not earlier. The renewal runs for ten years from the expiry date, so renewing early within that window doesn't shorten your protection.
