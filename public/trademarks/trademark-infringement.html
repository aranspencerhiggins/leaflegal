About trademark infringement

Trademark infringement happens when someone uses a sign that is identical or confusingly similar to a registered mark, for similar goods or services, without permission. If your mark is not registered, you may still have a claim in passing off, which protects the goodwill a business has built up.

A trademark solicitor can look at the strength of your rights and the evidence of use, send a formal letter setting out your rights, ask online marketplaces to remove listings and, if needed, bring a claim in the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court or the High Court. If you have been accused, they can tell you how strong the claim is and how to respond.

A trademark solicitor or chartered trade mark attorney checks whether your brand is free to use and register, files and prosecutes your application at the UK IPO, and acts for you if someone copies your brand or objects to your mark. Trademark infringement in brief

Trademark infringement is using a sign identical or similar to a registered trademark for similar goods or services without permission. If your brand is not registered, passing off can protect the goodwill you have built.

A trademark infringement solicitor assesses both sides, deals with marketplace and social media takedowns and can bring or defend a claim. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm that handles brand infringement.

Trademark infringement explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Few things are more frustrating for a business owner than finding someone else trading on the name or look they have worked hard to build. Equally, few things are more worrying than a letter claiming that your own brand infringes someone else's. Trademark infringement work covers both sides of that coin.

Trademark infringement happens when someone uses a sign in the course of trade that is identical or similar to a registered trademark, for goods or services that are identical or similar to those the mark is registered for, in a way that is likely to confuse the public. Marks with a reputation get wider protection and can be infringed by use that takes unfair advantage of that reputation or harms it, even for different goods.

If a brand is not registered, the owner may still have a claim in passing off. Passing off protects the goodwill a business has built up, and stops others misrepresenting their goods or services as connected with it. It is usually harder and more expensive to prove than infringement of a registered mark, because the owner has to show goodwill, misrepresentation and damage with evidence.

Common situations

  • A competitor starts using a similar name or logo
  • Lookalike products appear on online marketplaces
  • A former distributor or partner keeps using your brand after the relationship ends
  • Someone registers a domain name or social media handle using your brand
  • A new business opens nearby with a confusingly similar name
  • You receive a letter claiming your brand infringes someone else's

Who handles these cases

Infringement disputes are usually handled by trademark solicitors, who can take a claim to court if needed, and by chartered trade mark attorneys, many of whom work closely with solicitors for court work. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm that handles brand disputes. They will look at both sides' rights and explain your options and their fees before you decide anything.

Brand disputes are often emotional, particularly for owners who have put years into a name. A good firm will help you separate the commercial question, which is what outcome you actually need, from the legal question of what you could win. Keeping both in view usually leads to a quicker and cheaper resolution.

When you discover that someone is using your brand, the instinct is often to act immediately. Taking a little time to gather evidence and get advice usually leads to a better result.

Gather evidence

Take screenshots that show the web address and date, buy a sample product if appropriate, keep any messages from confused customers and note when you first became aware of the use. Evidence of actual confusion is particularly valuable.

Check your own rights

The firm will look at what you have registered, what goods and services the registration covers, when you started using the brand and whether there are any weaknesses, such as a registration that hasn't been used for some of its goods. Understanding your own position first avoids surprises later.

Consider the options

  • A friendly first approach, where the other side may simply not know about your rights
  • A formal letter setting out your rights and asking them to stop
  • Takedown requests to marketplaces, platforms or domain registries
  • Opposing their trademark application, if they have filed one
  • A court claim seeking an injunction, damages or an account of profits

Many cases end at the letter stage, with the other side agreeing to stop, change their branding or sell off stock by an agreed date. Court is usually the last resort.

Watch out for unjustified threats

UK law allows people to sue for unjustified threats of trademark infringement proceedings in some circumstances. A letter that threatens the wrong person, or goes further than the law allows, can backfire. This is a key reason to have a professional prepare or check any letter.

Think about the outcome you want

Before sending anything, decide what a good result looks like. You might want the other side to stop immediately, to change their name within a few months, to stop selling particular products, or to pay compensation. Being clear about the goal helps the firm choose the right approach and helps you judge any offer that comes back.

Receiving a letter alleging infringement can be alarming, especially if you chose your name in good faith. The allegations may be strong, weak or somewhere in between. A calm, informed response is the best way to protect your position.

Don't ignore it

Ignoring a well founded complaint can lead to a court claim and higher costs. Ignoring the deadline can also make you look unreasonable, which can matter later on costs. Equally, don't reply in anger or admit anything before getting advice.

What the firm will look at

  • Whether the other side's mark is registered, for what goods, and whether it is in use
  • How similar the marks and the goods really are
  • Whether you have any earlier rights or a defence
  • Whether their registration could be challenged
  • What they are actually asking you to do and by when

Possible responses

Depending on the strength of the claim, the response might deny infringement and explain why, propose changes to your branding, offer a period to sell off stock and rebrand, or open negotiations about coexistence. Sometimes a careful reply leads the other side to drop the complaint.

Defences

There are limited defences, for example using your own name honestly, using words to describe the characteristics of your goods, or using a mark to indicate what a product is compatible with, provided the use is in line with honest practices. Whether any of these apply depends on the facts.

Rebranding on your own terms

If the claim against you is strong, an agreed rebrand can be far better than losing in court. A negotiated timetable lets you use up existing stock, redirect your website and tell customers properly. The firm can negotiate terms that protect your business through the change.

It also helps to look at the commercial picture. How important is the name to your business, how much stock or marketing is tied up in it, and how difficult would a change be? The answers shape the best response, and the firm will ask about them.

A large share of brand disputes now happen online. Copycat listings on marketplaces, lookalike products advertised on social media and domain names that use a brand are all common.

Platform reporting tools

Most large marketplaces and social media platforms have procedures for reporting listings that infringe a registered trademark or copyright. A registered trademark makes these much more effective. Some marketplaces run brand registry schemes that give owners of registered marks extra tools to protect their listings.

Counter notices

If a listing is removed, the seller may file a counter notice disputing the complaint. The platform may then restore the listing unless the complainant takes further action. The firm can advise on what to do if this happens.

Domain names

Disputes over .uk domain names can often be resolved through Nominet's dispute resolution service, which is quicker and cheaper than court. Disputes over many other domain endings use a separate international procedure. Both require you to show rights in the name and that the registration is abusive or in bad faith.

Counterfeits

If the goods are counterfeits, there may be criminal offences as well as civil claims, and Trading Standards may be interested. Customs can also be asked to watch for counterfeit goods entering the UK. The firm can explain these options.

Online enforcement works best as a routine rather than a one off. Many businesses set up regular monitoring of the main marketplaces and social platforms, with a clear process for reporting new listings. The firm can help design a process that fits your budget.

Why registration helps online

Platforms generally ask for a registration number before they act on a trademark complaint. Without a registration, owners often have to rely on copyright in images or text, or on passing off, which platforms are less able to assess. Registering your main brand before problems arise makes online enforcement much quicker.

If a dispute can't be resolved by agreement, the owner may bring a claim in court. In England and Wales, smaller and simpler intellectual property claims are often heard in the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court, where the amount of costs one side can recover from the other is capped and the procedure is designed to be quicker. Larger and more complex cases go to the High Court. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own courts.

What a court can order

  • An injunction to stop the infringing use
  • Damages, or an account of the profits the infringer made
  • Delivery up or destruction of infringing goods
  • Publication of the judgment
  • A contribution to the winner's legal costs

Costs

Disputes are usually charged in stages, starting with a review and a letter, then negotiation, and only then court proceedings if needed. Court claims are a significant investment, and the losing side usually pays a share of the winner's costs, so it is important to understand the risks before starting. Some firms offer fixed fees for early stages. Ask for an estimate for each stage.

Trademark infringement questions and answers

What is passing off?
A claim that protects unregistered brands. You need to show goodwill in your business, a misrepresentation by the other side that is likely to confuse customers, and damage as a result.
What if my trademark isn’t registered?
You may still be able to stop someone using a similar name through a passing off claim, if you can show goodwill, a misrepresentation and damage. It is usually harder to prove than infringement of a registered mark.
Can I be sued for using a name I didn’t know was taken?
Yes. Infringement doesn’t depend on whether you knew about the earlier mark, although it can affect what remedies a court gives.
Where are trademark cases heard?
In England and Wales, smaller and simpler cases often go to the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court, where costs are capped. Larger cases go to the High Court.
Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?

What counts as trademark infringement?
Using a sign that is identical or similar to a registered mark, for identical or similar goods or services, in a way likely to cause confusion, without consent. Use must be in the course of trade. For marks with a reputation, use that takes unfair advantage of or harms that reputation can also infringe. Whether a particular use infringes depends on the facts.
Does the other side have to have copied deliberately?
No. Infringement doesn't depend on intention. A business can infringe even if it chose its name innocently without knowing about the earlier mark. Intention can affect the remedies and how a court views the case, but it is not needed for infringement.
What is passing off?
Passing off protects unregistered brands. The owner must show goodwill in the business, a misrepresentation by the other side that leads or is likely to lead customers to think the goods are connected, and damage as a result. It needs more evidence than a registered trademark claim.
Is using a similar logo infringement?
It can be. Logos are compared as a whole, looking at visual, aural and conceptual similarity. A logo that copies the distinctive parts of a registered logo, for similar goods, may infringe even if some details are different. The firm will compare them carefully.
Can I stop someone using my brand for different goods?
Usually only if the goods are similar, or if your mark has a reputation and their use would take unfair advantage of it or harm it. Different goods often mean no infringement, but well known brands can get wider protection.
Is it infringement to mention a competitor's brand?
Not always. Honest comparative advertising, describing compatibility and genuine resale of branded goods can be allowed within limits. Using a competitor's mark in a way that suggests a connection or takes unfair advantage can cross the line. The details matter.
Can a trademark be infringed outside the UK?
A UK registration protects against use in the UK. Use abroad is governed by the law of that country. Online sales targeted at UK customers can count as use in the UK, even if the seller is based overseas.
What is a mark with a reputation?
A mark that is known by a significant part of the relevant public for the goods or services it covers. Such marks can be protected against use on dissimilar goods where that use takes unfair advantage of, or harms, the mark's reputation or distinctive character. Proving reputation needs evidence.
Does selling genuine second hand goods infringe?
Generally no. Once genuine goods have been put on the market in the UK or the European Economic Area by or with the consent of the owner, reselling them is usually allowed. There are exceptions, for example where the goods have been changed or the resale damages the reputation of the mark.
What should I do first if someone copies my brand?
Gather evidence, including dated screenshots and samples, and get advice before contacting the other side. The firm will check your rights and suggest the best first step. Acting quickly can matter, because long delay can sometimes weaken your position.
Should I contact the other business myself?
It is usually better to take advice first. A friendly message may work, but a poorly worded one can trigger an unjustified threats claim or reveal weaknesses in your position. The firm can help you decide the right tone and content.
Can I get an urgent injunction?
In clear and urgent cases, a court can grant an interim injunction to stop infringing use quickly, before a full trial. Courts expect the claimant to act promptly and may require an undertaking to pay damages if the injunction turns out to be wrong. The firm can tell you whether this is realistic.
What can I recover if I win?
Remedies can include an injunction, damages or the infringer's profits, delivery up or destruction of infringing goods, and a contribution to your legal costs. The amount depends on the evidence of loss or profit. The firm will give you a realistic view.
How long do I have to bring a claim?
Claims for damages are generally subject to a six year limitation period from the infringing acts. However, waiting a long time before acting can make it harder to get an injunction and can weaken your case in other ways. It is best not to delay.
What if the infringer is overseas?
If they sell into the UK, you may be able to act here, and platform takedowns work regardless of where the seller is. Enforcing a judgment abroad is harder. The firm can explain the practical options.
What is an account of profits?
Instead of damages, a successful claimant can sometimes choose to recover the profits the infringer made from the infringing use. It can be useful where the infringer earned more than the claimant lost. Working out the profits can be complex.
Will the other side have to pay my legal costs?
If you win in court, you are usually awarded a contribution to your costs, but rarely all of them. In the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court recoverable costs are capped. Settlements often include an agreement about costs. The firm will explain what you might realistically recover.
Can I deal with infringement without going to court?
Yes, and most cases are. Letters, negotiation, platform takedowns, oppositions and domain dispute procedures all resolve disputes without court. Mediation is also available. Court proceedings are usually the last step, used when other routes have failed or speed is essential.
I've received a letter saying I infringe. What should I do?
Note the deadline, keep the letter and get advice before responding. The firm will assess the strength of the claim and help you reply in a way that protects your position. Many complaints are resolved through correspondence without court.
Do I have to stop using my brand immediately?
Not necessarily. It depends on how strong the claim is and what the risks are of carrying on. The firm may suggest continuing while the dispute is assessed, making changes or agreeing a phased rebrand. Each option carries different risks.
Can I challenge the other side's trademark?
Possibly. If their mark hasn't been used for five years, it may be revoked for non use. If it should never have been registered, it may be declared invalid. A successful challenge can remove or narrow the rights they rely on.
What if I was using my name first?
Earlier use can give you rights of your own and sometimes a defence, particularly if you have built up local or national goodwill before their mark was filed. The details matter, so evidence of your early use is very important.
Will I have to pay damages?
If you are found to infringe, you may have to pay damages or account for profits, as well as costs. A negotiated settlement often avoids this, particularly if you agree to stop or change your branding. The firm will explain your exposure.
Can I use my own surname as a brand?
There is a defence for honest use of your own name in some cases, but it is narrow and applies to individuals rather than company names in most situations. It doesn't permit use that suggests a connection with another business. The firm will look at the facts.
Can a marketplace remove my listings because of a complaint?
Yes. Platforms often remove listings when they receive a complaint from a trademark owner, sometimes without looking closely at the merits. You can usually file a counter notice if the complaint is wrong. The firm can help you respond and get listings restored where appropriate.
What is an undertaking?
An undertaking is a formal promise, for example to stop using a name or to destroy infringing stock. Letters often ask for signed undertakings. Breaking an undertaking given to a court can be serious, so get advice before signing anything.
How do I remove copycat listings from a marketplace?
Use the platform's reporting process, usually supported by your registration number. A registered trademark makes this much easier. The firm can file reports, respond to counter notices and help you decide whether further action is needed.
Someone has registered a domain with my brand. What can I do?
For .uk domains, Nominet's dispute resolution service can transfer or cancel abusive registrations. For many other endings, an international procedure applies. Both are usually quicker and cheaper than court. The firm will explain what you need to show.
Can I stop someone using my brand on social media?
Social media platforms have trademark reporting tools, and impersonation accounts can often be removed. A registered mark makes reports more effective. Persistent misuse may need a formal letter or further action.
What about counterfeit goods?
Counterfeits can involve criminal offences as well as civil claims. Owners can involve Trading Standards and ask customs to watch for counterfeits at the border. The firm can explain these options alongside civil action.
Can I use a competitor's brand as an advertising keyword?
Bidding on a competitor's trademark as a search keyword is not automatically infringement, but the advert must not suggest a connection with the brand owner or confuse users. The wording of the advert matters a great deal.

How quickly will someone contact me?
Usually within one working day. If there is a deadline or ongoing damage to your business, mention it in the form so the firm can prioritise.
What details should I give in the form?
Tell us whether you are the brand owner or have been accused, where the use is happening, whether your brand is registered and any deadline. This helps the firm understand the situation before the first call.
What does cease and desist mean?
It means stop and do not start again. A cease and desist letter asks the recipient to stop a particular activity, such as using a name or copying content, and usually to confirm in writing that they won't do it again.
Is a cease and desist letter legally binding?
No. It is a formal demand, not a court order. The recipient doesn't have to comply, but ignoring a well founded letter can lead to a court claim, and a court may take into account how each side behaved when deciding costs.
Do I need a solicitor to send a cease and desist letter?
You can write your own, but in IP disputes the unjustified threats rules mean a badly worded letter can expose you to a claim. A letter from a solicitor or trademark attorney also tends to be taken more seriously. Many businesses use a professional for this reason.
What is an unjustified threat?
It is a threat to sue someone for infringing a trademark, design or patent where the threat turns out not to be justified. In some circumstances the person threatened can sue for a declaration, an injunction and damages. Certain communications are permitted, and professionals know how to stay within the rules.
Do the threats rules apply to copyright?
The statutory unjustified threats rules apply to trademarks, designs and patents, not copyright. Copyright letters still need care, because exaggerated or inaccurate claims can damage your position and your reputation, but the specific threats regime does not apply.
Can I send a cease and desist letter by email?
Yes. Many letters are sent by email as well as post. The method matters less than the content, the recipient and keeping a record of when it was sent and received.
How long should the deadline be?
It should be reasonable in the circumstances. Around fourteen days is common. A shorter deadline may be justified where harm is ongoing or urgent. Unreasonably short deadlines can look aggressive and may count against the sender later.
What is a letter before claim?
It is a letter sent before court proceedings begin, setting out the claim in detail and inviting a response. Court rules expect parties to exchange information before suing. A cease and desist letter can serve as the first step, and a fuller letter before claim may follow if the dispute continues.
