About renewals and ownership changes

UK trademarks last ten years and can be renewed indefinitely, every ten years, as long as the renewal fee is paid. You can renew up to six months before the expiry date, and there is a six month late period with an extra fee. After that, the mark can be removed from the register, although restoration is sometimes possible.

Trademark professionals also record changes of owner when a business is sold or restructured, update addresses, grant licences, and check whether old registrations are vulnerable to cancellation because they haven’t been used for five years.

A trademark solicitor or chartered trade mark attorney checks whether your brand is free to use and register, files and prosecutes your application at the UK IPO, and acts for you if someone copies your brand or objects to your mark. Trademark renewal in brief

A UK trademark lasts ten years and can be renewed again and again. Trademark renewal can be done up to six months before expiry, with a further six month late period for an extra fee.

A trademark attorney or solicitor can renew your marks, record a change of owner after a sale and review whether older registrations are still protected. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm.

Renewals and ownership changes explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A registered trademark is a long term asset, but it doesn't look after itself. UK registrations last for ten years from the filing date and must be renewed to stay in force. The register also needs to show the right owner and up to date details, and the mark needs to be used to stay safe from challenge. Renewals and ownership work keeps all of that in order.

This work is often less urgent than a dispute, but it matters just as much. A registration that lapses because a renewal was missed, or that sits in the name of a company that no longer exists, can cause real problems when you need to enforce it, raise investment or sell the business. Putting things right later is usually possible but can be slower and more expensive.

What this work covers

  • Renewing UK registrations on time
  • Late renewals within the six month grace period
  • Restoring registrations that have been removed from the register
  • Recording a new owner after a sale, merger or restructure
  • Recording licences and security interests
  • Updating names and addresses
  • Reviewing a portfolio for gaps, overlaps and non use risks
  • Renewing international registrations alongside UK ones

Who handles it

Many chartered trade mark attorneys and trademark solicitors manage portfolios for clients, keeping track of renewal dates and handling recordals as a routine service. Why it often gets missed

Ten years is a long time in the life of a business. Staff change, advisers retire, companies move offices and the person who filed the original application may have left. It is easy for a renewal reminder to go to an old address or an inbox nobody checks. Many owners only discover a problem when they try to enforce a mark, apply for a marketplace brand programme or answer questions from a buyer. Having one person or firm responsible for the portfolio solves most of these problems.

A little routine housekeeping goes a long way. Knowing what you own, keeping the register up to date and renewing on time means that when you need your trademarks, they are ready to use.

A UK trademark registration lasts ten years from its filing date. It can be renewed for further ten year periods indefinitely, as long as the renewal fee is paid each time. There is no limit to the number of renewals, which is why some marks are more than a century old.

When you can renew

Renewal can be made up to six months before the expiry date. The UK IPO usually sends a reminder to the owner or their representative, but it is the owner's responsibility to renew, so it is best not to rely on the reminder alone. Firms that manage portfolios track dates and contact you well before each deadline.

The late period

If the expiry date passes without renewal, there is a further six month period in which the mark can still be renewed on payment of an additional late fee. During this time the registration remains on the register, so acting within the late period keeps your rights intact.

Renewing part of a registration

You don't have to renew every class or every item in the specification. If the business no longer uses the mark for some goods or services, it may make sense to renew only the classes that matter. The firm can help you decide, taking into account the cost and the risk of a non use challenge for goods you no longer sell.

Renewing international registrations

International registrations under the Madrid system are renewed centrally through the World Intellectual Property Organization, on their own cycle. If you hold both UK and international registrations, the firm can co-ordinate the dates so nothing is missed.

Checking before you renew

Renewal is a good moment to look at each mark. Is it still used? Does the specification still match what the business sells? Is it in the right owner's name? Fixing ownership or address details before renewal avoids complications. If the business has moved on to a new brand, the firm can help you decide whether the old mark is still worth renewing.

Renewal is also a useful prompt to think about protection abroad. If the business has started selling overseas since the mark was first filed, the firm can advise on extending protection to the markets that now matter.

If a mark isn't renewed within the late period, it is removed from the register. That doesn't always mean it is lost for good.

Restoration

An owner can usually ask the UK IPO to restore a removed registration within six months of the date it was removed. The request must explain why the renewal was missed, and the UK IPO will consider whether the failure to renew was unintentional. Restoration involves an extra fee and is at the UK IPO's discretion, so it is not guaranteed.

Third party rights

While a mark is off the register, someone else may start using or applying for a similar mark. If the registration is restored, there may be questions about those intervening rights. Acting quickly reduces this risk.

Filing again

If restoration isn't possible, the owner can file a fresh application. The new application will have a new filing date, which means any third party who filed in the meantime may have priority. It will also be examined again and published for opposition. For an important mark, the firm may recommend both seeking restoration and filing a new application as a back up.

Avoiding lapses

  • Keep one up to date list of all your marks and their renewal dates
  • Make sure the UK IPO has your current address or a representative's address
  • Diary renewals at least six months in advance
  • Review whether each mark is still needed before it falls due
  • Ask a firm to manage renewals if you have several marks

What to do if you find a lapse

If you discover that a mark has lapsed, act straight away. Note when you found out and gather anything that explains why the renewal was missed, such as a change of address or a missed reminder. Contact a firm promptly, because the time limits for late renewal and restoration are short and fixed. The firm can also check whether anyone has filed a similar mark in the meantime.

When a business is sold, merged or restructured, its trademarks often change hands. The change should be documented in writing and recorded at the UK IPO, so the register shows the right owner.

Why recording matters

An unrecorded transfer can cause practical problems. The new owner may find it harder to enforce the mark, to deal with online platforms or to prove ownership to investors and buyers. In some circumstances, the right to recover costs or damages for infringement before the transfer is recorded can be affected. Recording the assignment promptly avoids these issues.

Common situations

  • Selling a business or a brand
  • Moving marks from an owner's personal name into a company
  • Transferring marks between group companies
  • Merging with another business
  • Receiving marks as part of an insolvency sale

Licences and security

Licences of a registered mark, and security interests such as a charge in favour of a lender, can also be recorded. Recording a licence can strengthen the licensee's position. Lenders often require their security interest to be recorded.

Name and address changes

If your company changes its name or address, the register should be updated. This is a simple step but is often overlooked, and outdated details can mean you miss important letters from the UK IPO or other parties.

Timing on a sale

On a business sale, the trademark assignment is usually signed at completion, alongside the other transfer documents. It should then be recorded at the UK IPO promptly. Buyers often make recording a condition of the deal, and sellers benefit from a clean handover. The firm can work with the corporate lawyers on the transaction to make sure the IP side is covered.

Getting ownership right also matters for online selling. Marketplace brand programmes usually check that the applicant matches the owner shown on the register. If your marks are still in an old company name or a personal name, the application may be refused until the register is updated.

For businesses with several marks, a periodic review can save money and close gaps. Firms usually look at what you own, what you use and what you need.

What a review covers

  • Whether each mark is in the right owner's name
  • Whether the specifications still match what the business sells
  • Whether any marks or classes are no longer needed
  • Whether new brands, products or markets need protection
  • Whether any registrations are vulnerable to non use challenges
  • Upcoming renewal dates and budgets

The five year use rule

A registered mark that has not been genuinely used in the UK for the registered goods or services for a continuous five year period can be revoked on application by anyone. Marks that have been rebranded, or whose specification covers goods the business stopped selling, may be at risk. A review identifies these risks and the evidence you need to keep.

Keeping evidence of use

Good evidence of use includes dated invoices, catalogues, packaging, screenshots of websites and marketplace listings, and advertising showing the mark. Keeping a small archive each year makes it much easier to defend a registration if it is challenged.

Before a sale or investment

Buyers and investors often check trademark ownership and status as part of due diligence. A review beforehand allows problems to be fixed in good time, rather than holding up a deal.

A review doesn't have to be a large exercise. For many smaller businesses it is a short call and a spreadsheet, listing each mark, its owner, its classes, its renewal date and whether it is in use. For larger portfolios, the firm may produce a written report with recommendations and a budget for the coming years.

If the review shows that a mark is not in use for some of its goods, the firm may suggest narrowing the registration voluntarily, keeping the core protection while removing goods that could invite a challenge.

Renewals and recordals involve official UK IPO fees and the firm's professional fees. Official fees depend on the number of classes and the type of request, and they change from time to time, so check GOV.UK for current figures or ask the firm.

Many firms charge a fixed fee per renewal or per recordal. Portfolio management is often charged per mark per year or as part of a wider retainer. Restoration requests and complex ownership changes, for example after a group restructure, may be quoted separately.

Ask the firm what is included, how they will remind you of upcoming dates and whether they charge for reminders you decide not to act on.

Renewals and ownership changes questions and answers

When do I need to renew my trademark?
Every ten years from the filing date. You can renew up to six months before expiry, or up to six months after with a late fee.
Can a registered trademark be cancelled?
Yes. Anyone can apply to revoke a mark that hasn’t been genuinely used for a continuous five year period, so it helps to keep evidence of use.
Do I need to record a new owner?
Yes. If you buy a business or a brand, the change of ownership should be recorded at the UK IPO so the register shows the right owner.
Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.

When do I need to renew my trademark?
Every ten years from the filing date of the application. You can renew up to six months before the expiry date, and there is a six month late period after expiry in which you can still renew by paying an extra fee.
Will the UK IPO remind me?
The UK IPO usually sends a reminder to the address on the register. It is still your responsibility to renew. If your address has changed, or the reminder goes to a former adviser, you may never see it. Many owners ask a firm to track dates for them.
Can I renew only some classes?
Yes. You can choose to renew only the classes or goods and services you still need. This can save money. The firm can help you weigh the saving against the value of keeping wider protection.
What happens if I renew late?
If you renew within six months after expiry, you pay a late fee and the registration continues as if it had been renewed on time. After that, the mark is removed from the register and you would need to ask for restoration or file again.
Can I renew a trademark myself?
Yes. Renewals can be done online through the UK IPO. Many owners use a firm when they have several marks, when the specification needs reviewing or when they want someone else to keep track of dates.
Does renewing change my rights?
No. Renewal simply continues the registration for another ten years. It doesn't widen the goods and services or change the mark. If you need wider protection, you need a new application.
Should I renew a mark I don't use any more?
It depends. Keeping an unused mark costs renewal fees and it becomes vulnerable to revocation after five years without use. Sometimes it is still worth keeping for defensive or future reasons. The firm can help you decide.
Can I renew early to get it out of the way?
You can renew from six months before the expiry date, but not earlier. The renewal runs for ten years from the expiry date, so renewing early within that window doesn't shorten your protection.
Is the renewal date the same as the registration date?
No. The ten year period runs from the filing date of the application, not from the date the mark was registered. Check the filing date on the register or your certificate when working out when renewal is due.
Can a renewal be refused?
Renewal is an administrative step and is not examined again on its merits. As long as the request is made in time with the correct fee, the registration is renewed. Problems usually arise only from missed deadlines or unpaid fees.
My trademark has expired. Is it lost?
Not necessarily. If you are within six months of expiry, you can still renew with a late fee. If the mark has been removed, you may be able to ask for restoration within six months of removal. After that, you would need to file a new application.
How does restoration work?
You ask the UK IPO to restore the registration, explain why it wasn't renewed and pay the fees. The UK IPO considers whether the failure to renew was unintentional. Restoration is not automatic, so a clear and honest explanation matters.
Can someone else register my mark while it has lapsed?
They might try. If your registration has been removed, a third party could apply for the same or a similar mark. Restoring quickly, or filing a new application, reduces the risk. You may also have unregistered rights if you have continued to use the mark.
Should I file a new application as well as asking for restoration?
For an important mark, firms sometimes recommend both, so that you have a fallback if restoration is refused. The new application will have a new filing date and will be examined and published like any other. The firm will advise on the best approach.
Will a lapsed registration affect my unregistered rights?
Unregistered rights, such as goodwill protected by passing off, depend on use rather than registration. If you have kept using the mark, those rights may continue. However, they are harder to enforce than a registration, which is why restoring or refiling is usually worthwhile.
Do I need to record a new owner?
Yes, if you have bought a business or a brand that includes registered marks. Recording the assignment at the UK IPO makes sure the register shows the right owner, which helps with enforcement, online platforms, investment and future sales.
My trademark is in my personal name. Should it be in my company's?
Often it should, if the company is the business using the brand. Some owners keep marks personally and license them to the company for planning reasons. The firm can explain the options. If you decide to transfer, the assignment should be recorded.
What documents are needed to transfer a trademark?
A written assignment signed by or on behalf of the current owner, identifying the marks and the new owner. The firm can draft it, and then record the change at the UK IPO. Transfers of international registrations are recorded through the Madrid system.
Can I transfer only some goods and services?
Yes. A registration can be partly assigned, so that different owners hold different goods or services. This needs care, because splitting a mark between owners can create a risk of confusion. The firm will advise on whether it is sensible.
Should I record a licence?
Recording a licence of a registered mark is not compulsory but can strengthen the licensee's position, including in enforcement. Licensees and lenders often ask for licences and security interests to be recorded.
How do I update my address on the register?
You can ask the UK IPO to record a change of name or address. It is a simple step but important, because official letters go to the address on the register. A firm can make the changes for all your marks at once.
What if the previous owner has been dissolved?
If a company that owned a mark has been dissolved, its property may have passed to the Crown, and getting a valid assignment can be complicated. The firm can look at the options, which may include restoring the company or applying for a new registration.
Do I need to tell anyone else when a mark changes hands?
Besides the UK IPO, it is worth updating online marketplace brand programmes, domain name registrations and any licensees or distributors. The firm can give you a checklist so nothing is missed.
What happens to trademarks if my business is sold?
They usually pass to the buyer as part of the deal, under the sale agreement and a separate assignment. The assignment should then be recorded at the UK IPO. Before a sale, it is worth checking that the marks are in the seller's name and in force.
Can a registered trademark be cancelled for non use?
Yes. Anyone can apply to revoke a mark that hasn't been genuinely used in the UK for the registered goods or services for a continuous five year period. Keeping dated evidence of use helps you defend the registration if challenged.
What is genuine use?
Use that is real and consistent with the mark's purpose of identifying the goods or services, in a way that creates or keeps a market for them. Token use just to keep a registration alive is not enough. The firm can look at your evidence.
How often should I review my portfolio?
Many businesses review once a year, or whenever they launch a new brand, change their logo, enter new markets or prepare for investment or a sale. A short review can catch problems early.
What evidence of use should I keep?
Dated examples showing the mark on goods, packaging, websites, marketplace listings, invoices and advertising, along with sales figures by year. Keeping a small file each year makes it easy to respond to a challenge.
Can a firm manage all my renewals?
Yes. Many trademark firms keep a docket of client marks, send reminders well before each deadline and handle renewals and recordals on instruction. This is especially useful if you have marks in several countries.
Do I need to protect my new logo separately?
If you change your logo, the old logo registration may not cover the new design. Word marks usually continue to protect the name. The firm can advise whether to file for the new logo and whether to keep the old registration.
Can a firm watch for similar marks as well?
Yes. Many firms combine portfolio management with a watch service that alerts you to new applications for similar marks, so you can decide whether to oppose them.
What is a trademark docket?
It is the list a firm keeps of all the marks it manages for you, with their numbers, owners, classes, countries and deadlines. It means one place holds everything, and the firm can remind you well in advance of each renewal.

My renewal deadline is close. How quickly can someone help?
Firms usually contact you within one working day. Put the expiry date in the form so the firm knows the timing. If you are in the late period, mention that too.
Can the firm help with marks outside the UK?
Yes. Most trademark firms renew international registrations and work with agents in other countries for national marks.
Do I need to register copyright in the UK?
No. Copyright arises automatically when an original work is created and recorded. There is no official register. Keep dated drafts, original files and records of publication, so you can show that you created the work and when.
What does the © symbol do?
It reminds people that the work is protected and who owns it. It is not required for protection in the UK, but it can deter copying and help if you need to show that someone knew the work was protected.
How long does copyright last?
For most written, musical and artistic works, including photographs, it lasts for the creator's life plus 70 years. Other works, such as sound recordings, films and broadcasts, have their own periods. After copyright expires, the work can be used freely.
Does copyright protect ideas?
No. Copyright protects the expression of an idea, such as the words, images or code, not the idea itself. Someone can use the same idea in their own way without infringing. Ideas may sometimes be protected by confidentiality agreements instead.
Is a name or a slogan protected by copyright?
Usually not. Short phrases, names and titles are rarely original enough to be protected by copyright. They are better protected as trademarks. Logos and longer creative text are different and can attract copyright.
What is substantial copying?
Infringement requires copying of the whole or a substantial part of a work. Substantial is judged by quality as much as quantity, so copying a small but important part can still infringe. The firm will compare the works.
Is copyright the same in every country?
No, but most countries protect works from other member countries of the main international treaties, so UK works are usually protected abroad automatically. The details, such as duration and exceptions, vary.
Is software protected by copyright?
Yes. Computer programs are protected as literary works, and preparatory design material is also protected. The underlying ideas and functionality are not protected by copyright, but the code and its structure can be.

More questions people ask

What counts as trademark infringement?
Using a sign that is identical or similar to a registered mark, for identical or similar goods or services, in a way likely to cause confusion, without consent. Use must be in the course of trade. For marks with a reputation, use that takes unfair advantage of or harms that reputation can also infringe. Whether a particular use infringes depends on the facts.
Does the other side have to have copied deliberately?
No. Infringement doesn't depend on intention. A business can infringe even if it chose its name innocently without knowing about the earlier mark. Intention can affect the remedies and how a court views the case, but it is not needed for infringement.
What is passing off?
Passing off protects unregistered brands. The owner must show goodwill in the business, a misrepresentation by the other side that leads or is likely to lead customers to think the goods are connected, and damage as a result. It needs more evidence than a registered trademark claim.
Is using a similar logo infringement?
It can be. Logos are compared as a whole, looking at visual, aural and conceptual similarity. A logo that copies the distinctive parts of a registered logo, for similar goods, may infringe even if some details are different. The firm will compare them carefully.
Can I stop someone using my brand for different goods?
Usually only if the goods are similar, or if your mark has a reputation and their use would take unfair advantage of it or harm it. Different goods often mean no infringement, but well known brands can get wider protection.
Is it infringement to mention a competitor's brand?
Not always. Honest comparative advertising, describing compatibility and genuine resale of branded goods can be allowed within limits. Using a competitor's mark in a way that suggests a connection or takes unfair advantage can cross the line. The details matter.
Can a trademark be infringed outside the UK?
A UK registration protects against use in the UK. Use abroad is governed by the law of that country. Online sales targeted at UK customers can count as use in the UK, even if the seller is based overseas.
What is a mark with a reputation?
A mark that is known by a significant part of the relevant public for the goods or services it covers. Such marks can be protected against use on dissimilar goods where that use takes unfair advantage of, or harms, the mark's reputation or distinctive character. Proving reputation needs evidence.
Does selling genuine second hand goods infringe?
Generally no. Once genuine goods have been put on the market in the UK or the European Economic Area by or with the consent of the owner, reselling them is usually allowed. There are exceptions, for example where the goods have been changed or the resale damages the reputation of the mark.
