About objections and oppositions

After you apply, the UK IPO examiner may object, for example because the mark describes the goods or is not distinctive. Once the application is published, anyone with earlier rights has two months, extendable to three, to oppose it. Owners also oppose other people’s applications that are too close to their own brand.

A trademark solicitor or attorney can answer an examiner’s report, negotiate with the other side, narrow the specification or argue the case before the UK IPO tribunal. Many oppositions settle, often through a coexistence agreement, before a hearing.

Trademark opposition in brief

A trademark opposition is a challenge to an application, brought by someone with earlier rights during the two month opposition period after publication. Examiners can also object to an application before it is published.

A trademark attorney or solicitor can respond to an examiner, oppose a trademark that clashes with yours or defend your own application. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm.

Objections and oppositions explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Not every trademark application sails through. Some meet an objection from the UK IPO examiner, and some are challenged by another business that believes the new mark clashes with its own. Both situations run to fixed deadlines, and both can usually be handled well with the right help.

An objection comes from the UK IPO itself. After you file, an examiner checks whether the mark can be registered, for example whether it is distinctive, whether it simply describes the goods or services, and whether the list of goods and services is clear. If something is wrong, the examiner sends an examination report explaining the problem and giving you a deadline to respond.

An opposition comes from a third party. Once an application is accepted, it is published in the Trade Marks Journal. For two months anyone can oppose it, and that period can be extended to three months by filing a notice of threatened opposition within the first two. Oppositions are usually brought by owners of earlier marks who think the new mark is too close to theirs, but they can also be based on other grounds such as bad faith.

Owners also bring oppositions themselves, to stop someone else registering a mark that is too close to their brand. Opposing at the application stage is usually quicker and cheaper than trying to cancel a mark after it is registered.

A firm instructed on the last day may only have time to file the minimum needed to protect your position.

Examiner objections fall into a few common types. Knowing which you are facing helps you understand the options.

Lack of distinctiveness

The examiner may say the mark is not distinctive, meaning customers would not see it as a sign of where the goods come from. Common examples are ordinary words, simple shapes, slogans that read as plain promotional statements and single letters or numbers. The response might argue that the mark is in fact distinctive, show that it has become distinctive through use, or propose a change.

Descriptive marks

A mark that describes the kind, quality, purpose, value or origin of the goods is usually refused for those goods. A mark called Quick Clean for cleaning products is a classic example. The response may narrow the goods to ones the mark does not describe, or argue that the mark is more than a plain description.

Unclear specifications

The examiner may say that some terms in the list of goods and services are too vague or are in the wrong class. These objections are usually straightforward to fix by rewording or moving terms, and the firm can suggest acceptable wording.

Other objections

Less common objections include marks that are deceptive, contrary to public policy or that include protected emblems or flags. The firm will explain what the objection means and whether it can be overcome.

How to respond

Responses are usually made in writing within the deadline in the report. Sometimes a short call with the examiner, or a hearing, helps. If the objection cannot be overcome, the application may be refused in whole or in part, and in some cases there is a right of appeal.

It is worth remembering that the examiner is applying published practice, and the UK IPO's manual explains how it approaches each type of objection. A firm that deals with these reports regularly will know which arguments tend to succeed, which changes the examiner is likely to accept and when it is better to file a fresh application instead of fighting the objection.

An opposition is a formal proceeding before the UK IPO tribunal. It has its own rules, forms and timetable, and the outcome is decided by a hearing officer.

Common grounds

  • The new mark is identical or similar to an earlier mark for identical or similar goods, and confusion is likely
  • The new mark would take unfair advantage of, or harm, the reputation of an earlier mark
  • The opponent has earlier unregistered rights and could stop use under the law of passing off
  • The application was made in bad faith
  • The mark is descriptive or not distinctive, and should not have been accepted

The usual stages

The opponent files a notice of opposition setting out its grounds. The applicant then files a counterstatement explaining why the opposition should fail. If the case goes ahead, each side may file evidence, there may be a hearing or a decision on the papers, and the hearing officer issues a written decision.

Proof of use

If the opponent relies on an earlier mark that has been registered for five years or more, the applicant can ask the opponent to prove they have genuinely used it. If they can't, they may be unable to rely on it for some or all of its goods. This can change the balance of a case significantly.

Cooling off

In many cases the parties can agree a cooling off period, which pauses the opposition while they try to reach a settlement. Many oppositions end during this time.

Costs at the tribunal

The UK IPO tribunal usually awards costs from a published scale, which is lower than the costs a court might order. The loser may have to pay a contribution to the winner's costs, but rarely the full amount.

Cancelling a registered mark

If the opposition period has passed, a registered mark can still be challenged. A revocation action can remove a mark that has not been used for five years, and an invalidity action can remove a mark that should never have been registered, for example because it conflicted with earlier rights. These actions follow a similar process to an opposition.

Most oppositions settle without a decision. That is often the best outcome for both sides, because it gives certainty sooner and at a lower cost than a contested case.

Common ways to settle

  • Narrowing the goods or services in the application
  • Agreeing limits on how the mark is used, such as colours, fonts or markets
  • A coexistence agreement setting out how both marks can be used side by side
  • Consent from the earlier owner, sometimes in return for a payment or undertakings
  • Withdrawing the application and agreeing a timetable to rebrand

Settlement talks usually start with a letter or call between the firms acting for each side. A good settlement focuses on what each business really needs. An opponent may only care about one product line, and an applicant may be happy to give up goods it never planned to sell.

Putting it in writing

Settlements should be recorded in a written agreement, so that both sides know where they stand in future. The firm will make sure the agreement covers the points that matter, such as what each side can register and use, in which markets, and what happens if either business changes.

When settling may not be right

Sometimes the other side's position is weak, or the terms they want would damage your business. In those cases it may be better to defend or pursue the opposition to a decision. The firm will help you weigh the cost and the chances of success against what a settlement would give you, so the choice is yours with the full picture.

Replying to an examination report is often a fixed fee piece of work. Oppositions are harder to price at the outset, because the cost depends on whether the other side settles, how much evidence is needed and whether there is a hearing. Firms usually estimate each stage separately.

A contested opposition that runs all the way to a decision can take many months, and sometimes more than a year, though many settle much sooner.

Objections and oppositions questions and answers

How do I oppose a trademark in the UK?
File a notice of opposition with the UK IPO within two months of the application being published, or within three months if you file a notice of threatened opposition first.
How long do I have to oppose a trademark?
Two months from publication in the Trade Marks Journal, which can be extended to three months by filing a notice of threatened opposition within the first two months.
What happens if I miss the deadline?
You may still be able to apply to invalidate the mark after it is registered, but that is usually slower and more expensive.
Do oppositions always go to a hearing?
No. Many are settled by agreement, and some are decided on the papers without a hearing.
Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?

What is an examination report?
It is a letter from the UK IPO explaining why the examiner thinks your application cannot be accepted as filed. It sets out the objection and gives a deadline to respond. Many objections can be overcome by argument, evidence or changes to the list of goods and services, so it is worth getting advice before giving up.
How long do I have to respond to an objection?
The deadline is shown on the report and is usually around two months. Extensions may be possible in some circumstances. If you don't respond in time, the application may be refused. Passing the report to a firm promptly gives them the most time to prepare a good response.
Can I change my mark to overcome an objection?
Only in limited ways. You can't make a significant change to the mark after filing. Sometimes a minor amendment is allowed, but often the answer is to argue the point, narrow the goods or file a fresh application for a stronger version of the mark. The firm will explain the options.
What does it mean if my mark is descriptive?
It means the examiner thinks customers would see the mark as describing the goods or services, for example their type, quality or purpose, rather than as a brand. Descriptive marks are usually refused for the goods they describe. They may be acceptable for other goods where the meaning doesn't apply.
Can evidence of use overcome an objection?
Yes, sometimes. If a mark lacks distinctiveness on its face but customers have come to recognise it as your brand through use, detailed evidence can overcome the objection. It usually needs to show substantial use across the UK over time, so it is not always possible for newer brands.
What if the examiner refuses my application?
You may be able to ask for a hearing, appeal the decision or file a new application for a different version of the mark. The right step depends on the reasons for refusal and how important the mark is to you. The firm will explain the choices and their costs.
Can I talk to the examiner?
Yes, in many cases. Firms often call the examiner to understand the objection and test a possible solution before filing a written response. A short conversation can sometimes resolve a point that would otherwise take several letters.
Does an objection mean my brand is unsafe to use?
Not necessarily. An objection is about whether the mark can be registered. A mark refused as descriptive may still be usable, but you may find it hard to stop others using similar words. The firm can explain what the objection means for your business in practice.
How long do I have to oppose a trademark?
Two months from the date the application is published in the Trade Marks Journal. You can extend this to three months by filing a notice of threatened opposition within the first two months. After that, the chance to oppose is gone and you would need to apply to cancel the mark once it is registered.
What is a notice of threatened opposition?
It is a short form that tells the applicant you are considering opposing and extends your deadline to three months. It costs little and is often used to open settlement talks. The applicant is told it has been filed, so it acts as an early warning.
Who can oppose my application?
Anyone can oppose on some grounds, such as descriptiveness or bad faith. Oppositions based on earlier marks or earlier rights must usually be brought by the owner of those rights. In practice most oppositions come from owners of similar marks.
What happens if I ignore an opposition?
If you don't file a counterstatement by the deadline, the application is usually treated as abandoned. You may also lose the opportunity to argue your case. Even if you plan to give up, getting advice can help you limit any costs and plan a sensible next step.
Can I continue using my brand during an opposition?
An opposition is about whether your mark can be registered, not directly about whether you can use it. However, an opponent who thinks your use infringes their rights may also take separate action. The firm can tell you how much risk continued use carries in your case.
What is proof of use?
If the opponent relies on a mark that has been registered for at least five years, the applicant can require them to prove genuine use of it during the relevant period. If they can't prove use for some goods, they can't rely on the mark for those goods. This often narrows the dispute.
Do oppositions always go to a hearing?
No. Many settle, and many are decided on the written evidence without a hearing. Either side can ask for a hearing, which is usually held by video. The hearing officer then gives a written decision with reasons.
Can an opposition be withdrawn?
Yes. An opponent can withdraw at any time, and often does when a settlement is reached. Costs may still be awarded for work done up to that point, depending on the circumstances. Any settlement should deal with costs clearly.
What evidence is used in an opposition?
Evidence is usually given in written witness statements with exhibits, such as examples of the marks in use, sales figures, advertising and evidence of how customers see the marks. Evidence must be relevant and properly dated, and the firm will help gather and present it.
What can the tribunal decide?
The hearing officer can reject the opposition, uphold it completely, or uphold it in part, meaning the application goes ahead for some goods or services but not others. Partial outcomes are common, which is why narrowing the goods is often a sensible settlement.
Will I have to pay the other side's costs if I lose?
Possibly. The UK IPO usually awards a contribution to costs based on a published scale, which is often well below the actual legal costs. In unusual cases, such as unreasonable behaviour, higher awards are possible. The firm will explain the likely exposure.
Can I appeal an opposition decision?
Yes. Appeals can go to an Appointed Person, whose decision is final, or to the High Court. There are short deadlines for appealing, so get advice quickly if you want to challenge a decision.
How much does an opposition cost?
It depends on how far it goes. Filing or answering an opposition and negotiating a settlement may cost relatively little. A fully contested case with evidence and a hearing costs considerably more. Firms usually estimate each stage, so you can decide as the case develops.
How long does an opposition take?
A case that settles early may be over within a few months. A contested case with evidence rounds and a hearing can take a year or more. Cooling off periods add time but can save costs if they lead to a settlement.
Can I get a quick decision?
The UK IPO has a fast track opposition procedure for straightforward cases based on a small number of earlier registered marks, where proof of use is provided at the start. It is quicker and cheaper but has limits, and it isn't suitable for every case.
Is an opposition the same as a court case?
No. An opposition is handled by the UK IPO tribunal, which is less formal and usually much cheaper than court. It decides whether a mark can be registered. Questions about infringement and damages are decided by the courts.
Should I oppose or wait and cancel later?
Opposing during the publication period is usually quicker and cheaper than cancelling a registered mark later. The burden of proof and the procedures are similar, but stopping a mark before it is registered also prevents the applicant from relying on the registration in the meantime.
How do I find out about new applications?
The Trade Marks Journal is published online and can be searched. Most businesses use a watch service through a trademark firm, which alerts them to applications for similar marks so they have time to act before the deadline.
Can I oppose if my brand isn't registered?
Possibly. You may be able to oppose based on unregistered rights if you can show goodwill in your brand and that use of the new mark would amount to passing off. The evidence needed is more demanding than for a registered mark.
What if the applicant is acting in bad faith?
Bad faith is a ground for opposition. It can apply, for example, where someone files a mark they know belongs to another business, such as a former distributor or partner. The firm will consider whether the facts support this ground, which needs careful evidence.
Is there a risk in opposing?
Yes. If the opposition fails, you may have to contribute to the applicant's costs. Opposing also tells the applicant you see them as a threat, which can lead them to look closely at your own registrations. The firm will help you weigh this before you file.
Can I oppose an international registration that covers the UK?
Yes. International registrations that designate the UK are published and can be opposed in much the same way as UK applications. The timetable is set by the publication date in the UK.

What counts as trademark infringement?
Using a sign that is identical or similar to a registered mark, for identical or similar goods or services, in a way likely to cause confusion, without consent. Use must be in the course of trade. For marks with a reputation, use that takes unfair advantage of or harms that reputation can also infringe. Whether a particular use infringes depends on the facts.
Does the other side have to have copied deliberately?
No. Infringement doesn't depend on intention. A business can infringe even if it chose its name innocently without knowing about the earlier mark. Intention can affect the remedies and how a court views the case, but it is not needed for infringement.
What is passing off?
Passing off protects unregistered brands. The owner must show goodwill in the business, a misrepresentation by the other side that leads or is likely to lead customers to think the goods are connected, and damage as a result. It needs more evidence than a registered trademark claim.
Is using a similar logo infringement?
It can be. Logos are compared as a whole, looking at visual, aural and conceptual similarity. A logo that copies the distinctive parts of a registered logo, for similar goods, may infringe even if some details are different. The firm will compare them carefully.
Can I stop someone using my brand for different goods?
Usually only if the goods are similar, or if your mark has a reputation and their use would take unfair advantage of it or harm it. Different goods often mean no infringement, but well known brands can get wider protection.
Is it infringement to mention a competitor's brand?
Not always. Honest comparative advertising, describing compatibility and genuine resale of branded goods can be allowed within limits. Using a competitor's mark in a way that suggests a connection or takes unfair advantage can cross the line. The details matter.
Can a trademark be infringed outside the UK?
A UK registration protects against use in the UK. Use abroad is governed by the law of that country. Online sales targeted at UK customers can count as use in the UK, even if the seller is based overseas.
What is a mark with a reputation?
A mark that is known by a significant part of the relevant public for the goods or services it covers. Such marks can be protected against use on dissimilar goods where that use takes unfair advantage of, or harms, the mark's reputation or distinctive character. Proving reputation needs evidence.
