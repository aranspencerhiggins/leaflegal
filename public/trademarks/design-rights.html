About design rights

Design rights protect the appearance of a product, such as its shape, pattern, contours or decoration. A registered design gives the strongest protection, for up to 25 years in five year periods, and is applied for at the UK IPO. Unregistered design rights also arise automatically but are shorter and harder to enforce.

Registered design in brief

A registered design protects the shape, pattern and decoration of a product for up to 25 years. Unregistered design rights also arise automatically, but they last for less time and are harder to prove.

An IP solicitor or attorney can prepare a design registration and act if a competitor copies your product. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm.

Design rights explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Design rights protect the way a product looks. That includes its shape, its configuration, the lines and contours, colours, texture, materials and decoration. Furniture, lighting, homewares, fashion, jewellery, packaging, toys, consumer electronics, vehicle parts and graphical symbols can all be protected, along with the look of a whole product or just part of it.

In the UK there are two main kinds of protection. A registered design is applied for at the UK Intellectual Property Office and gives the strongest rights, for up to 25 years. Unregistered design rights arise automatically when a design is created or first disclosed, but they last for less time and are harder to enforce, because the owner usually needs to show that the other side copied.

Designs often sit alongside other rights. A product's name may be a trademark, its drawings may be protected by copyright and its technical features may be patentable. Design rights fill the gap for appearance, which is often what customers notice first and what copycats imitate.

When design protection matters

  • Launching a new product with a distinctive look
  • Selling through retailers or marketplaces where copies appear quickly
  • Licensing a design to a manufacturer or another brand
  • Dealing with a competitor's lookalike product
  • Defending a claim that your product copies someone else's
  • Planning a trade fair or crowdfunding launch where the design will be seen

Who can help

Design registration and design disputes are handled by IP solicitors and by chartered trade mark attorneys, many of whom file designs regularly. Why designs are often overlooked

Many product businesses think about trademarks and forget designs, or assume that copying is just part of selling a successful product. Design protection is often quicker and cheaper to obtain than people expect, and a registered design can be a powerful tool against lookalikes, particularly on online marketplaces where copies appear within weeks of a launch. Thinking about designs early, before the product is shown, keeps the most options open.

A registered design gives the owner the exclusive right to use the design and any design that does not produce a different overall impression on an informed user. Crucially, infringement of a registered design does not depend on copying. Someone who independently creates a design that is too close can still infringe, which makes registration much easier to enforce.

What can be registered

To be registered, a design must be new and have individual character. New means no identical design, or one differing only in immaterial details, has been made available to the public before. Individual character means the overall impression it gives an informed user is different from earlier designs.

The application

The application includes images showing the design clearly, usually several views. What the images show defines what is protected, so they need care. Line drawings, photographs or computer renderings can be used, and features that are not meant to be protected can be shown in dotted lines or left out.

Examination and registration

The UK IPO checks the application mainly for formal requirements. It does not search for earlier designs in the way the trademark system does, so registration can be quick, often within weeks. The flip side is that a registered design can later be challenged if it turns out not to be new.

Duration and renewal

A registered design lasts for five years and can be renewed every five years up to a maximum of 25 years, provided renewal fees are paid.

Deferring publication

Owners can ask for publication to be deferred for a period, which can be useful if a product launch is planned and you don't want the design to be visible on the register before then.

Designs that came from EU registrations

Registered Community designs that were in force at the end of the Brexit transition period were automatically turned into comparable UK registered designs. If you held EU designs before then, you may already hold UK rights. The firm can check what you have and when renewals fall due.

A registered design is also a useful business asset. It can be licensed, sold or used as part of a brand's value when raising investment, and it gives retailers confidence that the product is genuinely yours.

Even without registering, a design may be protected automatically. The UK has more than one type of unregistered design right, and they protect different things for different periods.

UK unregistered design right

This protects the shape and configuration of a product, inside or out, but not surface decoration. It generally lasts for 15 years from when the design was first recorded or 10 years from when products were first sold, whichever ends first. In the final five years, others can ask for a licence as of right. There are exclusions for features that must fit or must match another product.

Supplementary unregistered design

This protects the appearance of a product, including surface decoration, for three years from first disclosure in the UK. It was introduced at the end of the Brexit transition period to replace the EU unregistered design right for UK purposes. Where a design is first disclosed matters, so a launch outside the UK may affect protection here.

Limits of unregistered rights

Unregistered rights only protect against copying. The owner must show that the other side copied the design, rather than coming up with it independently. Proving that can be difficult, and the short duration of some rights means they may expire before a dispute is resolved. That is why many businesses register their most important designs.

Copyright

Some designs, particularly works of artistic craftsmanship and surface patterns, may also be protected by copyright. The overlap between copyright and design rights is complex, and the firm will consider every right that may apply.

Keeping records

Because unregistered rights depend on dates and on proving copying, good records are essential. Keep dated sketches, design files, prototypes, emails with manufacturers and launch materials. Note when and where the design was first shown or sold. These records can make the difference in a dispute.

Because the rules on where a design was first disclosed can affect protection, businesses launching in several countries at once should take advice on the order and place of launch. A short conversation before launch can avoid losing rights by accident.

Lookalike products are a common problem, especially for small brands whose designs are copied and sold more cheaply online. Equally, businesses sometimes receive claims that their own products copy someone else's design.

If your design is copied

  • Gather evidence of your design, including dated drawings, prototypes and launch materials
  • Buy or photograph the copy and record where and when you found it
  • Check which rights you have, registered or unregistered
  • Consider platform reporting tools for online listings
  • Consider a carefully worded letter, bearing in mind the unjustified threats rules
  • Consider a court claim if the matter can't be resolved

Unjustified threats

Like trademarks and patents, designs are covered by the Intellectual Property (Unjustified Threats) Act 2017. Threatening the wrong person, such as a retailer rather than the manufacturer, or making a threat that turns out to be unjustified, can lead to a claim against the sender. A professional can make sure any letter stays within the rules.

If you are accused

The firm will compare the designs, check whether the other side's rights are valid and still in force, look at the dates and consider whether your design was created independently. Registered designs can be challenged if they were not new when filed. A careful reply often resolves weak claims.

Remedies

Courts can order the copying to stop, award damages or the infringer's profits and order infringing goods to be handed over. Intentionally copying a registered design in the course of business can also be a criminal offence in the UK.

Online marketplaces

Marketplaces generally act more readily on complaints backed by a registered design than on unregistered rights. Some brand protection programmes accept design registrations alongside trademarks. If copies of your products appear online regularly, registration and a routine reporting process can save a great deal of time.

As with any IP dispute, think about the outcome you want before you act. Stopping sales, recovering compensation and reaching a licence deal with the copier all call for slightly different approaches, and the firm will tailor the strategy to your goal.

Renewal reminders

Registered designs must be renewed every five years. Missing a renewal can end protection, although there is a short period for late renewal with an extra fee. Firms that manage designs track these dates for you.

The timing of design protection matters more than many people realise. Showing a design publicly can affect whether it can be registered later and which unregistered rights apply.

The grace period

UK law allows a 12 month grace period, so a designer can usually still register a design within 12 months of first disclosing it. After that, the earlier disclosure can count against the design's novelty. Relying on the grace period carries some risk, because someone else could disclose a similar design in the meantime, so many businesses file before launch.

Trade fairs, crowdfunding and social media

Showing a product at a trade fair, on a crowdfunding page or on social media is disclosure. If you plan to do any of these, speak to a firm first. Keep records of when and where the design was first shown, because those dates can be important later.

  • Where you sell now and plan to sell

If an external designer or manufacturer created the design, check who owns it. Ownership rules for designs can differ from copyright, and a written agreement avoids doubt.

Confidentiality before launch

Before a design is registered or launched, share it only under confidentiality where you can. Non disclosure agreements with manufacturers, investors and retailers help keep a design secret and can count as a confidential disclosure rather than a public one. If you have already launched without registering, don't assume it is too late. Check how long ago the design was first shown, because you may still be within the grace period.

Design rights questions and answers

Do I need to register a design?
Not always, because some protection is automatic. Registration gives clearer, longer protection and makes it easier to act against copies.
Can I register a design I have already shown publicly?
Often yes, within 12 months of first disclosing it, but it is best to get advice before showing it.
How long does a registered design last?
Up to 25 years, renewed every five years.
Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.

What does a design right protect?
The appearance of a product or part of a product, such as its shape, contours, lines, colours, texture, materials or decoration. It does not protect how a product works, which may be a matter for patents, or a brand name, which is a matter for trademarks.
Do I need to register a design?
Not always, because some protection is automatic. Registration gives clearer and longer protection and doesn't require proof of copying, which makes it much easier to act against lookalikes. Many businesses register their most important designs.
How long does a registered design last?
Up to 25 years, in five year periods. It must be renewed every five years by paying a renewal fee. If you stop renewing, protection ends.
How long do unregistered design rights last?
It depends on the right. UK unregistered design right for shape generally lasts up to 15 years from creation or 10 years from first sale, whichever is earlier. Supplementary unregistered design protects appearance for three years from first disclosure in the UK.
What makes a design new?
A design is new if no identical design, or one differing only in immaterial details, has been made available to the public before the filing date, subject to the 12 month grace period for the designer's own disclosures.
What is individual character?
A design has individual character if the overall impression it gives an informed user differs from the overall impression of earlier designs. The informed user is someone familiar with the products in that sector, more observant than an average consumer.
Can I protect part of a product?
Yes. A registered design can cover part of a product, such as the handle of a kettle or the sole of a shoe. The images show which part is protected, often by drawing other parts in dotted lines.
Is a design right the same as a trademark?
No. A design right protects the look of a product. A trademark protects a sign that tells customers who the product comes from, such as a name or logo. Some shapes can be both, but the rules and tests are different.
Can I register a design I have already shown publicly?
Often yes, if you apply within 12 months of first disclosing it. After that, your own disclosure can stop the design being registered. If you plan to show a product, it is best to speak to a firm first.
What images do I need?
Clear images showing the design from enough angles to make it understood, often several views. Line drawings, photographs or renderings can be used. The images define the protection, so they need to be prepared carefully.
Does the UK IPO check whether my design is new?
Not in detail. The UK IPO mainly checks formal requirements and does not search earlier designs before registering. A registered design can be challenged later if it was not new, so it is worth being confident about your design.
Can I register several designs together?
Yes. You can file multiple designs in one application, which can reduce costs. Each design is treated separately for protection and renewal.
Can I delay publication of my registered design?
Yes. You can ask for publication to be deferred, which can help if you want to keep the design confidential until launch. The firm can explain the time limits.
Do I need protection abroad?
A UK registered design only covers the UK. You can register in other countries, including through the Hague system for international designs. If you manufacture or sell abroad, consider where copies are most likely.
Who owns a design created by an external designer?
Ownership rules for designs can differ from copyright and from the position for employees. Get a written agreement that assigns the design rights to your business, so there is no doubt when you register or enforce.
How long does registration take?
If the application meets the formal requirements, registration often takes a few weeks. Deferring publication will delay when the design appears on the register. The firm can tell you the likely timing for your application.
Can a design be registered if it is functional?
Features dictated solely by the product's technical function can't be protected by a registered design. Most products combine function with aesthetic choices, and those aesthetic features can be protected. The firm will help decide what to include in the images.
A competitor has copied my product. What can I do?
Gather evidence of your design and the copy, check which rights you have and get advice. Options include platform takedowns, a carefully worded letter and a court claim. A registered design makes action much easier, because copying doesn't need to be proved.
Do I have to prove copying?
For a registered design, no. If the other design gives the same overall impression, it can infringe even if it was created independently. For unregistered rights, you do need to show copying, which can be harder.
Is copying a registered design a crime?
Intentionally copying a registered design in the course of business, so as to make a product exactly like it or with features that differ only immaterially, can be a criminal offence in the UK. Civil remedies are also available.
I've been accused of copying a design. What should I do?
Don't ignore the letter and don't reply in anger. Gather evidence of how and when you created your design. The firm will compare the designs, check the other side's rights and help you respond.
Can I challenge a registered design?
Yes. A registered design can be declared invalid, for example if it was not new or lacked individual character when filed. A successful challenge removes the registration. Evidence of earlier designs is key.
What is a must fit or must match exclusion?
UK unregistered design right doesn't protect features that must fit another product to work, or that must match the appearance of another article of which the product is part, such as some spare parts. The details are technical, and the firm will advise.
Can I stop copies being imported?
Customs can be asked to watch for goods that infringe registered designs and other IP rights entering the UK. An application to customs needs details of your rights and how to identify copies. The firm can help prepare it.
What if the copy is slightly different?
For a registered design, the question is whether the other product gives the informed user a different overall impression. Small changes may not be enough to avoid infringement. For unregistered rights, the question is whether the design was copied exactly or substantially. The firm will compare the products.
How long do I have to bring a claim?
Claims for damages are generally subject to a six year limitation period, but delay can weaken your case and make an injunction harder to obtain. Unregistered rights may also expire if you wait too long, so it is best to act promptly.
Can I license my design?
Yes. A licence lets a manufacturer or another brand use your design on agreed terms, often in return for a royalty. Licences of registered designs should be in writing. The firm can prepare or review the agreement.
Can I sell my design rights?
Yes, by assignment. An assignment of a registered design should be in writing and recorded at the UK IPO so the register shows the new owner.
Should I use the word registered on my product?
You can mark products with the words registered design and the registration number. Marking can make it harder for an infringer to claim they didn't know about your rights, which can affect damages. Never mark a product as registered if it isn't.
How do I protect a product design and its brand together?
Many product businesses register both the design of the product and the brand name as a trademark. The firm can plan both together and file them at the same time.
Should my manufacturer sign anything about designs?
Yes, it is sensible. An agreement with your manufacturer should confirm that you own the design, that they can only make the product for you and that they keep your designs confidential. This helps prevent the manufacturer selling copies to others.
Does a registered design help with investors?
It can. Registered designs are clear, recorded assets that investors and buyers can check. Together with trademarks, they show that a product business has protected what makes its products distinctive.

Do you help with patents?
No. Patents protect how an invention works and are handled by patent attorneys, which is outside the work we introduce. If your product has a new technical feature, the Chartered Institute of Patent Attorneys has a directory.
How quickly will someone contact me?
Usually within one working day. If you have a launch date or a deadline, mention it in the form.
Do I need to search before I apply?
It is not compulsory, but it is usually wise. The UK IPO does not refuse an application just because a similar earlier mark exists. Instead, the owner of that mark may oppose. A search helps you avoid paying for an application that is likely to be challenged, or a brand that you may later have to change.
Can I apply for a name I haven't started using yet?
Yes. You can apply for a mark you intend to use. You need a genuine intention to use it for the goods and services listed, and if you don't use it within five years of registration it becomes vulnerable to being revoked.
Who should own the trademark?
Usually the business that will use the brand, such as your limited company. Some owners file in their own name and license the company to use it, which can have tax or planning reasons. The firm can explain the options. Getting this right early saves work during investment or a sale.
What makes a mark distinctive?
A mark is distinctive if customers would see it as a sign of where the goods come from rather than a description of them. Invented words and words unrelated to the goods are strongest. Descriptive words, laudatory terms like best or premium and common shapes are weakest. The firm can tell you where your mark sits.
Should I file a word mark or a logo first?
If budget allows only one, many firms suggest the word mark because it protects the name whatever the styling. If the name itself is weak but the logo is distinctive, a logo mark may be easier to register. The firm will explain the trade off for your brand.
Can I trademark my own name?
Often yes, if it is distinctive for your goods or services and nobody has earlier rights in the same or a similar name. Common surnames can be harder. Others with the same name may still be able to use it honestly in some circumstances.
How long before launch should I file?
As early as you can once you are confident in the name. Your rights run from the filing date, so filing before launch reduces the risk that someone else files first or starts using something similar. It also gives you time to deal with any objection before your marketing starts.
Is it worth registering if I am a small business?
Many small businesses find it worthwhile, because a brand is often one of their most valuable assets and copying can hurt a small business more than a large one. A registration also makes it much easier to deal with copies on online marketplaces. The firm can help you decide what level of protection makes sense for your budget.
