About copyright

Copyright protects original creative work, such as writing, photographs, artwork, music, film, software and website content. In the UK it arises automatically when the work is created, so there is no register to join. It usually lasts for the creator’s life plus 70 years for most written and artistic works.

Copyright solicitor in brief

Copyright protects original writing, photos, art, music, film and software, and arises automatically in the UK. Copyright infringement happens when someone copies a substantial part of a work without permission.

A copyright solicitor or copyright lawyer can help you prove ownership, act against copying or respond to a claim. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm.

Copyright explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Copyright protects original creative work. In the UK that includes writing of all kinds, photographs, illustrations, artwork, music, sound recordings, films, broadcasts and computer software, as well as website content and many product manuals and marketing materials. The law is set out mainly in the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988.

Copyright arises automatically as soon as an original work is created and recorded in some form, such as written down, saved as a file or photographed. There is no official register in the UK and nothing to pay. The owner has the exclusive right to copy the work, issue copies to the public, perform or show it in public, communicate it online and adapt it. Anyone who does one of those things with a substantial part of the work without permission may infringe copyright.

How long copyright lasts depends on the kind of work. For most written, musical and artistic works, including photographs, it lasts for the creator's life plus 70 years from the end of the year in which they died. Other works, such as sound recordings and broadcasts, have their own periods.

Common copyright problems

  • A competitor or website copies your text, photos or designs
  • You receive a demand for using a photo or image online
  • A freelancer and client disagree about who owns commissioned work
  • A business wants to license or sell its creative work
  • Software or code has been reused without permission
  • A former employee or partner keeps using work after leaving

Copyright often overlaps with other rights. A logo can be protected by copyright and as a trademark. A product design may have design rights as well as copyright in the drawings. That is why trademark and IP firms handle copyright alongside brands and designs.

Copyright online

Most copyright questions today involve the internet. Copying is easy, images and text spread quickly, and automated tools make it simple both to copy work and to find copies. That cuts both ways. Creators can find infringements more easily, and businesses are more likely to receive claims for content they used without checking. Understanding the basics helps on either side.

Working out who owns copyright is often the first question in any dispute, and the answer is not always obvious.

The basic rule

The first owner is usually the author, meaning the person who created the work. For a photograph, that is generally the photographer. For text, it is the writer. For software, it is the programmer.

Employees

Where an employee creates a work in the course of their employment, the employer is usually the first owner, unless there is an agreement saying otherwise. Work an employee creates in their own time and outside their job is usually theirs.

Freelancers and agencies

This is where many problems arise. If you pay a freelancer, designer, photographer or agency to create something, they usually own the copyright unless there is a written assignment signed by them that transfers it to you. Paying an invoice is not enough on its own. You may have an implied licence to use the work for the purpose it was commissioned, but that may not let you adapt it, register it as a trademark or stop others using it.

Fixing ownership

If you discover that you don't own work you thought you did, the usual solution is to ask the creator to sign an assignment. Most will, sometimes for a fee. It is far easier to do this before a dispute or a sale than during one.

Moral rights

Creators also have moral rights, such as the right to be identified as the author and the right to object to derogatory treatment of their work. These rights can't be assigned, but they can be waived in writing. Commissioning contracts often deal with them.

Joint authors

Where two or more people create a work together and their contributions can't be separated, they may be joint authors and joint owners. Joint owners usually need each other's consent to license the work. This can matter for bands, writing partnerships and collaborative design projects, and a written agreement at the start avoids later arguments.

Ownership and investment

Investors and buyers often ask whether a business owns the copyright in its key assets, such as its software, website and branding. Gaps in ownership, especially for work done by freelancers or early contractors, can delay a deal. A review and a few assignments before fundraising or a sale can save a lot of time.

Finding your work copied, whether it is website text, product photos or artwork, can feel personal as well as commercial. There are usually several ways to deal with it.

Gather evidence

Take dated screenshots that show the copied work and the web address. Keep your original files, drafts and anything showing when you created the work. Evidence of creation and of the copying is the foundation of any claim.

Platform takedowns

Most website hosts, social media platforms and marketplaces have procedures for reporting copyright infringement and asking for copied material to be removed. These can be quick and effective, particularly for online copying.

A letter to the copier

A letter setting out your rights and asking the other side to remove the work, stop using it and sometimes pay compensation is often the next step. Many disputes end at this stage. Unlike trademarks and designs, copyright is not covered by the statutory unjustified threats rules, but a letter still needs to be accurate and proportionate.

Court

If a dispute can't be resolved, the owner can bring a claim. In England and Wales, many copyright claims are heard in the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court, which has a small claims track for lower value claims and caps on recoverable costs for larger ones. The court can order the copying to stop, award damages or an account of profits, and in some cases award additional damages for flagrant infringement.

Choosing the right route

The best route depends on what you want. If you simply want the copy taken down, a platform report may be enough. If you want compensation or need to stop a business that keeps copying, a letter and, if necessary, a claim may be needed. The firm will help you choose a route that fits the value of the work and the damage done.

Act reasonably promptly. Waiting a long time before complaining can make it harder to get an injunction and may affect the compensation available. Claims for damages are generally subject to a six year limitation period.

Many businesses receive copyright demands, especially about images used on websites and social media. Some claims are entirely justified, some are overstated and some are wrong.

Image claims

Image libraries and photographers use automated tools to find their images online and then send demands for a licence fee. If you used an image without a licence, you may well have infringed, even if you found it through a search engine, credited the photographer or removed it quickly. However, the amount demanded is not always justified, and there may be questions about who owns the image or how it was used.

What to do

  • Don't ignore the demand
  • Don't pay before checking the claim
  • Check where the image came from and whether you had a licence
  • Remove the image if you can't show a licence, without admitting liability
  • Keep copies of the letter and your website as it was
  • Get advice on the amount and how to respond

Possible defences

There are exceptions to copyright, known as fair dealing, which allow limited use for purposes such as criticism and review, quotation, news reporting, and parody, caricature and pastiche. These exceptions are narrow and depend on the facts. Using a work for advertising or decoration rarely qualifies.

Negotiating

Many image and content claims are settled by agreeing a reasonable licence fee for past use. The firm can assess what a fair figure might be and negotiate on your behalf.

Preventing future claims

Once a claim is resolved, it is worth checking the rest of your website and social media for images or content you can't show a licence for. Keeping a simple record of where each image came from and the licence that covers it makes future claims much easier to deal with.

Claims about content you wrote

Not every claim is about images. Businesses sometimes receive complaints that their website text, product descriptions or course materials copy someone else's work. If you wrote the content yourself or commissioned it, gather your drafts and any agreements. Similar ideas expressed independently are not copying, and the firm can compare the works to see whether there is any real overlap.

Copyright can be licensed, which means giving permission to use the work on agreed terms, or assigned, which means transferring ownership entirely. Getting the paperwork right protects both creators and the businesses that use their work.

Licences

A licence can be exclusive or non exclusive, limited to particular uses, places or periods, and paid for by a fee or a royalty. Clear licences avoid disputes about what was agreed. Exclusive licences should be in writing and signed.

Assignments

An assignment transfers ownership. It must be in writing and signed by or on behalf of the owner. Businesses commissioning logos, websites, software and marketing materials often ask for an assignment so they can use, adapt and protect the work freely.

Commissioning terms

  • Who will own the copyright when the work is finished
  • Whether the creator can reuse the work or show it in a portfolio
  • Whether moral rights are waived
  • What happens if the project ends early
  • What warranties the creator gives about originality

A firm can prepare template terms for a business that commissions creative work regularly, or for a creator who wants standard terms to send to clients.

Collecting societies also license some uses of music, broadcasts and printed works on behalf of creators. Businesses that play music in public, for example, usually need a licence. The firm can explain which licences apply to your business.

If you are a creator, a short set of standard terms that you send with every quote can prevent most disputes. It tells clients clearly what they are buying, whether that is a licence for particular uses or full ownership, and what extra uses would cost.

Copyright questions and answers

Do I need to register copyright in the UK?
No. Copyright arises automatically. It helps to keep dated drafts, files and records so you can prove you created the work and when.
I used a photo from the internet and got a bill. What now?
Don’t ignore it. A solicitor can check whether the claim and the amount are justified and help you respond.
Who owns work I paid a freelancer to create?
Usually the freelancer, unless there is a written assignment. Employees’ work made in the course of employment usually belongs to the employer.
Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.

Do I need to register copyright in the UK?
No. Copyright arises automatically when an original work is created and recorded. There is no official register. Keep dated drafts, original files and records of publication, so you can show that you created the work and when.
What does the © symbol do?
It reminds people that the work is protected and who owns it. It is not required for protection in the UK, but it can deter copying and help if you need to show that someone knew the work was protected.
How long does copyright last?
For most written, musical and artistic works, including photographs, it lasts for the creator's life plus 70 years. Other works, such as sound recordings, films and broadcasts, have their own periods. After copyright expires, the work can be used freely.
Does copyright protect ideas?
No. Copyright protects the expression of an idea, such as the words, images or code, not the idea itself. Someone can use the same idea in their own way without infringing. Ideas may sometimes be protected by confidentiality agreements instead.
Is a name or a slogan protected by copyright?
Usually not. Short phrases, names and titles are rarely original enough to be protected by copyright. They are better protected as trademarks. Logos and longer creative text are different and can attract copyright.
What is substantial copying?
Infringement requires copying of the whole or a substantial part of a work. Substantial is judged by quality as much as quantity, so copying a small but important part can still infringe. The firm will compare the works.
Is copyright the same in every country?
No, but most countries protect works from other member countries of the main international treaties, so UK works are usually protected abroad automatically. The details, such as duration and exceptions, vary.
Is software protected by copyright?
Yes. Computer programs are protected as literary works, and preparatory design material is also protected. The underlying ideas and functionality are not protected by copyright, but the code and its structure can be.
Is artificial intelligence output protected by copyright?
This is a developing area. UK law has a specific rule for computer generated works without a human author, but how it applies to modern AI tools is uncertain and under review. If AI generated content is important to your business, ask the firm about the current position.
Who owns work I paid a freelancer to create?
Usually the freelancer, unless they have signed a written assignment transferring copyright to you. You may have an implied right to use the work for its intended purpose, but not necessarily to adapt it or stop others using it. An assignment fixes this.
Who owns work created by my employees?
Usually the employer, if the work was created in the course of employment, unless there is an agreement saying otherwise. Work created outside the job is usually the employee's. Contracts of employment often make this clear.
Can I get the copyright transferred after the work is done?
Yes, if the creator agrees. A written assignment signed by the creator can be made at any time. It is easier before there is a dispute or a sale. The firm can prepare the document.
What are moral rights?
Rights that stay with the creator, including the right to be identified as the author and to object to derogatory treatment of the work. They can't be transferred but can be waived in writing, which many commissioning contracts include.
Does an agency own the logo it designed for me?
Unless your agreement assigns copyright to you, the agency or the designer may own it. This can cause problems if you want to register the logo as a trademark or change it. It is worth checking your terms.
Who owns photos taken at a commissioned shoot?
Usually the photographer, unless the contract assigns copyright to the client. The client typically gets a licence to use the photos for agreed purposes. If you need to use the photos in other ways, check the terms or ask for a wider licence or an assignment.
Someone has copied my website. What can I do?
Gather dated evidence, then consider asking the website host to remove the copied content, sending a letter to the owner or both. The firm can advise on the quickest route and whether compensation is realistic.
I received a bill for using a photo online. Do I have to pay?
Don't ignore it and don't pay before checking. If you used the image without a licence, you may owe something, but the amount demanded may not be justified. The firm can check who owns the image and what a reasonable fee would be.
I credited the photographer. Isn't that enough?
No. Crediting the creator doesn't give you permission to use the work. You need a licence unless an exception applies. Credits are good practice but not a substitute for permission.
I found the image on a search engine. Is it free to use?
No. Images found through search engines are usually protected by copyright. Only use images you have created, licensed or that are clearly offered under a licence that covers your use.
What is fair dealing?
Fair dealing covers a few limited exceptions, such as criticism and review, quotation, news reporting and parody, caricature and pastiche. The use must be fair and usually acknowledged. Commercial use for advertising rarely qualifies.
Can I sue someone for copying my work?
Yes, if they copied a substantial part without permission. Many claims are settled before court. In England and Wales, lower value claims can go to the small claims track of the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court.
What can a court award for copyright infringement?
An injunction to stop the copying, damages or the infringer's profits, delivery up of infringing copies and sometimes additional damages for flagrant infringement. The court may also order a contribution to costs.
Should I remove an image after receiving a claim?
Removing an image you can't show a licence for is usually sensible, as it stops further use. It doesn't erase past use, so the claim for that use may remain. Remove it without admitting liability and get advice on the rest.
Can a copyright claim be made against my business for something a contractor did?
Yes. If your website or marketing uses infringing content, your business can be liable even if a web designer or agency added it. You may then have a claim against the contractor under your contract with them. Check your terms.
What is the small claims track for IP?
It is part of the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court for lower value copyright, trademark and design claims. It is simpler and cheaper than other court routes, and the costs that can be recovered are very limited. Some people bring claims there without a lawyer, though advice is still useful.
What is the difference between a licence and an assignment?
A licence gives permission to use the work while the owner keeps ownership. An assignment transfers ownership completely. Both should be in writing, and an assignment must be signed by or on behalf of the owner.
Can I license my work to several businesses?
Yes, with non exclusive licences. An exclusive licence gives one licensee sole rights for the agreed scope, which usually commands a higher fee. The terms should be clear about what each licensee can do.
Do I need a contract with my photographer or designer?
It is strongly recommended. A written agreement sets out who owns the work, what you can do with it and what you pay. It avoids misunderstandings and disputes, especially if you want to use the work as a brand or sell the business later.
Can I use music in my business videos?
Only with the right licences. Music usually involves rights in the composition and the recording. Stock music services offer licences for many uses. Using commercial music without permission is a common cause of claims and takedowns.
Can I sell the copyright in my work?
Yes. Copyright can be sold by assignment, in whole or in part, for example for certain uses or territories. Before selling, consider whether a licence might give you better value by letting you keep ownership.
What should a commissioning contract say about copyright?
It should say who owns the copyright when the work is finished, what each side can do with it, whether moral rights are waived and whether the creator can show the work in a portfolio. It should also say what happens if the project ends early.

What does a design right protect?
The appearance of a product or part of a product, such as its shape, contours, lines, colours, texture, materials or decoration. It does not protect how a product works, which may be a matter for patents, or a brand name, which is a matter for trademarks.
Do I need to register a design?
Not always, because some protection is automatic. Registration gives clearer and longer protection and doesn't require proof of copying, which makes it much easier to act against lookalikes. Many businesses register their most important designs.
How long does a registered design last?
Up to 25 years, in five year periods. It must be renewed every five years by paying a renewal fee. If you stop renewing, protection ends.
How long do unregistered design rights last?
It depends on the right. UK unregistered design right for shape generally lasts up to 15 years from creation or 10 years from first sale, whichever is earlier. Supplementary unregistered design protects appearance for three years from first disclosure in the UK.
What makes a design new?
A design is new if no identical design, or one differing only in immaterial details, has been made available to the public before the filing date, subject to the 12 month grace period for the designer's own disclosures.
What is individual character?
A design has individual character if the overall impression it gives an informed user differs from the overall impression of earlier designs. The informed user is someone familiar with the products in that sector, more observant than an average consumer.
Can I protect part of a product?
Yes. A registered design can cover part of a product, such as the handle of a kettle or the sole of a shoe. The images show which part is protected, often by drawing other parts in dotted lines.
Is a design right the same as a trademark?
No. A design right protects the look of a product. A trademark protects a sign that tells customers who the product comes from, such as a name or logo. Some shapes can be both, but the rules and tests are different.

More questions people ask

When do I need to renew my trademark?
Every ten years from the filing date of the application. You can renew up to six months before the expiry date, and there is a six month late period after expiry in which you can still renew by paying an extra fee.
Will the UK IPO remind me?
The UK IPO usually sends a reminder to the address on the register. It is still your responsibility to renew. If your address has changed, or the reminder goes to a former adviser, you may never see it. Many owners ask a firm to track dates for them.
Can I renew only some classes?
Yes. You can choose to renew only the classes or goods and services you still need. This can save money. The firm can help you weigh the saving against the value of keeping wider protection.
What happens if I renew late?
If you renew within six months after expiry, you pay a late fee and the registration continues as if it had been renewed on time. After that, the mark is removed from the register and you would need to ask for restoration or file again.
Can I renew a trademark myself?
Yes. Renewals can be done online through the UK IPO. Many owners use a firm when they have several marks, when the specification needs reviewing or when they want someone else to keep track of dates.
Does renewing change my rights?
No. Renewal simply continues the registration for another ten years. It doesn't widen the goods and services or change the mark. If you need wider protection, you need a new application.
Should I renew a mark I don't use any more?
It depends. Keeping an unused mark costs renewal fees and it becomes vulnerable to revocation after five years without use. Sometimes it is still worth keeping for defensive or future reasons. The firm can help you decide.
Can I renew early to get it out of the way?
You can renew from six months before the expiry date, but not earlier. The renewal runs for ten years from the expiry date, so renewing early within that window doesn't shorten your protection.
Is the renewal date the same as the registration date?
No. The ten year period runs from the filing date of the application, not from the date the mark was registered. Check the filing date on the register or your certificate when working out when renewal is due.
Can a renewal be refused?
Renewal is an administrative step and is not examined again on its merits. As long as the request is made in time with the correct fee, the registration is renewed. Problems usually arise only from missed deadlines or unpaid fees.
