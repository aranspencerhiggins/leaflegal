Immigration explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Immigration law decides who can come to the UK, how long they can stay, what they can do while they are here and whether they can make the UK their permanent home. It touches people at some of the most important moments of their lives, from starting a new job or marrying a partner to bringing children home or becoming a British citizen. It also matters to businesses, which increasingly rely on international staff.

The rules are set by the Home Office and apply across the whole of the UK. They are long, detailed and change often. New routes open, old ones close, requirements are tightened or relaxed and fees go up. Something a friend did a few years ago may not work the same way today.

Because the stakes are high, the law in the UK says that only regulated people can give immigration advice or provide immigration services. That protects people from bad advice, which can lead to refused applications, lost fees and, in some cases, a loss of lawful status.

Where Leaflegal fits in

Leaflegal is not a law firm and is not regulated to give immigration advice. We do not give immigration advice and we do not provide immigration services. We do not ask about your immigration history and we do not assess whether you qualify for anything.

They contact you, explain how they can help and what they charge, and you decide whether to go ahead.

What a regulated adviser does

A regulated adviser listens to your situation, explains what your options might be and what each would involve, and helps you prepare and submit applications if you decide to go ahead. They can check documents, deal with the Home Office, explain decisions and help if something goes wrong. A good adviser will also tell you honestly when something is unlikely to work.

  • Couples who want to live together in the UK

We introduce people and businesses to regulated advisers across a range of immigration matters. Each matter has its own page with more general information about how things usually work.

Work and business

Individuals looking at work visas often want help understanding the routes, preparing their application or changing employer. Employers looking at sponsor licences want help applying for a licence and keeping it in good order once they have one.

Partners and families

Many enquiries are about spouse and partner visas, where a British or settled person wants their partner to join or stay with them. Others are about family visas for children, parents and other relatives.

Settlement and citizenship

People who have lived in the UK for some time often look into indefinite leave to remain, which is permission to stay without a time limit, and later British citizenship. These applications look back over a person's history, so many people like a regulated adviser to review everything first.

Refusals and appeals

If an application has been refused, there may be ways to challenge the decision, or a fresh application may be the better option. Time limits can be very short, so these enquiries are treated as urgent.

EU Settlement Scheme

Some people with status under the EU Settlement Scheme, or who think they should have it, need help with late applications, moving from pre-settled to settled status or proving their status to employers and landlords.

If your situation does not fit neatly into any of these, you can still make an enquiry. Tell us briefly what it is about, and we will introduce you to a regulated firm that can look at it.

Choosing who to trust with an immigration matter is one of the most important decisions in the whole process. The points below are general things many people look for. They apply whether you find a firm through us or on your own.

Check they are regulated

In the UK, immigration advice can be given by solicitors regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, by some other regulated legal professionals, and by advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, which used to be called the OISC. You can check solicitors on the SRA register and advisers on the Immigration Advice Authority register. Every firm we introduce is regulated.

Check their level

Advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority are registered at different levels. The level shows what kind of work they are allowed to do, from straightforward applications to complex cases and appeals. It is reasonable to ask an adviser what level they hold and whether it covers your matter.

Look for relevant experience

Immigration covers many different routes. A firm that mainly handles business immigration may not be the best fit for a complex family case, and the reverse is also true. Ask how often the firm handles matters like yours.

Ask about fees in writing

A good firm will explain its fees clearly before you commit, including what is included, what would cost extra and whether government fees are part of the quote.

Be wary of promises

Nobody can guarantee that the Home Office will grant an application. Be cautious of anyone who promises a result, asks you to pay for a job or a certificate of sponsorship, or suggests leaving information out of an application.

Questions worth asking

  • How often do you handle matters like mine?
  • Who will work on my matter day to day?
  • What is your fee, and what does it include?
  • Are government fees included or extra?
  • How will you keep me updated, and how quickly do you reply?
  • What happens if the Home Office asks for more information?
  • What is your complaints procedure?

Making complaints

Regulated firms must have a complaints procedure. If a complaint is not resolved, you may be able to take it further, for example to the Legal Ombudsman or to the adviser's regulator. It is worth knowing this before you start, even though most matters run without any problems.

Immigration matters usually involve two kinds of cost. One is what the government charges. The other is what your adviser charges, if you choose to use one.

Government fees

Most applications have an application fee. Many people also pay the Immigration Health Surcharge, which covers NHS care for the length of their visa. Employers who sponsor workers pay their own set of fees and charges. All of these change from time to time, sometimes by a lot, so always check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask your adviser.

Adviser fees

Many immigration advisers work on fixed fees for standard applications, which helps you know the cost upfront. More complex work, such as appeals or cases with a difficult history, may be charged hourly or in stages. Some firms charge a fee for an initial consultation, and others offer a short free first conversation. Ask the firm to confirm its approach in writing.

Help with costs

In some situations it may be possible to ask for a Home Office fee to be waived, and legal aid is available for some immigration matters. Both are limited, and most immigration work is privately funded. A regulated adviser can tell you whether either is worth exploring for you.

How long an immigration matter takes depends on the type of application, where it is made from and how busy the Home Office is. There are three stages to think about.

Preparing

Preparation is often the longest part, and the part you have most control over. Gathering documents, booking tests, getting translations and building up financial or relationship evidence can take weeks. Applications that are rushed are more likely to have gaps.

The Home Office decision

The Home Office publishes standard processing times on GOV.UK for many applications. Some routes have faster services for an extra fee, depending on where you apply. Complex applications, or those where the Home Office needs more information, can take longer than the standard time.

After the decision

A grant usually comes with conditions and an end date, so the next application is already on the horizon. A refusal may come with short time limits for a challenge. Either way, it pays to talk to your adviser about next steps as soon as the decision arrives.

When things are urgent

Some situations cannot wait, such as a visa ending in the next few weeks, a recent refusal, a sponsor licence suspension or a letter from the Home Office with a deadline. If your enquiry is urgent, tell us when you fill in the form so that the firm knows to prioritise it.

Planning ahead

People who start early almost always find the process less stressful. If you know an important date is coming, such as a visa ending or a planned move, contact a regulated adviser months rather than days beforehand. It gives you time to prepare properly and to deal with anything unexpected.

You do not need to have everything ready before you make an enquiry. But having some basic information to hand will make your first conversation with the adviser much more useful.

For individuals and families

  • Passports for everyone involved
  • Your current visa or status, and its end date
  • Any letters or decisions from the Home Office
  • Details of any previous applications or refusals, in the UK or elsewhere
  • Key documents such as marriage, birth or divorce certificates
  • Any job offer, if the matter is about work

For employers

  • Your company details and sector
  • The roles you want to fill and roughly what they pay
  • Whether you already hold a sponsor licence
  • Any deadlines, such as a candidate's start date
  • Any letters from the Home Office about your licence

Be open about your history

Advisers can only help properly if they know the full picture. If there is something in your past that worries you, such as a refusal, an overstay or a criminal conviction, mention it at the start. It is much better for your adviser to know early than to find out later.

Letters and deadlines

If you have received a letter or email from the Home Office, keep it safe and note any deadline straight away. Some time limits are short. Mention the deadline when you make your enquiry, and have the letter ready to send to your adviser when they get in touch.

Keeping your own records

Keep copies of every application, letter and email you send or receive about your immigration matters, along with the dates. Many people also keep a simple list of trips abroad and changes of address. Good records make it far easier for any adviser to help you, now and in future.

Ask for the fee and what it covers in writing before you agree to anything. A regulated adviser must explain their costs clearly and tell you how to complain if something goes wrong.

Do you cover the whole of the UK?
Immigration rules apply across the whole of the UK, and many immigration advisers work with clients remotely by phone, video and email. That means we can usually introduce you to a regulated adviser wherever you are in the UK, and in many cases where your family members are overseas.
Can you help if I am outside the UK?
Yes, in many cases. Lots of immigration applications are made from overseas, and regulated advisers in the UK often help people applying from abroad. Tell us where you and any family members are when you make your enquiry, so we can pass it to a suitable firm.
Is Leaflegal a law firm?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service, not a law firm or an immigration adviser. We do not give legal or immigration advice, and we do not handle applications. We pass your enquiry to one regulated firm or adviser, and they are responsible for any advice and work they carry out for you.
Who is allowed to give immigration advice?
In the UK, immigration advice can only be given by people who are regulated to do so. That includes solicitors regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, some other regulated legal professionals, and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, which was formerly the OISC. Giving immigration advice without being regulated is a criminal offence.
What is the Immigration Advice Authority?
It is the body that regulates immigration advisers who are not solicitors or other authorised legal professionals. It was previously called the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner, or OISC. It keeps a public register of advisers and the level of work each one is registered to do.
How can I check an adviser is regulated?
You can search for a solicitor or firm on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and for an adviser on the Immigration Advice Authority register. Both are free to use online. A genuine adviser will be happy to give you their registration details if you ask.
Is a solicitor better than an immigration adviser?
Not necessarily. Both can be highly experienced, and what matters most is that the person is regulated, has the right level of authorisation for your matter and has experience of cases like yours. Fees and ways of working can differ, so it is worth asking questions before you decide.
What if I am unhappy with my adviser?
Regulated firms and advisers must have a complaints procedure, and you can ask for a copy. If you are still unhappy after using it, you may be able to take your complaint further, for example to the Legal Ombudsman or to the adviser's regulator. The firm should explain how this works at the start.
Can friends or community groups help me?
People can help in practical ways, such as translating or gathering documents, but they should not give immigration advice unless they are regulated. Some charities and community organisations employ regulated advisers and can give advice. If you are unsure, ask whether the person is registered.
What questions should I ask an adviser?
Many people ask how often the adviser handles matters like theirs, who will work on the matter day to day, what the fee is and what it includes, whether government fees are extra, how they will keep in touch and what happens if the Home Office has questions. A good adviser will welcome these questions.
Is Leaflegal free?
Yes. Leaflegal is free for individuals and businesses to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction. That fee never changes what you pay the firm, and you are under no obligation to instruct them.
How much do immigration advisers charge?
It depends on the type of matter and the firm. Many advisers offer fixed fees for standard applications, while complex cases may be charged hourly or in stages. Always ask for the fee in writing, including what is covered and whether government fees are included, before you agree to anything.
Are government fees included in the adviser's price?
Usually not. Government fees, such as application fees and the Immigration Health Surcharge, are normally paid separately and can be significant. These amounts change from time to time. Ask your adviser for a full breakdown so you can see the total cost, and check the current figures on GOV.UK.
Is legal aid available for immigration?
Legal aid for immigration matters is limited, and most immigration work is privately funded. It may be available in some situations, often involving children or people in vulnerable circumstances. Whether it could apply depends on the matter and your circumstances. A regulated adviser can tell you whether it is worth exploring in your case.
Can I get a fee waiver?
In some situations, people who cannot afford a Home Office fee can ask for it to be waived. Waivers are not available for every application, and evidence of your finances is usually needed. Whether a waiver could be possible depends on the application and your circumstances, so ask a regulated adviser whether it is worth exploring.
Will my employer pay?
Some employers pay for their workers' visa costs and advisers, some pay part and some leave it to the worker. What your employer pays is a matter between you and them, so it is worth asking early. Be wary of anyone who asks you to pay for a job itself, and check with a regulated adviser before paying.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, and often sooner. If your matter is urgent, such as a visa ending soon or a recent refusal, say so on the form. We flag urgent enquiries so the firm knows to prioritise them.
My visa ends very soon. What should I do?
Leaflegal cannot advise on your situation, but we can introduce you to a regulated adviser quickly. Tell us the end date of your visa when you enquire. Your current permission and what happens when it ends can be very important, so it is best to speak to an adviser as early as possible.
I have just had a refusal. Is there a deadline?
There can be very short time limits for challenging some immigration decisions. The refusal letter usually explains any rights and deadlines. Tell us the date on the decision when you enquire, and have the letter ready when the adviser calls, so they can look at it straight away.
How long do immigration applications take?
It depends on the type of application, where it is made and how busy the Home Office is. The Home Office publishes standard processing times on GOV.UK, and some routes offer faster services for an extra fee. Preparation time matters too. Your adviser can give you a realistic idea for your matter.
Can I travel while my application is being decided?
Travel plans and pending applications can interact in ways that are not obvious, and the effect can depend on the type of application. Leaflegal cannot advise on this. If you have an application in progress, or are about to make one, ask your regulated adviser before you book any travel so that they can explain anything you need to know.
What if the Home Office asks for more information?
The Home Office sometimes writes asking for more documents or an interview, usually with a deadline. Missing that deadline can harm your application. If you receive a request like this, contact your adviser straight away. If you do not have an adviser yet, mention the deadline when you enquire.
How long does the first conversation take?
It varies between firms. Some start with a short call to understand what you need and then arrange a longer consultation. Others go into detail straight away. The firm should tell you in advance how long it expects to take and whether there is a charge.
What information do you ask for?
We ask for your contact details, the type of help you need, how urgent it is and the best time to call. We do not ask for details of your immigration history. That conversation happens directly with the regulated adviser, who is bound by confidentiality rules.
Who sees my enquiry?
Your enquiry is shared only with the one firm we introduce you to, and only with your agreement. We do not sell your details, share them with advertising platforms or pass them to several firms. You can read more about how we handle your information in our privacy notice.
Will the Home Office know I made an enquiry?
No. Leaflegal does not share enquiries with the Home Office or any other government body. Your enquiry goes only to the firm we introduce you to. What you discuss with a regulated adviser is confidential, under the rules of their regulator.
Do I have to go ahead after speaking to the firm?
No. You are free to decide whether to instruct the firm, and there is no obligation. Nothing is agreed until you accept the firm's terms. If you decide not to go ahead, you do not have to give a reason, and we will not pass your enquiry to anyone else without asking.
Can someone else make an enquiry for me?
Yes. Family members, friends or colleagues sometimes make the first enquiry on someone's behalf. The adviser will usually need to speak to the person concerned, and may need their agreement before acting for them or discussing their details.

More questions people ask

What is a family visa?
It is a general term for immigration applications where someone comes to or stays in the UK because of a family member here. It can involve children, parents, grandparents and other relatives. We cover partners on a separate page. A regulated adviser can explain what a family application might involve for your family.
Can Leaflegal tell me which visa my family needs?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and never does. We do not ask about your immigration history or assess your family's chances. We introduce you to a firm or adviser who is regulated, and they will look at your family's circumstances and explain your options.
Can you help with relatives who are overseas?
Yes. We introduce families to regulated advisers who handle matters for people inside and outside the UK. Many advisers work by phone, video and email, so it does not matter where each family member is. Tell us where your relatives are when you make your enquiry.
My question involves several family members. Is that a problem?
Not at all. Many family matters involve more than one person. Tell us briefly who is involved when you enquire. The adviser will want to understand the whole family and can explain how they would handle linked matters together.
Can I enquire on behalf of a relative?
Yes. Family members often make the first enquiry for a relative, particularly an older parent or someone abroad. The adviser will usually need to speak to the person concerned at some point, and may need their agreement before acting for them. Tell us your relationship to the person and the best way to reach you both.
Is my enquiry confidential?
Yes. We share your enquiry only with the one firm we introduce you to, and only with your agreement. We never share it with advertising platforms. The firm will treat what you tell it as confidential under the rules of its regulator.
Why do family rules change so often?
Immigration policy is reviewed regularly by the government, and the rules for family members have changed many times. Fees, guidance and requirements can all move. That is why it is risky to rely on what worked for another family in the past. A regulated adviser explains things based on the rules that apply now.
What happens at the first consultation?
The adviser will ask about your family, where everyone lives, each person's status and what you hope to achieve. They will explain how they could help and what it might cost. You do not need every document. It is also your chance to decide whether you want to work with them.
Who in the family should attend?
Usually the family member who knows the situation best, along with anyone closely involved. Relatives abroad can often join by video. Let the firm know who will be attending, and agree among yourselves who will be the main point of contact afterwards. Having a short list of questions agreed in advance helps make sure nothing important is forgotten.
Is the first consultation free?
It depends on the firm. Some offer a short free call first, and others charge for the first consultation. The firm should tell you before the consultation whether there is a fee and how much it is, so that there are no surprises.
What should we have ready?
It helps to have passports, details of any current visas, any Home Office letters and key family documents to hand. A simple family tree with names, dates of birth and where everyone lives can be very useful. The adviser will tell you what else they need afterwards.
Should we mention past problems?
Yes. Advisers can only help properly if they know the whole family's history, including past refusals or problems with earlier visas. Leaving things out can cause bigger difficulties later. What you tell a regulated adviser is confidential under the rules of their regulator.
Can we have the consultation in our own language?
Some firms have advisers who speak other languages, and some can arrange interpreters. Mention your family's preferred language when you enquire. It is important that everyone involved understands what is being explained.
What happens after the consultation?
Most firms follow up in writing with a summary of what was discussed, what they can do for each family member and what it would cost. Read it carefully and share it with anyone else involved. If you decide to go ahead, the firm will send terms of business to agree before work starts.
