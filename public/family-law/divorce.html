About divorce

Since April 2022, divorce in England and Wales has been “no fault”. One or both spouses apply, saying the marriage has broken down, and nobody has to prove the other was to blame. There is a minimum period of 20 weeks between applying and the conditional order, and a further six weeks before the final order.

The divorce itself ends the marriage, but it doesn’t deal with money, property or children. A solicitor can handle the application and, more importantly, help you sort out the finances and arrangements that go with it.

A good family solicitor listens first, explains your options calmly and helps you find the least stressful way forward, which often means agreeing things without going to court. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they can help and what it is likely to cost.

Divorce solicitors in brief

Divorce solicitors guide you through the no fault divorce process and, just as importantly, help you sort out money, property and arrangements for children. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated divorce lawyer who handles your kind of situation.

The divorce itself is often straightforward and many firms offer it for a fixed fee. The financial side usually needs more care, and a financial order such as a consent order is the only way to make a settlement legally binding. A divorce solicitor can explain how much a divorce costs in your case before you start.

Divorce explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Divorce is the legal process that ends a marriage. In England and Wales it has been based on no fault since April 2022. That means nobody has to blame the other person or give reasons. One spouse, or both together, simply tell the court that the marriage has broken down irretrievably, and the court accepts that statement.

The change was designed to reduce conflict. Under the old rules, many people had to allege unreasonable behaviour or wait years for a separation based divorce. Now the process is the same whether the decision is mutual or not, and the person receiving the application cannot stop the divorce simply by disagreeing that the marriage is over.

To apply, you must have been married for at least one year, and the marriage must be recognised in the UK. You or your spouse also need a sufficient connection to England and Wales, which usually means living here or having your permanent home here. Same sex marriages are ended in exactly the same way. Civil partnerships follow a near identical process called dissolution.

What a divorce does and does not do

A divorce changes your legal status. Once the final order is made you are no longer married and you are free to marry again. But the divorce itself does not decide who keeps the house, how savings and pensions are shared, or where the children live. Those questions are dealt with separately, although usually at the same time.

This is why many people who could manage the divorce paperwork themselves still ask a solicitor for help. The paperwork is fairly simple. The decisions around it, especially about money and timing, can have consequences that last for years.

Who usually contacts a solicitor

  • People who have decided to divorce and want to understand the steps
  • People who have received a divorce application and are unsure what to do
  • Couples who want to apply jointly and sort out finances at the same time
  • People married abroad who now live in England or Wales
  • People whose spouse has stopped responding or cannot be found
  • People who started a divorce some time ago and never finished it

Every divorce follows the same basic stages, set by law. The details depend on whether you apply alone or jointly, and on how your spouse responds. Your solicitor will explain exactly what applies to you.

1. The application

The application is usually made online through the court service, although paper forms are still available. You can apply as a sole applicant, or jointly with your spouse. You will need your marriage certificate, or a certified copy, and your spouse's address or email address if you are applying alone. If the certificate is not in English, a certified translation is usually needed.

2. Notifying the other spouse

In a sole application, the court sends the papers to the other spouse, who becomes the respondent. They are asked to complete an acknowledgement of service, usually within 14 days. In a joint application, both people have already confirmed the application, so this step is simpler.

3. The 20 week reflection period

There must be at least 20 weeks between the date the application is issued and the date the applicant asks for the conditional order. This period gives time to reflect, and is often used to start sorting out finances and arrangements for children.

4. The conditional order

After the 20 weeks, the applicant can ask for a conditional order. A judge or legal adviser checks the paperwork and, if satisfied, the court gives a date when the conditional order will be pronounced. The conditional order was previously called the decree nisi. It does not end the marriage, but it is an important stage because it allows the court to approve a financial order.

5. The final order

Once six weeks and one day have passed since the conditional order, the applicant can apply for the final order. This is the order that legally ends the marriage. It used to be called the decree absolute. Keep it safe, as you will need it to remarry or to show organisations that you are divorced.

Switching from joint to sole

Couples who start jointly sometimes find one person stops engaging. The rules allow a joint application to continue as a sole one at the conditional order or final order stage, with notice given to the other spouse. A solicitor can explain how this works.

Using a solicitor or applying yourself

You can make the application yourself, and the online system guides you through it. If you instruct a solicitor, they usually make the application through a professional version of the same system, keep track of the deadlines and handle any correspondence from the court. Some people instruct a solicitor only for the finances and handle the divorce paperwork themselves. Either approach is common.

A divorce has fixed minimum waiting periods, so even the simplest case takes a little over six months from start to finish. Many take longer, sometimes by choice.

The minimum timeline

  • Application issued by the court
  • At least 20 weeks before the conditional order can be applied for
  • Time for the court to process the conditional order application
  • At least six weeks and one day before the final order can be applied for
  • Time for the court to issue the final order

Court processing times vary and can change, so your solicitor will tell you what to expect at the time.

Why some divorces take longer

Some delays are within your control and some are not. The most common reason for a longer divorce is a deliberate pause before the final order while the finances are agreed and approved. Other reasons include the respondent not returning their acknowledgement, difficulty finding the respondent, or errors in the application that the court asks to be corrected.

If the respondent does not reply

If the other spouse does not acknowledge the application, the divorce can still go ahead, but you may need to show the court that they received the papers. Options include asking a court bailiff or a process server to deliver them, or asking the court to accept that they have been served by another method, such as by email or social media. A solicitor can explain which approach suits the situation.

If the applicant does not apply for the final order

Sometimes the applicant does not take the last step. If they have not applied for the final order, the respondent can usually apply themselves once a further three months have passed after the date the applicant could first have applied. If more than 12 months pass after the conditional order without a final order being applied for, the court will usually ask for an explanation of the delay.

Divorce and money are separate processes, but the timing of one can affect the other. This is one of the main reasons people take legal advice even when the divorce itself is agreed.

Financial claims stay open

Getting divorced does not close off financial claims between former spouses. Unless a court order deals with them, claims for a share of property, savings, pensions or income can remain open long after the final order. A financial agreement only becomes legally binding when it is approved by the court, usually as a consent order.

Pensions and the final order

Once a final order is made, you are no longer a spouse. That can mean losing rights to benefits that are only paid to a husband, wife or civil partner, such as some pension benefits if your former spouse dies. For this reason solicitors often discuss whether to delay applying for the final order until a financial order has been approved. Your solicitor will explain whether this matters in your case.

The family home

A divorce does not change who owns the home or whose name is on the mortgage. If the home is in your spouse's sole name, you may be able to protect your right to live there by registering a notice with HM Land Registry. Those rights usually end when the final order is made, unless the court orders otherwise. A solicitor can explain how this works.

Wills and remarriage

A divorce does not cancel your will. Instead, your former spouse is usually treated as if they had died, so gifts to them and their appointment as executor may no longer take effect. Many people make a new will once they separate. Remarrying before making a financial claim can also limit your own ability to claim later, so this is worth discussing with your solicitor before setting a wedding date.

Children

The divorce application asks about children, but it does not decide arrangements for them. Parents agree these separately, and only go to court if they cannot agree. Your solicitor can explain how arrangements are usually agreed.

The cost of a divorce depends on how much help you need and how straightforward the case is. It helps to think of the divorce itself separately from the finances and any children matters.

The court fee

The court charges a fee to issue a divorce application. In a joint application there is one fee. The amount changes from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask your solicitor for the current figure. If you have a low income or receive certain benefits, you may be able to get help with the fee.

Solicitor fees

Many family firms offer a fixed fee for an uncontested divorce, covering the application, the conditional order and the final order. Some offer a lower fixed fee to act for the respondent. Extra work, such as tracing a spouse who cannot be found, arranging service by a bailiff or dealing with a dispute about jurisdiction, is usually charged on top. Ask what the fixed fee includes and what would cost more.

Finances and children

Work on a financial settlement or arrangements for children is normally charged separately, often by the hour or in stages. This is where most of the cost of a separation usually sits. A good firm will give you a written estimate before work starts and update you if things change.

Who pays

Each person usually pays their own legal costs in a divorce. In some cases the applicant asks the respondent to contribute towards the costs of the divorce, but this is less common under no fault divorce. Your solicitor can explain whether this is realistic.

Most divorces go through without difficulty. These are some of the issues that can cause delay or extra cost, and that a solicitor can help you avoid or deal with.

Errors in the application

Small mistakes, such as names that do not match the marriage certificate or a missing translation, can lead the court to send the application back. Checking details carefully at the start saves time.

A spouse who cannot be found

If you have lost touch with your spouse, the court will usually expect you to make reasonable efforts to find them. This might include contacting relatives, checking social media or using a tracing agent. If they still cannot be found, the court can sometimes allow another method of service or, rarely, decide that service is not needed.

Disputes about the divorce itself

Under no fault divorce, a respondent can only dispute the divorce on limited grounds. Examples include the court not having jurisdiction, the marriage not being legally valid, or the marriage having already ended. A dispute about who is to blame is no longer a reason to stop a divorce.

Applying for the final order too soon

Applying for the final order before the finances are sorted can sometimes leave a person worse off, for example if pension benefits are lost. This is one of the most common reasons people wish they had taken advice earlier.

Religious divorces

A civil divorce does not end a religious marriage in the eyes of some faiths. In certain cases the court can delay the final order until a religious divorce has been obtained. A solicitor can explain whether this could apply.

Feeling pressured

If your spouse is pressuring or frightening you, or you are worried about your safety, contact the police on 999 in an emergency or a domestic abuse support service. A solicitor can explain how your contact details can be kept private during the divorce.

Not being sure divorce is right

Some people are not ready to divorce, or have religious or personal reasons for staying married. Judicial separation is a court process that lets a couple live apart and allows the court to deal with finances, without ending the marriage. A separation agreement is a less formal option. A solicitor can explain how these compare with divorce, so that you can decide what feels right for you.

Divorce questions and answers

How much does a divorce solicitor cost?
Many firms offer the divorce application for a fixed fee. Work on finances or children is often charged by the hour or in fixed stages. The firm explains its fees in writing before starting.
Do I need a divorce lawyer for a no fault divorce?
You can apply yourself, but a divorce lawyer helps you avoid mistakes and, most importantly, makes sure your finances are dealt with properly before the final order.
How long does a divorce take?
At least 26 weeks from application to final order because of the minimum waiting periods. Financial matters can make it longer.
Do I need a solicitor for a divorce?
You can apply online yourself. Many people use a solicitor to make sure the finances are dealt with properly and the timing of the final order doesn’t cause problems.
Can my spouse stop the divorce?
Under no fault divorce, a spouse can only dispute it on limited grounds, such as the court not having jurisdiction.
Which areas do you cover?
Family law enquiries in England and Wales.
I’m worried about my safety. Can you help?
If you are in danger, call 999. For confidential support, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free on 0808 2000 247, 24 hours a day.
Will my partner find out I made an enquiry?
No. We only share your details with the firm we introduce you to, and they contact you, not anyone else.
Can I get divorced if I have been married less than a year?
No. In England and Wales you must have been married for at least one year before applying for a divorce. If you separate within the first year, a solicitor can explain other options, such as a separation agreement, while you wait. In rare cases a marriage can be annulled, which is a different process.
Do I need to give a reason for the divorce?
No. Since April 2022 you only need to state that the marriage has broken down irretrievably. You do not have to explain why or blame anyone. The court treats the statement as conclusive evidence that the marriage has broken down. This means the application itself contains no allegations, which many people find makes the process less upsetting for everyone, including children.
Should we apply jointly or should one of us apply alone?
Both routes lead to the same result. A joint application can feel more cooperative and reduces paperwork for the respondent. A sole application can be simpler if one person is reluctant or slow to respond. A solicitor can talk through which is more practical for you, without deciding for you.
What if I cannot find my marriage certificate?
You can usually order a certified copy from the register office where you married, or from the General Register Office. If you married abroad, you may need to contact the authorities in that country. A photocopy is not normally accepted, and a certificate not in English usually needs a certified translation.
Can I divorce in England if we married abroad?
Often yes, as long as the marriage is legally recognised in the UK and you or your spouse have a sufficient connection to England and Wales, such as living here. International cases can raise questions about which country is the right place to divorce. A solicitor can explain how this works for you.
Can I apply online myself?
Yes. The court service has an online system for divorce applications, and many people use it without a solicitor. Some people still prefer a solicitor to handle the paperwork, or to advise on the finances and timing alongside it. The online service explains each step, and you can usually track progress through your account. If your situation is not straightforward, such as an overseas marriage or a spouse who cannot be found, advice can help avoid delay.
Does it matter who applies first?
Under no fault divorce there is very little practical difference. The applicant controls the timing of each step, which some people prefer. In cases with an international connection, applying first can sometimes matter, so it is worth asking a solicitor if either of you has links to another country. It can also help to think about timing.
Will my spouse find out where I live?
In a sole application you give your spouse's address, not the other way round, and you can give a correspondence address for yourself. If there are safety concerns, you can ask for your contact details to be kept confidential. If you are at risk of harm, contact the police on 999 in an emergency or a domestic abuse support service.
I have received divorce papers. What happens now?
You will usually be asked to complete an acknowledgement of service within 14 days. This confirms you have received the papers and says whether you intend to dispute the divorce. You can take advice before replying. Ignoring the papers does not stop the divorce, but it can cause delay and sometimes extra cost.
Can I stop the divorce?
Under no fault divorce, you can only dispute it on limited legal grounds, such as the court not having jurisdiction or the marriage not being valid. Disagreeing that the marriage has broken down is not a ground. A solicitor can explain whether any ground might apply. If you do not agree with the divorce, it may still help to take advice on the finances.
Do I need a solicitor if my spouse has one?
You do not have to, but many people take advice, particularly on finances. Your spouse's solicitor acts only for them and cannot advise you. Having your own solicitor can help make sure your interests are properly considered. Some people choose to take advice at key points only, such as before agreeing a financial settlement. Ask the firm whether they offer this kind of limited help.
Will I have to pay my spouse's costs?
Usually each person pays their own costs. An applicant can ask the respondent to contribute to the costs of the divorce, but under no fault divorce this is less common. If costs are claimed and you disagree, a solicitor can explain your position. Many couples agree to bear their own costs.
What if I do not agree with what the application says?
The application is much simpler than it used to be and does not include allegations. If you think a detail is wrong, such as a date or the place of marriage, you can raise it in your acknowledgement. A solicitor can help you do this correctly. Most small errors can be corrected easily.
What if I live abroad and receive divorce papers?
Divorce papers can be sent to a respondent overseas, often by email. You can usually respond online in the same way. If you think the courts of another country should deal with the divorce, take advice quickly, as delay can affect your options. A solicitor with international experience can explain how this works.
How long does a divorce take?
At least 20 weeks pass between the application being issued and the conditional order being applied for, then at least six weeks and one day before the final order. With court processing time, most divorces take seven months or more. Many take longer because people wait to resolve their finances first.
What is the difference between the conditional order and the final order?
The conditional order is the middle stage. It confirms the court sees no reason why you cannot divorce, and it allows a financial order to be approved. The final order is the document that legally ends the marriage. You remain married until the final order is made. Keep both documents safe.
Should I wait to apply for the final order?
Many people do, but it depends on your circumstances. Solicitors often discuss delaying the final order until a financial order is approved, to protect pension and other rights that depend on being married. Your solicitor will explain whether this matters in your case. You should not feel rushed, but very long delays can cause their own complications, so discuss the timing with your solicitor.
What happens if neither of us applies for the final order?
You stay married. If more than 12 months pass after the conditional order, the applicant will usually need to explain the delay when they apply. If the applicant does not apply, the respondent can usually apply after a further three months have passed. Some people discover years later that they were never finally divorced. A solicitor can help complete the process.
Can we stop the divorce if we get back together?
Yes. You can withdraw the application at any point before the final order is made. If you are unsure, your solicitor can explain how to pause or end the process. Some couples choose to pause the process while they try to reconcile, rather than withdrawing it straight away. Counselling services can help couples who want to try again.
When can I remarry?
Only once the final order has been made. Remarrying before resolving your finances can also limit your own ability to make financial claims later, so many people take advice before setting a date. If you are planning a wedding, allow enough time for the final order to be made, as court processing times can vary.
Can the 20 week period be shortened?
Only in very limited circumstances. The court can shorten the 20 week period if the applicant shows a good reason, but this is rare and is decided case by case. In most divorces the full 20 weeks applies. A solicitor can explain whether there is any realistic basis for asking.
Does the divorce decide who gets the house?
No. The divorce ends the marriage but does not divide property. That is dealt with through a financial agreement or financial court proceedings. Until then, ownership stays as it is on the title deeds, whatever happens with the divorce. If you do not own the home but live there, you may be able to protect your right to stay. A solicitor can explain how.
Can I be made to leave the family home?
Getting divorced does not by itself mean you have to leave. Both spouses usually have a right to live in the family home until the final order, or until the court decides otherwise. If there are concerns about safety, the court can make orders about who lives there. A solicitor can explain your position.
Do I need a financial order if we have no assets?
Even couples with little money sometimes choose a consent order with a clean break. This stops either person making a claim later if their circumstances change, for example after an inheritance or a pay rise. A solicitor can explain whether it is worth considering in your situation. The cost is often modest compared with the protection it gives.
What happens to my will?
Divorce does not cancel a will, but your former spouse is usually treated as if they had died. Gifts to them and their appointment as executor may no longer take effect. Many people make a new will when they separate, as the old one may not reflect their wishes. Divorce does not affect a will in the same way as marriage.
Does divorce affect arrangements for our children?
The divorce itself does not decide where children live or how they spend time with each parent. Parents usually agree this between themselves, with help from mediation or solicitors if needed. Both parents keep parental responsibility after divorce. If you cannot agree, a solicitor can explain options including mediation and, as a last resort, an application to court.
Can I go back to my previous surname?
Yes. Many people start using their previous surname again during or after a divorce. Banks and other organisations often accept your marriage certificate with your birth certificate, or a final order. Some may ask for a deed poll. You do not need a court order to change your name back.
How much does a divorce cost?
There is a court fee to apply, which changes from time to time, so check GOV.UK. Solicitors often charge a fixed fee for an uncontested divorce. Financial and children matters are charged separately and usually make up most of the total cost of a separation. A good firm will set out its fees in writing before starting.
Can I get help with the court fee?
If you have a low income, little savings or receive certain benefits, you may be able to get help with court fees. In a joint application, both people usually need to qualify. You can check eligibility on GOV.UK. You usually need to apply for help with fees before you submit the divorce application, and you will be given a reference number to use.
Is legal aid available for divorce?
Legal aid is not usually available for the divorce itself, although it can be available in some family cases involving domestic abuse. Leaflegal introduces people who are looking for paid help from a private family law firm. If you think you may qualify for legal aid, you can check on GOV.UK before deciding how to proceed.

You agree your fees directly with the firm, and they will explain them before you decide whether to go ahead. There is no obligation to instruct them after the first conversation.
What will the solicitor need from me?
Usually your marriage certificate, your spouse's contact details, the date you separated and a rough idea of your finances and any children. If you have received papers from the court, have them ready. Do not worry if you do not have everything at the first conversation. The more you can share, the more useful the advice.
Is everything split 50 50?
Not automatically. The court looks at a range of factors, including needs, resources, contributions and the length of the marriage. Equal sharing of assets built up during the marriage is often a starting point, but needs, especially housing for children, frequently lead to a different split. A solicitor can explain how this works in your situation.
Do we need a court order if we agree?
An agreement is not legally binding on its own. To make it binding and close off future claims, it is usually turned into a consent order approved by the court. Without one, claims can remain open for years, even after the divorce is final. The order also gives both people a clear way to enforce the terms if they are not followed.
When should we sort out finances?
Many couples start during the divorce, often in the 20 week reflection period. A consent order cannot be approved until the conditional order has been made. Solicitors often discuss delaying the final order until the financial order is in place, to protect certain rights. Starting early can also make the separation feel more settled for everyone.
What if we divorced years ago without a financial order?
Financial claims can stay open after divorce unless a court order has dealt with them. A former spouse may still be able to apply, although delay is one of the things the court takes into account. If you remarried before making a claim, your own claims may be limited. A solicitor can explain your position.
Does it matter whose name things are in?
Less than many people think. In a divorce, the court can look at assets held by either spouse, whether they are in joint names or one name. Legal ownership is still relevant, but it does not decide how assets will be divided. Assets held by companies or trusts can also be relevant. A solicitor can explain how ownership affects your case.
Does the reason for the divorce affect the money?
Usually not. The court only takes conduct into account in rare cases where it would be unfair to ignore it, such as serious financial misconduct. Most of the time, who was to blame for the breakdown makes no difference to the settlement. Under no fault divorce, the reasons for the breakdown are not part of the divorce application either.
Are unmarried couples treated the same way?
No. Unmarried couples do not have the same financial claims when they separate, however long they lived together. Disputes about a shared home are usually decided under property law. There may be claims for the benefit of children. A solicitor can explain the difference. This is why some couples who live together choose to make a cohabitation agreement.
Can we agree a settlement without solicitors?
Some couples do reach agreement themselves, sometimes with a mediator. To become legally binding, the agreement still needs to be turned into a consent order and approved by the court. Many people ask a solicitor to check the agreement and draft the order, so that it does what they intend.
