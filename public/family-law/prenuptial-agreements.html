About prenuptial agreements

A prenuptial agreement sets out how you would divide your assets if the marriage ended. They are not automatically binding in England and Wales, but the courts will usually give effect to one that was freely entered into, with full understanding, and is fair.

Getting it right means both of you giving full financial disclosure, taking independent advice and signing well before the wedding.

A good family solicitor listens first, explains your options calmly and helps you find the least stressful way forward, which often means agreeing things without going to court. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they can help and what it is likely to cost.

Prenup in brief

A prenup, or prenuptial agreement, sets out how money and property will be divided if a marriage or civil partnership ends. In the UK, courts will usually uphold a fair prenup that both people entered freely with full disclosure and proper advice.

Allow plenty of time before the wedding. Each person should have their own solicitor, and the prenuptial agreement should be signed well in advance.

Prenuptial agreements explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A prenuptial agreement, often called a prenup, is a written agreement made by a couple before they marry. It sets out how their money and property would be dealt with if the marriage ended. Couples planning a civil partnership can make a similar agreement, usually called a pre-registration agreement.

For many people the idea of a prenup feels unromantic. In practice, more and more couples see it as a sensible conversation about money at the start of a shared life. It can bring clarity, reduce the risk of a long and costly dispute later, and give reassurance to families who are passing on wealth or a business.

Prenups are not automatically binding in England and Wales. A court dealing with a divorce or dissolution still has the final say over the finances. But since a 2010 Supreme Court decision, the courts will usually give effect to a prenup that was freely entered into by each person with a full understanding of what it meant, unless it would not be fair to hold them to it in the circumstances at the time.

That means a well prepared prenup can carry a great deal of weight. A badly prepared one, signed in a hurry or without proper information, may carry very little. The way the agreement is made matters as much as what it says.

Without a prenup, a court dealing with a divorce looks at a range of factors to decide what is fair. These include the length of the marriage, each person's needs and resources, the contributions each has made and the needs of any children. The starting point in many cases is that assets built up during the marriage are shared, while assets brought in from outside may be treated differently. A prenup allows a couple to set out their own view of what would be fair, in advance and while they are on good terms.

It is also worth knowing what a prenup is not. It is not a prediction that the marriage will fail, and it does not have to be one sided. Many prenups are carefully balanced, protecting what each person brings while making sure both would be properly provided for if things went wrong.

Why couples make one

  • One or both have significant savings, property or investments
  • One of them owns or expects to inherit a share in a family business
  • They expect to receive an inheritance or gift from family
  • One or both have children from a previous relationship
  • One or both have been through a difficult divorce before
  • Their families have asked for wealth to be protected
  • They simply want certainty and an open conversation about money

Because a court can decide not to follow a prenup, the aim is to make an agreement that a court is likely to respect. Your solicitor will explain what this means for you. These are the features that are generally seen as important.

Free choice

Each person must enter the agreement of their own free will. Pressure, threats or emotional manipulation can undermine it. Signing very close to the wedding, when invitations have gone out and money has been spent, can make it look as though one person had no real choice.

Full understanding

Each person needs to understand what the agreement means and how it might affect them. That is why independent legal advice for each of you is strongly recommended. Each solicitor advises only their own client and can explain what that person may be giving up compared with the position without an agreement.

Financial disclosure

Each of you is usually expected to share a full picture of your income, assets and debts, often with supporting documents and values. Without this, one person may later argue they did not know what they were agreeing to. Some couples choose not to exchange full details, but that can increase the risk that the agreement is given less weight.

Fairness

Even a properly made prenup may not be followed if holding the couple to it would be unfair at the time of the divorce. A court will make sure that the needs of each person and any children are met. An agreement that leaves one spouse unable to house themselves or the children is unlikely to be upheld in full.

Timing

Many solicitors aim to have a prenup signed at least 28 days before the wedding, following recommendations made by the Law Commission. Starting the process several months ahead leaves time for disclosure, negotiation and advice without anyone feeling rushed.

None of this guarantees how a court would treat an agreement many years from now. Circumstances change, and the court will look at the position at the time. What a carefully made agreement does is give both of you the strongest possible starting point and a clear record of what you intended.

Every prenup is tailored to the couple. Some are short and deal with a single asset such as a house. Others are detailed and cover a wide range of situations. Your solicitor will help you think about what to include.

Before drafting starts, your solicitor will usually ask what you are worried about and what you want to protect. Some people want to ring fence a single property. Others want a framework for everything they own. Being clear about your aims helps keep the agreement focused and can reduce the cost.

Separate property

The core of many prenups is a list of assets that each person wants to keep as their own. This often includes property owned before the marriage, inheritances, gifts from family, trust interests and shares in a business. The agreement can say how these will be treated and whether any growth in value is shared.

Matrimonial property

Prenups often also deal with property built up during the marriage, such as a family home bought together, joint savings and pensions. The agreement may say these will be shared equally, in set proportions or according to a formula.

The family home

The home is often the most emotional asset. A prenup can set out who would keep it, whether it would be sold and how any contributions to the deposit or mortgage would be treated.

Income and maintenance

Some agreements say whether either person would pay maintenance to the other, how much and for how long. Courts are particularly careful about provisions that limit maintenance, because the needs of a financially weaker spouse can change a lot over a long marriage.

Children

A prenup cannot decide arrangements for children or reduce the support a child is entitled to. The court will always put the children's welfare first. Many agreements recognise this and leave room for changes if children are born.

Review clauses

Lots of agreements include a clause to review the terms after a certain number of years or when a child is born. This helps the agreement stay fair as life changes, which in turn makes it more likely to be respected.

The process is usually straightforward, though it needs time. Your solicitor will guide you through the steps and tell you what is needed from you.

Throughout the process you will be dealing with your own solicitor, and your partner with theirs. Many couples find it helps to agree the broad principles between themselves first, so that the solicitors' work is about getting the detail right rather than starting from scratch.

1. First meeting

You talk to a family solicitor about your relationship, your finances, your plans and what you want the agreement to achieve. They will also ask about your wedding date so that a timetable can be planned.

2. Disclosure

Each of you gathers information about your assets, income and debts. This might include property valuations, pension statements, bank statements and business accounts. The information is usually exchanged between the two solicitors.

3. Drafting

One solicitor prepares the first draft. It will reflect what you have discussed and the advice given. Drafting carefully matters because vague wording can lead to arguments later.

4. Negotiation

The other person's solicitor reviews the draft and advises their client. There may be some back and forth before both of you are happy. This is normal and is part of what makes the agreement robust.

5. Signing

The agreement is usually signed as a deed, with each signature witnessed. Each solicitor may also provide a certificate or letter confirming that advice was given. Copies are kept safely by both of you.

6. After the wedding

Keep a copy somewhere safe and revisit the agreement when life changes significantly, or on the review dates it sets.

The cost of a prenup depends on how complex your finances are, how much negotiation is needed and whether there are features such as trusts, businesses or property abroad. A simple agreement between two people with clear finances will usually cost much less than one involving family wealth across several countries.

How firms usually charge

Some firms offer a fixed fee for drafting a standard prenup, with extra charges if negotiation goes beyond a certain point. Others charge by the hour. The person whose solicitor drafts the agreement often pays more than the person whose solicitor reviews it, though both should expect to pay for proper advice.

Things that affect the cost

  • The number and type of assets involved
  • Business interests or family trusts
  • Property or assets outside England and Wales
  • How much negotiation is needed
  • How quickly disclosure is provided
  • Whether specialist valuations or tax advice are needed

Ask the firm to explain its fees and what is included before you commit. A good firm will confirm this in writing and will tell you if the estimate is likely to change.

Some couples see the cost of a prenup as a form of insurance. Compared with the cost and stress of a contested financial dispute on divorce, a well prepared agreement may be money well spent. Whether it makes sense for you is your decision, and the firm can help you understand what you would be paying for.

Most problems with prenups come from how they were made rather than what they say. Being aware of the common pitfalls can help you avoid them.

Leaving it too late

Starting a few weeks before the wedding puts pressure on everyone. It can also give one person grounds to argue later that they had no real choice. Starting early is one of the most helpful things a couple can do.

No independent advice

If only one person has a solicitor, the other may later say they did not understand the agreement. Separate advice for each person is widely recommended.

Incomplete disclosure

Leaving out an asset, undervaluing a business or failing to mention a likely inheritance can undermine the agreement. Openness at the start is usually the best protection.

Terms that do not meet needs

An agreement that leaves one person in real hardship, especially if they are caring for children, is unlikely to be followed in full. Agreements that make sure each person's basic needs are met tend to stand on firmer ground.

Not keeping records

If assets that were meant to stay separate are mixed with joint money, for example by paying an inheritance into a joint account or using it to pay off the family mortgage, it can become harder to show they were kept apart. Your solicitor can explain how your actions during the marriage might affect how the agreement works.

Never reviewing it

A prenup signed before children, a career change or a move abroad may look very different years later. Reviewing it keeps it in step with your lives.

What is a prenuptial agreement?
It is a written agreement made before marriage that sets out how a couple's money and property would be dealt with if the marriage ended. It often protects assets one person brings into the marriage or expects to receive. The equivalent for civil partners is usually called a pre-registration agreement.
Are prenups legally binding in England and Wales?
Not automatically. A court dealing with a divorce still decides the finances. However, the courts will usually give effect to a prenup that each person entered freely, with a full understanding of its effect, unless it would be unfair to hold them to it. A properly prepared agreement can carry a great deal of weight.
Do only wealthy people need a prenup?
No. Prenups are common where there is family wealth, but many couples with modest assets make one too. Someone who owns a flat, has savings from before the marriage or has children from a previous relationship may want clarity. The decision is a personal one. A solicitor can explain whether an agreement would make a real difference for you.
Is it unromantic to ask for a prenup?
Many people worry about this. Couples who go through the process often say the open conversation about money brought them closer. A solicitor can explain how to raise the subject and how the process works, so that it feels practical rather than adversarial. Raising it early, well before the wedding, usually makes the conversation easier for both of you.
Can civil partners make a similar agreement?
Yes. Couples entering a civil partnership can make a pre-registration agreement, which works in the same way as a prenup. Civil partners can also make an agreement after registration, similar to a postnuptial agreement. The same safeguards apply, including full disclosure, independent advice for each person and signing well before the registration date. The court approaches these agreements on dissolution in the same way as a prenup on divorce.
Can a prenup protect a future inheritance?
A prenup can say how an inheritance received during the marriage should be treated. Inherited assets are often treated differently from those built up together, but a court will still consider whether both people's needs are met. A solicitor can explain how an inheritance might be dealt with. Keeping an inheritance separate during the marriage can also help.
Can a prenup protect my business?
A prenup can set out how a business owned before the marriage, or a share in a family business, should be treated. This is one of the most common reasons people make one. A valuation of the business may be needed as part of disclosure. The agreement can also deal with any growth in the business during the marriage and whether that is shared.
What happens if we do not have a prenup?
If the marriage ends, the court decides how to divide the finances using a set of factors, including needs, contributions and the length of the marriage. Assets built up during the marriage are often shared. Assets brought in from outside may be treated differently, but there is no certainty. A prenup lets you set out your own view in advance.
How far before the wedding should we sign?
As early as possible. Many solicitors aim for it to be signed at least 28 days before the wedding, and starting several months ahead is common. Signing close to the wedding can suggest pressure and may weaken the agreement. If your wedding is very close, a solicitor can explain the options, which may include signing a postnuptial agreement after the wedding instead.
Do we both need our own solicitor?
It is strongly recommended. One solicitor cannot usually advise both of you because your interests are different. Each of you having independent advice helps show that you both understood what you were agreeing to. The cost of separate advice is usually seen as a worthwhile part of making the agreement robust. Your partner is free to choose their own firm.
Do we have to share full financial details?
Full and frank disclosure is generally recommended. It helps show each person knew what they were agreeing to. Some couples agree to limited disclosure, but that can make the agreement easier to challenge later. Your solicitor will explain the risks. Disclosure is usually exchanged between the two solicitors, and it is kept confidential.
What information will I need to provide?
Usually details of property, savings, investments, pensions, business interests, income and debts, with supporting documents. You may also be asked about expected inheritances or trust interests. Your solicitor will give you a list. Recent statements and valuations are best, and estimates are often fine at the start. If something is hard to value, such as a business or a trust interest, your solicitor will explain how it is usually dealt with.
How long does the process take?
It depends on how complex your finances are and how quickly information is exchanged. A straightforward agreement may take a few weeks of work. Complex agreements can take longer. Starting several months before the wedding gives everyone room. Delays are most often caused by waiting for documents or valuations, so gathering these early can help keep things moving.
Can my parents be involved?
Parents often encourage a prenup to protect family wealth, and they may provide information about trusts or gifts. However, the agreement is between the couple, and each person must decide freely. Too much family pressure can undermine an agreement. Your solicitor will usually speak to you on your own so they can be sure the decisions are yours.
What if we are marrying abroad?
You can still make a prenup in England and Wales. If either of you has links to another country, or you are marrying or living abroad, the agreement may need advice from a lawyer in that country too. Your solicitor will explain the issues. Some countries treat prenups very differently, so it is worth raising this at the start.
Can we use an online template?
Templates exist, but a prenup is only as good as the process behind it. Without independent advice and proper disclosure, a template agreement may carry little weight. Many couples use a template as a starting point for discussion and ask solicitors to prepare the final version. A solicitor can also check whether a template covers everything that matters to you.
What if my partner does not want a prenup?
Nobody can be forced to sign one, and pressure can undermine an agreement. Many couples find the conversation easier once they understand that a prenup can be balanced and protect both people. A solicitor can explain how the process usually works, which may help you both decide. In the end it is a decision for both of you.
Can a prenup deal with children?
A prenup cannot decide where children will live or limit the financial support a child is entitled to. A court will always put children's welfare first. An agreement can recognise that children may change things and include a review when they are born. Many couples find it reassuring to see the children's needs expressly protected in the agreement.
Can it cover maintenance?
Yes, a prenup can say whether either person would pay maintenance and on what terms. Courts look carefully at these provisions, especially after a long marriage or where one person has given up work to care for children. Meeting real needs remains important. Some agreements set out a fixed sum or period, while others leave maintenance to be decided based on needs at the time. Your solicitor can explain the approach that suits your circumstances.
Can it cover the family home?
Yes. A prenup can say who would keep the home, how it would be sold and how deposits or contributions would be treated. Because the home is often needed to house children, the court will still consider whether the terms meet everyone's needs. Some agreements treat the home as shared even if other assets are kept separate.
Can a prenup cover pensions?
Yes, it can say how pensions built up before and during the marriage would be treated. Pensions can be complex to value and divide. Your solicitor may suggest specialist input if pensions are a significant part of the finances. Some couples agree that pensions built up before the marriage stay separate while those built up during it are shared. A court order would still be needed to actually divide a pension on divorce.
Can it decide what happens if one of us dies?
A prenup deals mainly with what happens on divorce. What happens on death is dealt with through wills. Many couples review or make wills alongside their prenup, especially if they want to protect children from a previous relationship. Marriage usually cancels an existing will in England and Wales, unless it was made in expectation of that marriage. That is another reason many couples deal with wills at the same time as their prenup.
Can we include a sunset clause?
Some agreements say they will end or change after a certain number of years. Others include review dates instead. Your solicitor can explain the pros and cons of each approach. A sunset clause can give certainty but may also mean the protection ends when it is still wanted. A review clause is more flexible because it asks you to look at the agreement again rather than ending it automatically. The right choice depends on your aims.
Can a prenup deal with debts?
Yes. It can record who is responsible for debts each person brings into the marriage, such as student loans or business borrowing, and how debts taken on during the marriage would be shared. It does not change what you owe a lender, which depends on whose name the debt is in.
What is a postnuptial agreement?
It is an agreement made after marriage that works in a similar way to a prenup. Couples make one if they did not have time before the wedding, if circumstances change, or to update an earlier prenup. The same safeguards of disclosure, advice and fairness apply. Your solicitor can explain how it would work for you.
Can we change our prenup later?
Yes. If both of you agree, you can update or replace the agreement, usually with a postnuptial agreement. This is common after the birth of a child, a large inheritance or a move abroad. Any change should be made in writing with fresh disclosure and advice, so that the new agreement carries the same weight as the original. Informal changes, such as a side letter or a verbal promise, can cause confusion later.
What happens to the prenup if we divorce?
One or both of you would usually ask the court to make a financial order that reflects the agreement. The court will consider whether the agreement was properly made and whether it is fair to follow it. Many couples use it as the basis for a consent order. The judge will then decide whether to approve the order.
Can a court ignore a prenup?
A court can decide not to follow a prenup, or to follow only part of it, for example if it was signed under pressure, if disclosure was missing, or if the terms would leave someone in real need. Making the agreement properly reduces this risk. It cannot be ruled out completely, but it is much less likely.
Should we review our prenup?
It is sensible to review it when your circumstances change significantly, such as having children, changing careers or receiving a large inheritance. Many agreements build in review points so this happens as a matter of course. A review does not always mean the terms change. Sometimes it simply confirms that the agreement still reflects what you both want. If changes are needed, they are usually made through a postnuptial agreement with fresh disclosure and advice.
Does a prenup affect our finances during the marriage?
Not usually. A prenup is mainly about what would happen if the marriage ended. During the marriage you can manage your money as you choose. However, how you treat separate assets, such as mixing them with joint funds, can affect how the agreement works later. Your solicitor can explain this when the agreement is made.
How much does a prenup cost?
It depends on how complex your finances are and how much negotiation is needed. Some firms offer fixed fees for straightforward agreements. Others charge by the hour. Ask for a written estimate before work begins. The estimate should say what is included, how many rounds of negotiation it covers and what would cost extra. Your partner will usually pay their own solicitor separately for independent advice.

What is the difference between dissolution and divorce?
Dissolution is the legal process for ending a civil partnership, and divorce is the process for ending a marriage. The steps are almost identical. Both are no fault, both use the same online system and both have the same minimum waiting periods. The orders have the same names, and financial and children matters are dealt with in the same way.
How long do we need to have been civil partners before applying?
You cannot apply for dissolution until your civil partnership has lasted at least one year. If you separate before then, you can still make arrangements about money and children, for example through a separation agreement. Your solicitor can explain what is possible in the meantime and how to prepare for the application.
Do I need to give a reason for ending the civil partnership?
No. Since April 2022 you only need to state that the civil partnership has broken down irretrievably. You do not have to give details of behaviour or separation, and your civil partner is not blamed. This is intended to reduce conflict and allow couples to focus on practical arrangements. Nobody has to prove fault.
Can we apply together?
Yes. Civil partners can make a joint application if they both agree that the relationship has ended. A joint application can feel more cooperative. If one of you later decides not to continue jointly, the process can usually carry on as a sole application. Your solicitor can explain how this works in practice.
Can my civil partner stop the dissolution?
Only in limited circumstances. A civil partner can dispute the application on grounds such as the court not having jurisdiction, the civil partnership not being legally valid or it having already ended. They cannot dispute it simply because they do not want it to end. A solicitor can explain what to do if your civil partner objects.
What if I do not know where my civil partner is?
The court usually needs the papers to be served on your civil partner. If you cannot find them, there are ways to try to trace them or ask the court to allow another method of service. In some cases the court can dispense with service. Your solicitor can explain the options and what evidence might be needed.
Can opposite sex couples dissolve a civil partnership?
Yes. Opposite sex couples have been able to form civil partnerships in England and Wales since the end of 2019. The dissolution process is the same for every civil partnership, whatever the sex of the partners. The financial claims and arrangements for children are also the same. Your solicitor can explain the steps.
Is there an alternative to dissolution?
Some civil partners who do not want to end the relationship legally choose a separation agreement or apply for a separation order, which allows the court to deal with some financial matters without dissolving the civil partnership. In rare cases a civil partnership may be annulled. Your solicitor can explain whether any of these is relevant to you.
