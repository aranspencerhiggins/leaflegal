About child arrangements

When parents separate, they need to agree where the children will live and how much time they will spend with each parent. Most families manage this without going to court, sometimes with the help of mediation.

Where agreement isn’t possible, a parent can apply for a child arrangements order, usually after attending a mediation information meeting. A family solicitor will explain the process and help you put forward arrangements that work for your children.

A good family solicitor listens first, explains your options calmly and helps you find the least stressful way forward, which often means agreeing things without going to court. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they can help and what it is likely to cost.

Child arrangements order in brief

A child arrangements order sets out who a child lives with and spends time with. What people often call child custody is now dealt with this way in England and Wales. Most families agree arrangements without court, and a family solicitor can help.

If you cannot agree, you usually need to consider mediation before applying to court. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated family solicitor who handles child arrangements.

Child arrangements explained

Pick a topic to read more.

When parents separate, they need to decide where their children will live and how much time the children will spend with each parent. These decisions are known as child arrangements. They can cover weekdays and weekends, school holidays, birthdays and festivals, how handovers happen, and how children keep in touch by phone or video with the parent they are not with.

Most separated parents agree arrangements without going to court. Some manage this between themselves, while others use mediation or ask solicitors to help them reach an agreement. Where parents cannot agree, either can apply to the Family Court, which can make a child arrangements order. The court's focus throughout is the welfare of the child, not the wishes of either parent.

This can be an emotional and worrying time. Parents often feel a mix of grief, anger and fear about losing time with their children. A family solicitor who regularly handles children matters can explain how things usually work, help you put forward proposals that focus on your children, and support you if a court application becomes necessary.

If you are worried about safety

If you or your children are in immediate danger, call the police on 999. If you are experiencing domestic abuse, a support service such as the National Domestic Abuse Helpline can help you plan for your safety. If you are worried a child is at risk of harm, you can also contact your local council's children's services. Tell your solicitor about any safety concerns at the very start.

Who usually asks for help

  • Parents who have just separated and want to agree a fair routine
  • Parents whose existing arrangements have broken down
  • Parents who are being prevented from seeing their children
  • Parents worried about a child's safety during contact
  • Parents who want to move home, or move abroad, with their children
  • Grandparents and other relatives who want to stay in a child's life

Parental responsibility is the legal term for the rights, duties and authority a parent has for a child. It covers important decisions such as where the child goes to school, what medical treatment they receive, their religion and their name. Understanding who has it is a useful starting point in any children matter.

Who has parental responsibility

  • A mother automatically has parental responsibility
  • A father has it if he was married to the mother when the child was born
  • An unmarried father has it if he is named on the birth certificate for a birth registered in England and Wales after 1 December 2003
  • A second female parent can have it through marriage, civil partnership or being named on the birth certificate
  • Others can gain it through a formal agreement with the mother, or by a court order

Step parents, grandparents and other relatives do not have parental responsibility automatically, although they can obtain it in some circumstances, for example through a child arrangements order saying the child lives with them.

What it means in practice

Day to day decisions, such as bedtimes and what a child eats, are usually made by whichever parent the child is with. Important decisions, such as changing school, moving to a new area or a major medical procedure, should usually be discussed with everyone who has parental responsibility. Separation does not take parental responsibility away from either parent.

Parental responsibility and contact are different

Having parental responsibility does not decide how much time a parent spends with a child. A parent can have parental responsibility and see the child rarely, or spend a lot of time with a child without having it. A solicitor can explain how the two fit together in your situation.

Arrangements agreed between parents tend to work better and last longer than those imposed by a court. They can be shaped around the family's routines and changed as children grow up. The court also has a principle that it should only make an order if doing so is better for the child than making no order at all.

Parenting plans

Many parents find it helpful to write down what they have agreed in a parenting plan. A parenting plan is not legally binding, but it gives everyone clarity. It might cover regular time with each parent, school holidays, special occasions, how parents will communicate, handover arrangements, and how decisions about schooling or health will be made.

Help to agree

Mediation is often used to reach agreement about children. A mediator helps both parents talk and focus on the children's needs. Some mediators also offer child inclusive mediation, where a specially trained mediator meets the child so that their views can be taken into account. A government scheme has at times offered a contribution towards mediation costs for children cases, so it is worth asking a mediator whether it is currently available.

Solicitors can also help parents agree. They can exchange proposals, suggest practical solutions and help keep discussions focused on the children. Where agreement is reached, it can be recorded in writing or, in some cases, made into a consent order.

Parenting courses

There are online and in person courses for separated parents that help with communication and understanding how separation affects children. In court cases, parents may be directed to attend one. Many parents find them useful even without a court case.

When agreement is not safe or possible

Mediation and direct negotiation are not suitable for everyone. Where there has been domestic abuse, coercive control or serious concerns about a child's safety, a solicitor can explain other ways forward, including applying to court without first attending mediation.

Arrangements that change over time

What works for a toddler rarely works for a teenager. Young children often do better with shorter, more frequent time with each parent, while older children may want a say in how their weekends and holidays are arranged around school, friends and activities. Many parents build in a regular review of their arrangements, for example at the start of each school year, so that changes can be discussed calmly rather than in a crisis.

If parents cannot agree, either can apply to the Family Court. Court proceedings about children follow a set structure, although the details vary between areas. Your solicitor will explain how your local court is likely to deal with your case.

Before applying

In most cases, the person applying must first attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting, known as a MIAM. Exemptions apply in some situations, including where there is evidence of domestic abuse, a risk to a child, or genuine urgency.

Types of order

  • A child arrangements order, which decides who a child lives with and who they spend time with
  • A specific issue order, which decides a particular question, such as which school a child attends
  • A prohibited steps order, which stops a parent doing something, such as taking a child abroad

Safeguarding checks

After an application is made, Cafcass in England or Cafcass Cymru in Wales usually carries out safeguarding checks with the police and the local council. An officer usually speaks to each parent by phone before the first hearing and sends a short letter to the court about any risks.

The first hearing

The first hearing is usually short. The judge or magistrates try to identify what is agreed and what is not, consider any safety concerns and decide what happens next. Some cases are resolved at this hearing. In others the court may ask for a more detailed report about the child, known as a section 7 report, or direct the parents to attend a parenting course.

Fact finding hearings

Where there are serious allegations, such as domestic abuse, the court may hold a hearing to decide whether the allegations are true before deciding arrangements. The outcome can significantly affect the type of contact that is safe.

Later hearings

A dispute resolution hearing is usually held once reports are ready, to see whether agreement can be reached. If not, the case goes to a final hearing, where the court hears evidence and decides. In some areas courts are trying a newer approach that gathers information about the child earlier and aims to reduce the number of hearings.

What the court considers

The child's welfare is the court's paramount consideration. The court looks at a list of factors, including the child's wishes and feelings in light of their age and understanding, their physical, emotional and educational needs, the likely effect of any change, any harm they have suffered or are at risk of, and how capable each parent is of meeting their needs. The court also bears in mind that delay is generally harmful to children.

How long an order lasts

A child arrangements order usually lasts until the child turns 16, and an order saying who a child lives with can last until they turn 18. Parents can agree to vary arrangements informally, and either parent can ask the court to change an order if circumstances change significantly.

The cost of a children matter depends on whether arrangements can be agreed and, if not, how far a court case goes. A case that settles at the first hearing costs far less than one that needs a fact finding hearing and a final hearing.

How firms charge

Most firms charge for children work by the hour or in stages, with an estimate for each stage. Some offer a fixed fee for an initial meeting, for drafting a parenting plan or letter, or for preparing for and attending a particular hearing. Ask the firm to set out what is included in writing.

Court and other fees

There is a court fee to apply for most children orders. Other costs may include mediation, parenting courses, and barristers if the case goes to a contested hearing. Court fees change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask the firm. If you have a low income, you may be able to get help with court fees.

Legal aid

Legal aid can be available in some children cases, for example where there is evidence of domestic abuse or child abuse, depending on income and savings. GOV.UK has a tool to check. Leaflegal introduces people who are looking for paid help from a private family law firm.

Who pays

In children cases each parent usually pays their own costs. The court rarely orders one parent to pay the other's costs, although it can where someone has behaved unreasonably in the proceedings.

Children matters can be painful, and problems often arise even after arrangements have been agreed. These are some situations families commonly face.

Contact being stopped

Sometimes one parent stops or limits the other parent's time with the children, perhaps because of a disagreement or a worry about safety. Where there is an order, the court can be asked to enforce it. Where there is no order, the parent can apply for one. A solicitor can explain the options.

Concerns about safety

If you are worried that a child is not safe with the other parent, tell your solicitor straight away. The court takes allegations of harm seriously and can order supervised contact, contact at a centre, indirect contact such as letters or calls, or no contact at all where that is necessary to protect the child. In an emergency call the police on 999.

Moving away

A parent who wants to move a significant distance within the UK, or to another country, with the children may need the other parent's agreement or the court's permission. Relocation cases are complex and the court looks closely at the effect on the child's relationship with both parents.

Children refusing to go

Older children sometimes say they do not want to spend time with a parent. The court listens to children's wishes in light of their age and understanding, but those wishes are not the only factor. A solicitor can explain how this is usually approached.

Money and contact

Child maintenance and time with children are separate. A parent cannot usually stop contact because maintenance has not been paid, and a parent cannot stop paying because they are not seeing the children. Child maintenance is usually arranged between parents or through the Child Maintenance Service.

Child arrangements questions and answers

Do I have to try mediation first?
Usually you must attend a mediation information meeting before applying to court, unless an exemption applies.
What does the court focus on?
The child’s welfare is the court’s main concern. It will consider a range of factors, including the child’s wishes and needs.
Can I take my child abroad?
If there is a child arrangements order saying the child lives with you, you can usually take them abroad for a short period. Otherwise you may need consent or a court order.
Which areas do you cover?
Family law enquiries in England and Wales.
I’m worried about my safety. Can you help?
If you are in danger, call 999. For confidential support, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free on 0808 2000 247, 24 hours a day.
Will my partner find out I made an enquiry?
No. We only share your details with the firm we introduce you to, and they contact you, not anyone else.
Do I need a court order to see my child?
No. Most parents agree arrangements without a court order, and the court only makes an order if it is better for the child than no order. An order may be helpful if agreement cannot be reached or an existing arrangement keeps breaking down. A solicitor can explain the options. Many parents find agreement easier with help.
Is there a standard arrangement, such as every other weekend?
There is no standard arrangement. Every family is different, and arrangements depend on the children's ages, needs, schooling, the distance between homes and each parent's work. Many families share time in a range of ways. A solicitor can explain how arrangements are usually worked out. Arrangements often change as children grow.
What is a parenting plan?
It is a written record of what parents have agreed about the children, such as where they will live, school holidays, special occasions and how parents will communicate. It is not legally binding, but it can help avoid misunderstandings. Templates are available online, and solicitors and mediators can help prepare one.
Do unmarried fathers have rights to see their children?
An unmarried father can apply to the court for a child arrangements order whether or not he has parental responsibility. Whether he has parental responsibility depends mainly on whether he is named on the birth certificate for a birth registered after 1 December 2003. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Can grandparents apply to see their grandchildren?
Grandparents do not have an automatic right to see grandchildren, but they can ask the court for permission to apply for a child arrangements order. The court considers things like their connection with the child and whether the application could disrupt the child's life. Many grandparents use mediation first. Many disputes settle this way.
Does separation change parental responsibility?
No. Both parents keep parental responsibility after separation or divorce if they had it before. Important decisions, such as changing school or a major medical treatment, should usually be discussed with everyone who has parental responsibility. Parental responsibility usually lasts until a child turns 18. A solicitor can explain how decisions are usually shared between separated parents.
Can I change my child's surname?
If more than one person has parental responsibility, a child's surname is not usually changed without the agreement of all of them or the court's permission. The court looks at what is best for the child. A solicitor can explain how this works. A child's own views may also be relevant, depending on their age and understanding.
Does it matter who the children lived with before we separated?
The court looks at the child's welfare now and in the future, including the likely effect of any change. Who has been the main carer in the past is often relevant to that, but it does not decide the outcome on its own. A solicitor can explain how the court is likely to view your family's history.
Do I have to try mediation first?
Usually you must attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting before applying to court. There are exemptions, such as evidence of domestic abuse, concerns about a child's safety, or genuine urgency. You do not have to continue with mediation if it is not suitable. The mediator will assess whether mediation is suitable for your family.
What is Cafcass?
Cafcass is the service that advises the family courts in England about children's welfare. In Wales the equivalent is Cafcass Cymru. They carry out safeguarding checks, speak to parents and sometimes children, and may write reports for the court. They are independent of both parents. Their role is to focus on the child's welfare.
What is a section 7 report?
It is a report the court can ask for about a child's welfare. It is usually prepared by a Cafcass officer or a local council social worker. It may include interviews with parents and the child and recommendations about arrangements. The court considers it but makes its own decision. Reports can take some weeks.
Will my child have to go to court?
Children very rarely attend court in these cases. Their views are usually gathered by a Cafcass officer or social worker and passed to the court. In some cases an older child may be given the chance to write to or meet the judge, but this is not common. The aim is to keep children out of the dispute.
How long does a court case take?
It varies a great deal. Some cases settle at the first hearing. Others take many months, particularly where reports or a fact finding hearing are needed. Court workloads also differ between areas. A solicitor can give you a better idea once they know your situation. Urgent matters can be dealt with faster.
What happens if the other parent ignores a court order?
You can apply to the court to enforce a child arrangements order. The court can make a range of orders, including changing the arrangements, ordering unpaid work or requiring compensation for financial loss. The court will look at whether there was a reasonable excuse for not following the order. Keep a record of missed contact.
What is a prohibited steps order?
It is a court order that stops a parent, or someone else, from taking a particular step without the court's permission. Common examples include stopping a child being taken out of the country or moved to a different school. In urgent cases it can be made quickly. A solicitor can explain when it might be needed.
Can we turn our agreement into a court order?
Yes, in some cases. If parents reach agreement during court proceedings, the court can make a consent order recording it, provided it is satisfied the arrangements are in the child's interests. Outside proceedings, many parents simply record their agreement in a parenting plan, as the court only makes an order where that is better for the child.
At what age can my child decide where they live?
There is no fixed age in England and Wales. The court considers a child's wishes and feelings in light of their age and understanding. The views of a teenager are usually given more weight than those of a young child, but they are always one factor among several. The court always looks at the whole picture.
How are my child's views heard?
In court cases, a Cafcass officer or social worker usually speaks to the child in a way suited to their age, and reports their views to the court. Outside court, child inclusive mediation can give children a chance to be heard by a trained mediator without having to choose between parents.
My child does not want to see the other parent. What happens?
The reasons matter. The court tries to understand why a child feels this way and whether there are any safety concerns. Children's wishes are taken seriously but are not decisive on their own. A solicitor can explain how this is usually handled. Sometimes arrangements are rebuilt gradually, for example through shorter visits or indirect contact at first. A Cafcass officer may speak to the child.
How can we protect our children from the conflict?
Many parents find it helps to keep adult discussions away from the children, avoid asking children to pass messages, and make handovers calm and predictable. Parenting courses for separated parents can help. Your solicitor can point you to local support services. Children usually cope better with separation when they feel safe, are kept informed in a way suited to their age, and are not made to feel responsible for the adults' decisions.
Should I talk to my child about the court case?
Many parents find it helps to reassure children that they are loved and that the adults are sorting things out, without sharing details of the dispute or asking them to choose. Cafcass and other organisations publish guidance for parents on talking to children about separation. Your solicitor can point you to it.
Can I take my child on holiday abroad?
If a child arrangements order says the child lives with you, you can usually take them abroad for up to one month without the other parent's consent, unless the order says otherwise. Otherwise you generally need the written consent of everyone with parental responsibility, or the court's permission. Taking a child abroad without the right consent can be a criminal offence.
What if I think my child will be taken abroad without my consent?
This can be urgent. A solicitor can explain the orders the court can make to prevent removal, which can be obtained very quickly. If you believe a child is about to be taken out of the country, contact the police immediately. Keep copies of your child's passport details, recent photographs and the other parent's contact details abroad, as these can help the police and the court act quickly.
Can I move to another part of the UK with my children?
Moving within the UK does not always need the other parent's consent, but a significant move that affects their time with the children may lead them to apply to court. The court will focus on the child's welfare. A solicitor can explain how this might apply to you. Discussing plans early often helps.
Can I move abroad permanently with my children?
A permanent move abroad usually needs the consent of everyone with parental responsibility or the court's permission. The court looks closely at the reasons for the move, the plans for the child and the effect on their relationship with the other parent. These are complex cases where advice is important.
Can the other parent take our child abroad without asking me?
Usually not, unless they have a child arrangements order saying the child lives with them, in which case trips of up to one month are generally allowed. If you are worried about a planned trip, a solicitor can explain the options, including asking the court to set conditions. Holiday dates are often agreed in advance.
How much does a children case cost?
It depends on whether agreement is reached and how far a court case goes. Many firms charge hourly or in stages, and some offer fixed fees for particular steps. There is also a court fee for most applications. Ask for a written estimate. Leaflegal is free to use, and the firm pays us for the introduction.
Is legal aid available for children cases?
Legal aid can be available where there is evidence of domestic abuse or child abuse, depending on income and savings. GOV.UK has a tool to check. Leaflegal introduces people who are looking for paid help from private family law firms. If you think you may qualify, you can check before deciding how to proceed. Some firms can tell you whether they offer legal aid as well as private help.
Can I represent myself in court?
Yes. Many parents represent themselves in children cases. Some instruct a solicitor for advice at key points, such as before the first hearing, rather than for the whole case. Ask the firm whether they offer this kind of limited help. The court will explain the process, and there are guides on GOV.UK and from Cafcass to help parents who represent themselves.
What protection is there if I have experienced domestic abuse?
The court must consider any allegations of domestic abuse carefully. There are rules to stop an alleged abuser questioning the other person directly in court, and measures such as separate waiting areas or screens may be available. If you are in danger, call 999 or contact a domestic abuse support service.
What will the solicitor ask me?
Expect questions about your children's ages, the current arrangements, what you would like to happen, any previous court orders and any safety concerns. It helps to have a simple timeline of key events. Do not worry if you do not have every detail at the first conversation. Bring any letters or orders you have.
What if the other parent has a solicitor and I do not?
That is common in children cases. The court is used to parents representing themselves and the judge will explain the process. The other parent's solicitor cannot advise you. Some people instruct a solicitor for advice on key steps only, which can help keep costs down. You can also bring a supporter to some hearings.
What is a separation agreement?
It is a written agreement between a married couple or civil partners who have separated. It sets out how the home, money, debts and arrangements for children will be dealt with while they live apart. It is a private contract rather than a court order, so it works differently from the orders made in a divorce or dissolution.
Is a separation agreement legally binding?
It is a contract, but a court is not automatically bound by it if one of you later applies for a financial order. The courts have said a properly made agreement can carry significant weight. That is more likely where both people gave full disclosure, took independent advice and signed freely, and where the terms are fair. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Do we have to be separated for a set time first?
No. There is no minimum period of separation before you can make an agreement. Many couples make one soon after they decide to live apart, so that everyone knows where they stand from the start. Some couples also make an agreement before one of them moves out, to plan how the move will happen. A solicitor can explain what to think about at each stage.
Can we be separated while living under the same roof?
Yes, some couples separate but stay in the same home for practical or financial reasons. An agreement can still record the arrangements, including who uses which rooms and who pays which bills. Your solicitor can talk through how this would work in practice. If you later divorce, the date you separated may matter for some purposes.
Is it the same as a divorce?
No. You remain married or in a civil partnership. A separation agreement simply records the arrangements while you live apart. You can still divorce or dissolve your civil partnership later if you choose to. Staying married keeps some legal ties in place, such as rights under a will or pension, which is why many people take advice on what that means for them.
Does it make the divorce happen automatically later?
No. A separation agreement does not start or end any legal process. If you decide to divorce or dissolve your civil partnership, that is a separate application. Since April 2022 the process is no fault, so a period of separation is not needed to apply. Your agreement can still be useful at that point.
Can we write our own separation agreement?
You can, and templates are available. The risk is that a homemade agreement may leave out important points, use unclear wording or lack the safeguards a court looks for. Many couples who start with their own draft ask solicitors to check it and put it into proper form. That can be a cost effective middle ground.
Can civil partners make a separation agreement?
Yes. Civil partners can make a separation agreement in the same way as married couples. It records how the home, money, debts and children will be dealt with while you live apart, and the same safeguards apply. A solicitor can explain how it would work for you. It can later form the basis of a consent order on dissolution.
