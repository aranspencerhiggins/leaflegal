About consent orders

If you and your former partner have agreed how to divide your finances, a consent order turns that agreement into a court order. It is drafted by a solicitor, signed by both of you and approved by a judge.

Without one, financial claims can remain open, sometimes for years after the divorce. A solicitor will make sure the order says what you agreed and that it will be approved.

A good family solicitor listens first, explains your options calmly and helps you find the least stressful way forward, which often means agreeing things without going to court. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they can help and what it is likely to cost.

Consent orders explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A consent order is a court order that records a financial agreement between spouses or civil partners who are divorcing or ending their partnership. Both people agree its terms, both sign it, and a judge approves it. Once approved it is legally binding and can be enforced in the same way as an order made after a contested hearing.

Consent orders are the most common way financial matters are finalised in England and Wales. Most couples who agree how to share their money and property, whether between themselves, through mediation or through solicitors, finish the process by asking the court to approve a consent order.

The order does two important jobs. It sets out who will receive what, such as the transfer of the home, a lump sum, a pension share or maintenance. And, where appropriate, it brings financial claims between the couple to an end, so that neither can come back for more in the future.

Why an agreement on its own is not enough

An informal agreement, even one written down and signed, does not stop either person applying to the court for a financial order later. This can happen many years after the divorce, for example if one person's circumstances change or the other comes into money. A consent order with clean break terms is the usual way to prevent that.

Who usually asks for help

  • Couples who have agreed a settlement and need it put into legal form
  • People who reached agreement in mediation and have a summary from the mediator
  • People whose former partner's solicitor has sent them a draft order to sign
  • People with pensions that need to be shared
  • People who divorced in the past without ever getting a financial order

Consent orders are only available to people who are married or in a civil partnership and have started divorce or dissolution proceedings. Unmarried couples use different documents.

Before you agree the terms

A consent order is only as good as the agreement behind it. Before an order is drafted, most solicitors want to be sure that both people have shared full information about their finances and understand what they are giving up. That includes pensions, which are easy to overlook, and any assets held in one person's sole name. A solicitor will not tell you whether to accept a deal, but they can explain how it compares with the range of outcomes a court might consider fair, so that you can make your own decision with the full picture.

The process for getting a consent order approved is mostly done on paper, without anyone needing to attend court. The steps below show how it usually works. Your solicitor will explain exactly what applies in your case.

1. Reaching agreement

The terms need to be agreed first. This usually happens after both people have shared full information about their finances, so that each understands what the other has. Agreements reached without that information are more likely to be questioned by the judge or challenged later.

2. Drafting the order

A solicitor drafts the consent order. It has to use language the court recognises, and it must be precise. Vague wording can make an order difficult to carry out or enforce. The order usually includes background statements, promises made by each person, the orders themselves and clean break terms where appropriate.

3. The statement of information

Alongside the order, the court needs a short summary of each person's finances and circumstances, known as the statement of information form. This helps the judge understand whether the agreement is broadly fair without seeing every document.

4. Pension paperwork

If the order includes a pension share or pension attachment, separate forms set out the details for each pension. The pension provider usually needs to be notified, and some providers have their own requirements.

5. Signing and submitting

Both people sign the order, or their solicitors sign on their behalf. It is then sent to the court with the court fee. Solicitors usually submit consent orders online. A consent order can only be approved after the conditional order in the divorce has been made.

6. The judge's review

A judge reads the papers to check that the agreement is within a broad range of fair outcomes. If satisfied, they approve the order and it is sealed by the court. If the judge has concerns, they may ask questions, suggest changes or list a short hearing.

7. Putting the order into effect

Once approved, the terms need to be carried out, such as selling or transferring property or paying a lump sum. Some terms, including pension sharing, only take effect after the final order in the divorce has been made.

If your former partner will not sign

A consent order needs both people's agreement. If one person changes their mind or stops responding before signing, the order cannot be submitted as a consent order. Options then include further negotiation, mediation or, if needed, an application to court for a financial order. A solicitor can explain how to keep things moving.

Every consent order is different because every family's finances are different. Most orders are made up of a few standard parts. Knowing what they are can help you understand a draft you have been sent.

Recitals

These are background statements at the start of the order. They record things like the names of the parties, the date of the marriage, and any facts the order relies on. They can also record arrangements that the court cannot order directly but that both people have agreed.

Undertakings

An undertaking is a formal promise to the court, such as a promise to use best efforts to remove the other person from a mortgage, or to pay certain bills. Breaking an undertaking can have serious consequences, so it is important to understand any you are asked to give.

The orders

This is the main part of the document. It may include orders about the following.

  • Transferring the family home or other property from one person to the other
  • Selling a property and dividing the proceeds in set shares
  • Paying a lump sum, sometimes in instalments
  • Sharing or attaching a pension
  • Paying spousal maintenance for a set period
  • Child maintenance, where both parents agree to include it

Clean break terms

Where a clean break is agreed, the order dismisses each person's financial claims against the other, including claims against each other's estate after death. This is what gives both people certainty for the future. A clean break is not always appropriate, for example where ongoing maintenance is needed.

Practical details

Good orders also deal with the practical details, such as who pays the mortgage until a sale, what happens if a property does not sell, who chooses the estate agent and how the costs of sale are shared. Missing details are a common source of later disputes.

Many people assume that if they both agree, the court will simply approve their consent order. In most cases it will, but the judge does have a duty to check it, and some orders are sent back or questioned.

What the judge looks at

The judge is not looking for the outcome they would have chosen. They are checking that the agreement falls within a broad range of fair outcomes, taking into account the factors the court would normally consider. These include each person's needs, resources and contributions, and the welfare of any children. The statement of information form is the main evidence the judge uses.

Why an order might be queried

  • The statement of information is incomplete or the figures do not add up
  • One person appears to be left without a home or enough to meet their needs
  • The order departs a long way from an equal division without explanation
  • A clean break is proposed where one person seems to need ongoing support
  • The wording is unclear or contains orders the court cannot make

A short explanation of why the agreement makes sense for your family can often resolve a judge's concerns. Your solicitor will usually prepare the order with this in mind.

If the judge does not approve it

If the judge has concerns, they may ask for more information, suggest amendments or ask both people to attend a short hearing. This does not mean the agreement has failed. Most issues can be dealt with by explaining the background more fully or adjusting the wording.

Timing

How long approval takes depends on the court's workload, and can change. Your solicitor can tell you current timescales. Remember that the conditional order must be made before the consent order can be approved.

Getting it right first time

Orders that are returned by the court cause delay and sometimes extra cost. The most common reasons are simple, such as missing signatures, figures in the statement of information that do not match the order, or pension forms that have not been completed correctly. Careful preparation, and a short explanation for any unusual terms, usually avoids these problems.

Consent orders are one of the more predictable areas of family law work, and many firms offer them for a fixed fee. The cost depends mainly on how complex the agreement is and whether pensions are involved.

Solicitor fees

A fixed fee usually covers drafting the order, preparing the statement of information, submitting it to the court and dealing with routine queries. Pension sharing forms, negotiating changes to the terms, reviewing a draft prepared by the other side, or responding to concerns raised by the judge may be charged on top. Ask the firm exactly what their fee includes.

The court fee

The court charges a fee for considering a consent order. The amount changes from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask your solicitor. If you have a low income, you may be able to get help with court fees.

Other possible costs

  • Pension provider charges for implementing a pension share
  • Fees for a specialist pension report, if one is needed
  • Conveyancing costs for transferring or selling property
  • Lender fees for changes to a mortgage

Who pays

Couples often agree who will pay the costs of the consent order, and this can be included in the agreement. Otherwise, each person usually pays their own solicitor.

A consent order that is drafted carefully and based on full information rarely causes trouble. Most problems come from rushing, missing details or not taking advice.

Signing something you do not understand

If your former partner's solicitor sends you a draft to sign, remember that solicitor acts only for them. Taking independent advice before you sign helps you understand what you are agreeing to and what claims you may be giving up.

Pensions left out

Some people agree to divide the home and savings without considering pensions, which can be the largest asset of all. Once a clean break order is approved, it is usually too late to go back.

Unclear wording

Phrases that seem clear at the time can be read differently later. Orders that do not say exactly when a property must be sold, at what price or who pays which costs can lead to further disputes and cost.

The mortgage

A consent order cannot force a lender to release someone from a mortgage. Orders usually deal with this through undertakings and deadlines, but it is important to check early whether the person keeping the home can get a mortgage in their own name.

Changing your mind later

Approved consent orders about capital and property are generally final. They can only be set aside in limited circumstances, such as serious non disclosure, fraud or a major unforeseeable event soon after the order. This is why getting the agreement right before it is approved matters so much.

Feeling pressured to agree

No one should feel forced into a financial agreement. If you are being pressured or threatened, or you are worried about your safety, contact the police on 999 in an emergency or a domestic abuse support service, and tell your solicitor.

Consent orders questions and answers

When can a consent order be made?
Once the conditional order in your divorce has been made. Your solicitor will advise on timing.
Will a judge always approve it?
Not always. A judge checks that it is broadly fair. A well drafted order with full information is more likely to be approved.
Can one solicitor act for both of us?
Usually not. One solicitor drafts the order, and the other person may take separate advice.
Which areas do you cover?
Family law enquiries in England and Wales.
I’m worried about my safety. Can you help?
If you are in danger, call 999. For confidential support, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free on 0808 2000 247, 24 hours a day.
Will my partner find out I made an enquiry?
No. We only share your details with the firm we introduce you to, and they contact you, not anyone else.
What is a consent order?
It is a court order that sets out a financial agreement between divorcing spouses or separating civil partners. Both people agree the terms and a judge approves them. Once approved it is legally binding and can be enforced like any other court order. It is the usual way couples make a financial agreement final, whether they reached it themselves, in mediation or through solicitors.
When can a consent order be made?
The court can only approve a consent order once the conditional order in the divorce or dissolution has been made. Couples often start preparing the order earlier so that it is ready to submit at that point. Your solicitor will explain the best timing for you. Pension sharing terms only take effect after the final order.
Do we need a consent order if we have hardly any assets?
It is not compulsory, but many couples with modest finances still choose one with clean break terms. Without it, either person could make a claim in the future if their circumstances change, for example after an inheritance or a lottery win. A solicitor can explain whether it is worth considering.
Can we get a consent order years after our divorce?
Yes. A consent order can be made at any time after the conditional order, even many years after the final order, as long as neither of you has remarried and the claims have not already been dealt with. A solicitor can check whether this is possible in your case. Delay can also make things harder.
Is a consent order the same as a separation agreement?
No. A separation agreement is a contract between a couple who have separated, often before divorce. It is not automatically binding in the same way. A consent order is a court order made within divorce proceedings. A separation agreement is sometimes later turned into a consent order. Your solicitor can explain which suits your situation.
Can unmarried couples get a consent order?
Financial consent orders of this kind are for married couples and civil partners in divorce or dissolution proceedings. Unmarried couples who separate deal with property in other ways, such as a declaration of trust or a separation agreement. A solicitor can explain the options. Orders about children can be made whether or not the parents were married.
Do we have to go to court?
Usually not. Consent orders are normally dealt with on paper by a judge, without a hearing. A short hearing is only listed if the judge has questions that cannot be answered in writing. This keeps costs down and means most couples never see the inside of a courtroom. The judge reads the order and the statement of information, and approves it if satisfied it is broadly fair.
Can a consent order deal with children's arrangements?
A financial consent order deals with money and property. Arrangements for where children live and spend time are separate. If parents want a court order about children, that is made in different proceedings, and the court only makes one if it is better for the child than no order. Your solicitor can explain how both fit together.
Can one solicitor act for both of us?
No. A solicitor can only act for one person, because your interests may be different. Often one solicitor drafts the order and the other person takes independent advice from a different firm before signing. This helps protect both people and makes the order less likely to be challenged later. The judge may also be more comfortable approving an order where both people have had the chance to take independent advice.
My ex's solicitor has sent me a draft order. What should I do?
Read it carefully and consider taking independent advice before you sign. Their solicitor acts only for your former partner and cannot advise you. A solicitor can explain what the order means, check it reflects what you agreed and point out anything that might have been missed. Do not feel rushed into signing.
What is a clean break order?
It is a consent order, or part of one, that ends each person's financial claims against the other. It usually also prevents claims against each other's estate after death. A clean break gives certainty, but it is not suitable for everyone, for example where ongoing maintenance is needed. Your solicitor will explain whether it suits you.
Can child maintenance be included?
Yes, if both parents agree. Child maintenance included in a consent order can usually be replaced by a Child Maintenance Service calculation after a year if either parent applies to the service. A solicitor can explain how this works. Including it can give parents more certainty about what will be paid. Child maintenance is separate from time with the children, and one does not depend on the other.
What information is needed to draft the order?
The solicitor will need details of what you have agreed, a summary of both people's assets, debts, income and pensions, your divorce case number, and details of any property and pension providers. If you reached agreement in mediation, the mediator's summary is very helpful. Do not worry if you do not have everything at first. Your solicitor will tell you what is missing.
Can a mediation agreement become a consent order?
Yes. Mediators usually produce a written summary of the proposals reached, along with a summary of each person's finances. A solicitor can use this to draft a consent order and check whether anything important needs to be added or clarified. A mediator's summary is not binding on its own, so it is important that the consent order reflects it accurately. Each person may also want independent advice before signing.
What is an undertaking?
It is a formal promise to the court that is recorded in the order. Common examples include promising to try to remove the other person from the mortgage or to pay certain bills. Breaking an undertaking can have serious consequences, so make sure you understand any you agree to give.
What if I have already signed but now regret it?
If the order has not yet been approved, speak to a solicitor quickly, as it may still be possible to withdraw consent before the judge approves it. Once approved, it is much harder to change. A solicitor can explain where you stand based on how far the process has gone.
Will a judge always approve our order?
Not always. The judge checks that the agreement falls within a broad range of fair outcomes. Most well prepared orders are approved, but a judge may ask questions or suggest changes if something looks unbalanced or unclear. A short explanation of the background often resolves their concerns. Your solicitor will prepare it with approval in mind.
What is the statement of information form?
It is a short form that summarises each person's finances and circumstances, such as their income, assets, housing plans and whether they plan to remarry. The judge uses it to check whether the agreement is fair. It must be accurate and complete. Both people usually sign it, or it can be completed separately. Errors or gaps are a common reason for orders being returned.
How long does approval take?
It depends on the court's workload, which changes over time. Your solicitor can tell you the current timescale. The conditional order must also have been made before the consent order can be approved. Approval can be quicker where the paperwork is complete and the order is clearly drafted. Orders that are returned for corrections take longer, so it is worth getting the details right first time.
What happens if the judge asks for a hearing?
Both people, or their solicitors, are usually asked to attend a short hearing to answer the judge's questions. This is often a chance to explain why the agreement suits your family. It does not mean the agreement has failed. Hearings of this kind are usually short. Your solicitor can attend with you or on your behalf and help explain the agreement.
Can the judge change our agreement?
The judge cannot rewrite your agreement without your consent. They can refuse to approve it, ask for more information or suggest changes. If changes are needed, both people need to agree before the order is approved. In practice most judges simply ask for an explanation or a small change in wording. Your solicitor will discuss any request with you before anything is agreed.
Can the consent order be submitted before the conditional order?
The papers can be prepared and agreed in advance, and some couples submit them so that they are ready. The judge cannot approve the order until the conditional order has been made. Your solicitor will explain the timing that works best with your divorce. Preparing early can avoid delay. Ask the firm.
How is a pension shared in a consent order?
A pension sharing order transfers a percentage of one person's pension into a new pension for the other. A separate form sets out the details for each pension, and the pension provider must be told. The share takes effect after the final order and the pension provider then implements it.
Do pension providers charge to implement a share?
Many do. Charges vary between providers and schemes, and the order or agreement usually says who pays. Your solicitor can check the provider's charges and requirements before the order is submitted. The provider also needs certain information before it can implement the share, and it usually has a set period to do so once the order and final order are received. Delays can arise if paperwork is missing.
Does a consent order remove me from the mortgage?
Not by itself. The lender has to agree to release a borrower from a mortgage. Orders usually deal with this through undertakings and deadlines, and sometimes say what happens if the person keeping the home cannot get a mortgage in their own name. Check with the lender as early as possible.
Who deals with transferring the house?
A property transfer ordered in a consent order still has to be completed through a conveyancing process, including updating HM Land Registry. Your family solicitor may do this or refer you to a conveyancer. There may be extra costs and lender requirements. Stamp duty and other tax rules can also apply, and they change, so ask your solicitor or conveyancer.
What if the house does not sell?
Well drafted orders usually say what happens if a property does not sell within a set time, such as reducing the asking price or giving the court power to order a sale. If the order is silent, a further court application may be needed. This is one reason careful drafting matters.
Can a consent order be changed later?
Orders dealing with property and lump sums are generally final. Maintenance terms can usually be varied if circumstances change. In rare cases an order can be set aside, for example where there was serious non disclosure or fraud. Always take advice quickly if you think there is a problem. Delay can affect your options.
What if my ex does not do what the order says?
A consent order can be enforced through the court. Options might include orders to sell property, attach earnings or recover money owed. A solicitor can explain how to enforce the particular terms that have not been followed. Clear wording in the order makes enforcement easier. Keep a record of what has and has not been done.
Does a consent order affect my will?
A clean break consent order usually ends claims against each other's estate. It does not change your will. Many people review and update their will after separation and divorce, so that it reflects their wishes. You may also want to update pension nominations and life insurance beneficiaries, as these are not always changed automatically by divorce or a consent order.
How much does a consent order cost?
Many firms charge a fixed fee for drafting and submitting a consent order, with extra charges for pension forms or negotiation. There is also a court fee, which changes from time to time, so check GOV.UK. Leaflegal is free to use, and the firm pays us for the introduction. Ask what is included.
What if my circumstances change after the order?
Capital and property terms are usually final, so a change in circumstances does not normally reopen them. Maintenance can usually be varied if circumstances change significantly. In very rare cases, an unforeseen event soon after the order may allow it to be revisited. Take advice quickly if this happens. Time limits apply.
Do I need a court order to see my child?
No. Most parents agree arrangements without a court order, and the court only makes an order if it is better for the child than no order. An order may be helpful if agreement cannot be reached or an existing arrangement keeps breaking down. A solicitor can explain the options. Many parents find agreement easier with help.
Is there a standard arrangement, such as every other weekend?
There is no standard arrangement. Every family is different, and arrangements depend on the children's ages, needs, schooling, the distance between homes and each parent's work. Many families share time in a range of ways. A solicitor can explain how arrangements are usually worked out. Arrangements often change as children grow.
What is a parenting plan?
It is a written record of what parents have agreed about the children, such as where they will live, school holidays, special occasions and how parents will communicate. It is not legally binding, but it can help avoid misunderstandings. Templates are available online, and solicitors and mediators can help prepare one.
Do unmarried fathers have rights to see their children?
An unmarried father can apply to the court for a child arrangements order whether or not he has parental responsibility. Whether he has parental responsibility depends mainly on whether he is named on the birth certificate for a birth registered after 1 December 2003. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Can grandparents apply to see their grandchildren?
Grandparents do not have an automatic right to see grandchildren, but they can ask the court for permission to apply for a child arrangements order. The court considers things like their connection with the child and whether the application could disrupt the child's life. Many grandparents use mediation first. Many disputes settle this way.
Does separation change parental responsibility?
No. Both parents keep parental responsibility after separation or divorce if they had it before. Important decisions, such as changing school or a major medical treatment, should usually be discussed with everyone who has parental responsibility. Parental responsibility usually lasts until a child turns 18. A solicitor can explain how decisions are usually shared between separated parents.
Can I change my child's surname?
If more than one person has parental responsibility, a child's surname is not usually changed without the agreement of all of them or the court's permission. The court looks at what is best for the child. A solicitor can explain how this works. A child's own views may also be relevant, depending on their age and understanding.
Does it matter who the children lived with before we separated?
The court looks at the child's welfare now and in the future, including the likely effect of any change. Who has been the main carer in the past is often relevant to that, but it does not decide the outcome on its own. A solicitor can explain how the court is likely to view your family's history.
