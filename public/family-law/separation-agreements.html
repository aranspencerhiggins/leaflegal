About separation agreements

Some couples separate without divorcing straight away. A separation agreement records how you will deal with the home, money and children in the meantime.

It isn’t automatically binding in the way a court order is, but a court is likely to take it into account if it was fairly made with full information and advice.

A good family solicitor listens first, explains your options calmly and helps you find the least stressful way forward, which often means agreeing things without going to court. When we introduce you, the firm will explain how they can help and what it is likely to cost.

Separation agreement in brief

A separation agreement records what you have agreed about money, property and children when you separate but are not ready to divorce. It is not the same as a legal separation order, but courts often give weight to a fair agreement made with advice.

A family solicitor can draft the separation agreement so it can later form the basis of a consent order.

Separation agreements explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A separation agreement is a written document that sets out how a married couple or civil partners will organise their lives once they are living apart. It usually covers who stays in the home, who pays which bills, how savings and debts are handled, and how the children will spend their time. It is a practical record of what you have both agreed while you are separated.

People choose this route for many reasons. Some are not ready to make a final decision about the marriage. Some want time to see whether living apart works before taking the next step. Others have religious or family reasons for not divorcing, or simply want to put a calm structure in place while emotions settle. Whatever the reason, a clear agreement can take a lot of uncertainty out of a very unsettled time.

A separation agreement is not the same as a divorce or dissolution. You remain legally married or in a civil partnership. That matters for things like inheritance, pensions and tax, and a solicitor can explain how staying married affects your own position.

It is also different from a court order. In England and Wales a separation agreement is a private contract between the two of you. A court is not automatically bound by it if one of you later asks the court to decide your finances. But the courts have made clear that a properly made agreement can carry real weight, especially where both people gave full financial information, took independent legal advice and were not under pressure when they signed.

Some couples are surprised by how much a separation agreement can achieve. It cannot end the marriage or divide a pension, but it can bring order to everyday life. Knowing who pays the mortgage this month, which parent has the children at half term and what happens to the joint account can make a real difference to how everyone copes in the months after a separation.

Who it can help

  • Couples who have separated but are not ready to divorce
  • People who want to test living apart before deciding
  • Couples who do not wish to divorce for religious or personal reasons
  • Parents who want a clear plan for the children straight away
  • Anyone who wants certainty about the home and bills while things are worked out
  • Couples who intend to divorce later and want the groundwork done now

Every separation agreement is tailored to the couple making it. Your solicitor will talk you through what is sensible to include. These are the areas most agreements deal with.

The family home

The agreement can say who will live in the home, who pays the mortgage or rent, and who covers repairs and insurance. It can also say what will happen to the home in the longer term, for example whether it will be sold, transferred to one of you, or kept until the children are older. If the home is rented, the agreement can deal with who stays on the tenancy, though the landlord's position will also matter.

Money and maintenance

Many agreements set out whether one person will pay the other a regular amount to help with living costs, and for how long. They can also deal with joint bank accounts, savings, investments and who keeps which car or belongings.

Debts

Joint debts such as loans, overdrafts and credit cards are a common source of worry. The agreement can record who will repay which debt. It is worth knowing that an agreement between the two of you does not change what you owe a lender. If a loan is in joint names, the lender can usually still pursue either of you.

Children

Arrangements for children often sit at the heart of an agreement. It can record where the children will live, how they will spend time with each parent, how holidays and special occasions will be shared and how child maintenance will be paid. The children's welfare always comes first, and arrangements can be revisited as they grow.

Pensions

Pensions can be one of the largest assets in a marriage. A separation agreement can record what you intend to do about them, but a pension cannot actually be divided until there is a court order made as part of a divorce or dissolution. Your solicitor can explain how pensions fit into your plans.

Future plans

Many couples include a clause about what will happen if either of them later starts a divorce or dissolution, such as agreeing to ask the court to turn the agreement into a consent order. Some include a date to review the agreement or events that would trigger a review.

You do not have to cover everything at once. Some couples agree the immediate arrangements now and leave bigger decisions, such as the long term future of the home, for a later date. The agreement can say this clearly so that nobody is left guessing about what has and has not been decided.

There is no single path, but most separation agreements are put together in a similar way. Your solicitor will explain the steps that apply to you.

1. A first conversation

You will talk to a family solicitor about your situation, what you and your spouse or partner have discussed and what matters most to you. This is a chance to ask questions and get a sense of how the process and costs would work.

2. Sharing financial information

Both of you are usually asked to give full and honest details of your income, property, savings, pensions and debts, with supporting documents. This is called financial disclosure. It is one of the most important parts of the process, because an agreement made without a clear picture of the finances is much easier to challenge later.

3. Reaching agreement

Some couples have already agreed most things and simply need them written down properly. Others need help to reach agreement. This can happen through solicitors writing to each other, round table meetings or family mediation. Your solicitor can explain which approach might suit you.

4. Drafting the agreement

Once the terms are agreed, one solicitor usually prepares a draft. The other person's solicitor reviews it and suggests changes. The wording matters, because vague terms can cause arguments later.

5. Independent advice and signing

Each of you should ideally have your own solicitor, so that both of you understand what you are signing and the effect it has. The agreement is then signed, often as a deed. Your solicitor will explain how it should be signed and witnessed.

6. Living with the agreement

After signing, you follow the arrangements you agreed. If circumstances change, the agreement can be reviewed. If you later decide to divorce or dissolve your civil partnership, your solicitor can help turn it into a consent order.

The time this takes varies. Where most terms are already agreed and both of you provide documents promptly, the process can be fairly quick. Where there is a lot to work out, or one person is slow to share information, it naturally takes longer. Your solicitor will keep you updated on what is happening and what is needed from you.

A separation agreement is only as useful as it is reliable. If one of you later asks a court to decide your finances, the court will look at how the agreement was made and whether it is fair to hold you both to it. Nobody can guarantee that an agreement will be upheld, but some features make it much more likely to be given weight.

  • Both of you gave full and frank financial disclosure
  • Both of you had the chance to take independent legal advice
  • Neither of you was pressured, rushed or misled into signing
  • The terms are reasonably fair to both of you
  • The children's needs are properly met
  • There has been no major unforeseen change since you signed

An agreement can lose weight if circumstances change a lot. A serious illness, a new baby, a large inheritance or the loss of a job might all make the original terms look unfair. Building in a review date can help you deal with change in a planned way.

It also helps if the agreement is clearly written and covers the things that matter. Gaps tend to be filled by argument. A well drafted agreement says what happens to each significant asset and debt, deals with the children's arrangements and explains what will happen if the plan does not work out. Your solicitor will think about the questions that tend to come up later so that you are not caught out.

Timing can matter too. An agreement made in a rush, perhaps the week after one person moved out, may later be said to have been signed under emotional pressure. Taking a little time to gather information and reflect can make the final document stronger, even when you both want things settled quickly.

Separation agreement or court order

A separation agreement suits couples who are not divorcing yet. If you are already divorcing or dissolving a civil partnership, the usual way to make a financial agreement binding is a consent order approved by the court. Your solicitor can explain the difference between the two and which one fits your timing.

Judicial separation

There is also a court process called judicial separation for married couples, or a separation order for civil partners. It allows the court to make certain financial orders without ending the marriage. It is used less often, usually where there are religious or other reasons for not divorcing. A solicitor can explain whether it is relevant to you.

The cost of a separation agreement depends mostly on how much you already agree and how complicated your finances are. A simple agreement between two people who have worked out the main points will usually cost much less than one that needs lots of negotiation.

How firms usually charge

Some firms offer a fixed fee for drafting a separation agreement where the terms are already agreed. Others charge by the hour, particularly if there is negotiation, mediation or complex property involved. Reviewing an agreement drafted by the other person's solicitor is often charged separately.

Things that affect the cost

  • Whether you have already agreed the main terms
  • The number of properties, businesses or investments involved
  • Whether pensions need valuing
  • How much negotiation is needed
  • Whether mediation is used
  • How quickly you both provide financial documents

It can help to think about costs in proportion to what is at stake. Spending a lot on negotiating over a small difference may not make sense, while getting proper advice on a large asset such as the family home or a pension usually does. A solicitor can help you think this through, though the decisions are always yours.

Ask the firm to explain its fees clearly at the start, what is included and what might cost extra. A good firm will confirm this in writing before any work begins, and will tell you if the estimate is likely to change.

Separating is hard, even when it is amicable. You do not need to have everything worked out before you speak to a solicitor. Having a few things to hand will help the first conversation go smoothly.

  • The date you married or registered your civil partnership
  • The date you separated, or plan to
  • A rough list of property, savings, pensions and debts
  • Recent payslips or accounts if you are self employed
  • Mortgage or tenancy details for the home
  • Your children's names and ages
  • Anything you and your spouse or partner have already agreed

It also helps to think about what matters most to you. For some people it is staying in the family home until the children finish school. For others it is a clean break from shared debts. Knowing your priorities makes it easier for your solicitor to explain how the agreement could reflect them.

Try to be as open as possible with the firm, even about things that feel awkward, such as debts your spouse or partner does not know about or worries about how you will manage financially. Solicitors deal with these issues all the time, and they can only explain your options properly if they have the full picture.

Separation agreements questions and answers

Is a separation agreement legally binding?
Not in the same way as a court order, but it can carry significant weight if made properly.
Do we both need solicitors?
Taking separate advice makes the agreement more likely to be upheld.
Can we divorce later?
Yes. The agreement can form the basis of a consent order when you do.
Which areas do you cover?

I’m worried about my safety. Can you help?

Will my partner find out I made an enquiry?
No. We only share your details with the firm we introduce you to, and they contact you, not anyone else.
What is a separation agreement?
It is a written agreement between a married couple or civil partners who have separated. It sets out how the home, money, debts and arrangements for children will be dealt with while they live apart. It is a private contract rather than a court order, so it works differently from the orders made in a divorce or dissolution.
Is a separation agreement legally binding?
It is a contract, but a court is not automatically bound by it if one of you later applies for a financial order. The courts have said a properly made agreement can carry significant weight. That is more likely where both people gave full disclosure, took independent advice and signed freely, and where the terms are fair. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Do we have to be separated for a set time first?
No. There is no minimum period of separation before you can make an agreement. Many couples make one soon after they decide to live apart, so that everyone knows where they stand from the start. Some couples also make an agreement before one of them moves out, to plan how the move will happen. A solicitor can explain what to think about at each stage.
Can we be separated while living under the same roof?
Yes, some couples separate but stay in the same home for practical or financial reasons. An agreement can still record the arrangements, including who uses which rooms and who pays which bills. Your solicitor can talk through how this would work in practice. If you later divorce, the date you separated may matter for some purposes.
Is it the same as a divorce?
No. You remain married or in a civil partnership. A separation agreement simply records the arrangements while you live apart. You can still divorce or dissolve your civil partnership later if you choose to. Staying married keeps some legal ties in place, such as rights under a will or pension, which is why many people take advice on what that means for them.
Does it make the divorce happen automatically later?
No. A separation agreement does not start or end any legal process. If you decide to divorce or dissolve your civil partnership, that is a separate application. Since April 2022 the process is no fault, so a period of separation is not needed to apply. Your agreement can still be useful at that point.
Can we write our own separation agreement?
You can, and templates are available. The risk is that a homemade agreement may leave out important points, use unclear wording or lack the safeguards a court looks for. Many couples who start with their own draft ask solicitors to check it and put it into proper form. That can be a cost effective middle ground.
Can civil partners make a separation agreement?
Yes. Civil partners can make a separation agreement in the same way as married couples. It records how the home, money, debts and children will be dealt with while you live apart, and the same safeguards apply. A solicitor can explain how it would work for you. It can later form the basis of a consent order on dissolution.
Who stays in the family home?
That is for the two of you to agree, and the agreement records it. Common arrangements include one person staying with the children, the home being sold, or one person buying the other out. A solicitor can explain how each option might work, but the decision rests on your own circumstances.
Can the agreement cover the mortgage?
Yes. It can say who will pay the mortgage and other costs of the home. It is important to know that your lender is not bound by your agreement. If the mortgage is in joint names, the lender can usually still ask either of you to pay. Speak to your lender if you are worried about keeping up payments.
Can it include maintenance for my spouse?
Yes. Many agreements include regular payments from one person to the other, often for a set period or until a review date. The agreement can say how much, how often and what happens if circumstances change. Your solicitor can explain the factors usually considered. Some agreements also say what will happen to maintenance if the paying person loses their job or the receiving person starts working more.
What about child maintenance?
The agreement can record how much child maintenance will be paid. Even with an agreement in place, either parent can still apply to the Child Maintenance Service. Many parents use the government's online child maintenance calculator as a starting point, and your solicitor can explain how this fits together. It is good to know this from the start.
Can we divide our pensions in a separation agreement?
You can record what you intend to happen, but a pension cannot actually be shared without a court order. That order is made as part of a divorce or dissolution. Your solicitor can explain how to protect your position until then. Some couples ask for pension valuations at this stage so that everyone has a realistic picture.
What happens to our joint debts?
The agreement can say who repays each debt. It does not change your contract with the lender, so if a debt is in joint names, either of you can usually still be pursued. Some couples choose to close or refinance joint debts as part of the arrangement. Your solicitor can explain the practical options.
Are there tax issues to think about?
There can be. Transferring property, shares or other assets between spouses can have tax consequences, and the rules for separating couples have changed over time. Your solicitor may suggest speaking to an accountant or tax adviser before you finalise the agreement. Timing can sometimes affect the tax position, so it is worth raising early.
What if we do not know the value of everything?
That is common. Your solicitor can explain how values are usually worked out, for example through estate agent appraisals for a home or valuations from pension providers. Agreeing values is part of the process, and having reliable figures makes the agreement stronger. Estimates are often fine as a starting point.
Can one of us buy the other out of the home?
Yes, that is a common arrangement. The agreement can set out the price or how it will be worked out, when the buyout will happen and how the mortgage will be dealt with. A lender usually needs to agree before one person takes on a joint mortgage alone, so timing and affordability matter.
Can a separation agreement deal with the children?
Yes. It can record where the children will live, how they will spend time with each parent, holidays, schooling and other practical matters. Any arrangement should put the children's welfare first and can be reviewed as they grow. Many parents find that writing the arrangements down reduces arguments, because everyone can see what was agreed. It also helps the children know what to expect.
Is the part about children binding?
Arrangements for children can always be looked at again if circumstances change, and a court will make decisions based on the child's welfare at the time. That is why many parents treat this part of the agreement as a working plan they can adjust together. Writing it down still helps everyone know what to expect.
What if we cannot agree about the children?
Many parents find family mediation helpful. If agreement still is not possible, a parent can apply to court for a child arrangements order, usually after attending a mediation information meeting. A solicitor can explain the options and what each involves. Many disputes are settled before a final hearing. Your solicitor can talk you through each step.
Should the children be told about the agreement?
That depends on their ages and your family. Many parents explain the practical arrangements to their children in simple terms without going into the legal detail. Family professionals and mediators can offer guidance on how to talk to children about separation. Keeping children out of adult disagreements is widely seen as important for their wellbeing.
Can we include arrangements for moving away?
Yes, you can agree how decisions about moving home, changing schools or travelling abroad will be made. If one parent later wants to move a long distance with the children, the other parent's agreement or a court order may be needed. A solicitor can explain how this works. Planning ahead can avoid disputes.
Can grandparents be included in the arrangements?
The agreement is between the two of you, but you can record how you will support the children's relationships with grandparents and wider family. Many parents find this reassures relatives at a difficult time. If a dispute about contact with grandparents arises later, a solicitor can explain the options. It is a common concern.
Can the agreement be changed?
Yes, if you both agree. Many agreements include a review date or list events that will lead to a review, such as one of you moving in with a new partner. Changes should be made in writing and, ideally, with advice so the new terms are clear. Your solicitor can help with this.
What happens if one of us breaks the agreement?
Because it is a contract, the other person may be able to take steps to enforce it. Often the more practical route is to raise the issue through solicitors or mediation first. A solicitor can explain what options are available in your situation. Keeping records of payments and arrangements can help if a problem arises.
Can it be turned into a consent order if we divorce?
Yes. Many couples do this. Once a divorce or dissolution reaches the conditional order stage, the agreed terms can be put into a consent order and sent to the court for approval. A judge will check that the terms are broadly fair before approving it. Once approved, the order is binding and enforceable.
Do I need to update my will?
Separation alone does not cancel a will or change who would inherit if you died without one. Your spouse or civil partner may still benefit. Many people review their will, pension nominations and life insurance when they separate. Your solicitor can explain what to think about. It is often overlooked.
What if my spouse or partner meets someone new?
That does not automatically change the agreement, unless it says so. Some agreements include clauses about cohabitation or remarriage, especially for maintenance. If nothing is said, a change like this may be a reason to review the terms together. A solicitor can explain how the agreement deals with this, or how it could.
How long does a separation agreement last?
It lasts for as long as its terms say, or until it is replaced by a court order or a new agreement. Some agreements are meant to cover a short period while a couple decides what to do. Others cover several years. Including a review date can help keep it up to date.
Do we both need our own solicitor?
It is usually recommended. One solicitor cannot normally act for both of you because your interests may differ. Each of you having independent advice also makes the agreement more likely to be given weight later. Your spouse or partner is free to choose any firm they like, and the two firms will usually deal with each other directly.
Can we use mediation instead?
Mediation can help you reach agreement, and many couples use it alongside solicitors. The mediator does not advise either of you individually. Once you agree in mediation, a solicitor can turn the outcome into a formal separation agreement. Many people find mediation less stressful and less costly than negotiating entirely through solicitors.
How long does it take?
If you have already agreed the main points and provide your documents promptly, an agreement can often be finalised in a matter of weeks. Where there is negotiation or complex property, it can take longer. Your solicitor can give you a realistic estimate. Delays most often come from waiting for documents, so gathering them early helps.

What if my spouse or partner will not engage?
An agreement needs both of you to take part. If your spouse or partner will not engage, a solicitor can explain other routes, which may include mediation or, in some cases, applying to court. Every situation is different, so it helps to talk it through. Your solicitor can explain the realistic options.
Do cohabiting couples have the same rights as married couples?
No. In England and Wales, living together does not give the same financial rights as marriage or civil partnership, however long you have been together. The idea of a common law spouse is a myth. Your rights depend mainly on property and trust law and on any agreements you made.
I have lived with my partner for years. Does that give me a claim?
The length of the relationship alone does not create a claim. What matters is how the property is owned, what each of you contributed and what was agreed or intended about ownership. A long relationship may mean there is more evidence of shared intentions, but each case depends on its facts.
Can I claim a share of a house in my partner's sole name?
Possibly. You may have a claim if there was a shared understanding that you would have an interest and you relied on it to your disadvantage. Paying towards the mortgage, the deposit or major improvements can be relevant. A solicitor will look at the evidence and explain your position. Evidence is often key.
We own our home jointly. Do we each get half?
Not always. It depends on whether you are joint tenants or tenants in common, whether there is a declaration of trust and what you intended. Joint owners are often presumed to share equally unless there is good reason to think otherwise, but that presumption can sometimes be displaced. Your solicitor will look at the documents and the history.
What is a declaration of trust?
It is a document that sets out how a property is owned and how proceeds will be divided if it is sold. If you signed one when you bought your home, it is usually the starting point in any dispute. Your conveyancing solicitor may hold a copy if you are unsure.
Does paying household bills give me a share in the home?
Not on its own, as a rule. Paying for food and bills while your partner paid the mortgage can sometimes be relevant, but courts tend to give more weight to direct payments towards buying the property. The overall picture and what you both intended also matter. A solicitor can explain how your contributions might be viewed.
My partner promised I would always have a home. Does that count?
It might. If a clear promise was made and you relied on it in a significant way, such as giving up your own home or working unpaid in a family business, the law may sometimes give you a remedy. These claims depend heavily on evidence, so try to keep any messages or records.
Can I stay in the home while we sort things out?
That depends on whether you own or rent it and in whose name. A joint owner usually has a right to live there. A partner with no legal ownership may have more limited rights. A solicitor can explain your position, and you should tell them if you have any safety concerns.
