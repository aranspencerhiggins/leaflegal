About cohabitation disputes

Unmarried couples don’t have the same rights as married couples when they separate, however long they have been together. Disputes about a shared home are usually decided under property and trust law, which looks at ownership and what was agreed or intended.

Cohabitation disputes explained

Pick a topic to read more.

More and more couples in England and Wales live together without marrying or forming a civil partnership. Many assume that after a few years they have the same rights as a married couple. They do not. There is no such thing as a common law marriage here, however long you have lived together and whether or not you have children.

When a cohabiting couple separates, there is no general power for a court to divide property fairly or to order one partner to support the other. Instead, disputes about a shared home are usually decided under property and trust law. That law looks mainly at who legally owns the property, what each person contributed, and what the couple agreed or intended about ownership.

This can come as a shock. Someone who gave up work to raise children, or who paid for years of household bills while their partner paid the mortgage, may find their position is very different from what they expected. On the other hand, a partner who owns the home may be worried about a claim from someone who moved in later. Both sides often feel uncertain, and both benefit from clear information early on.

There have been calls for many years to give cohabiting couples more protection, and the government has signalled an interest in reform. At the time of writing the law has not changed, so the rules described on this page still apply. Your solicitor will tell you if anything has changed by the time you speak to them.

Common situations

  • A home in one partner's sole name where the other contributed
  • A jointly owned home where you disagree about shares
  • Arguments about money one partner put in from savings or an inheritance
  • Promises that a partner would always have a home or a share of it
  • Disputes over furniture, cars, pets or other belongings
  • Needs of children after their parents separate
  • Claims after a partner has died

If you are in the middle of a separation, it can be hard to think clearly about property and money. You do not need to have the answers before contacting a firm. A solicitor can help you understand the questions that matter.

Your rights after separating depend heavily on how the home is owned and what happened between you while you lived together. A solicitor will want to understand both. These are the main things they usually look at.

How the home is owned

If the home is owned jointly, the title at HM Land Registry will show both names. Joint owners hold the property either as joint tenants or as tenants in common. Joint tenants own the whole property together. Tenants in common each own a share, which may or may not be equal. The difference matters, particularly if one owner dies.

Declarations of trust

Some couples sign a declaration of trust when they buy, setting out who owns what share and how the money will be divided on a sale. Where a valid declaration exists, it is usually the starting point and can be very hard to go behind. Finding out whether one exists, and what it says, is often an early step.

Homes in one name

If the home is in one partner's sole name, the other partner may still be able to claim a share. This usually depends on showing there was a shared intention that they would have an interest and that they relied on it to their detriment. Contributions to the purchase price or mortgage can be important, but so can conversations, promises and the way the couple organised their money.

Promises

Sometimes one partner makes clear promises that the other will always have a home or will share in the property, and the other partner acts on that promise in a significant way. In some situations the law can step in to prevent the promise being unfairly withdrawn. These claims depend on detailed facts and evidence.

Rented homes

Where the home is rented, the tenancy agreement matters. If you are joint tenants, ending the tenancy and dealing with rent arrears can raise practical problems. If the tenancy is in one name only, the other partner may have limited rights to stay. Your solicitor can explain how this works for your type of tenancy.

Money outside the home

Savings, investments and pensions usually belong to whoever holds them, even after many years together. Joint bank accounts can be more complicated, especially where both of you paid in and took money out. A cohabiting partner has no general right to share in the other's pension on separation. This is one of the clearest differences from the position of married couples.

Understanding your rights does not always mean going to court. For many people it simply means knowing where they stand so they can negotiate with confidence.

Cohabitation property disputes are usually dealt with in the civil courts under the Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996, often shortened to TOLATA. This is very different from the family court process that married couples use. Your solicitor will explain the process in detail, but the general picture looks like this.

These cases can feel very formal compared with family proceedings. The court looks closely at documents and evidence rather than at what would seem fair overall, and the rules on costs are stricter. Knowing this from the start helps people make realistic decisions about whether and how to pursue or defend a claim.

1. Gathering the facts

Your solicitor will want to see the title documents, any declaration of trust, mortgage records and bank statements showing who paid what. Emails, messages and other records of what was discussed can also be important. These cases often turn on events from years ago, so gathering evidence early helps.

2. Setting out the claim

Before going to court, the person making the claim is expected to write to the other side setting out what they say and why. The other side is given time to respond. This exchange often clarifies the issues and can lead to settlement.

3. Trying to settle

Courts expect people to consider negotiation or another form of dispute resolution before and during proceedings. Many cohabitation disputes are settled through solicitors' letters, round table meetings or mediation. Settling can save a great deal of cost and stress.

4. Court proceedings

If agreement is not possible, either partner may apply to court. The court can decide who owns what share of the property and whether it should be sold. It cannot simply divide everything in a way it thinks fair, as it can for married couples. Cases can take many months and involve witness statements and a hearing.

Costs risk

One important difference from family cases is that in civil proceedings the losing party is often ordered to pay a large part of the winning party's legal costs. This makes it especially important to understand the strength of your case and to think carefully about any offers to settle. Your solicitor can explain how offers to settle can affect who pays costs.

If you have children together, there may be additional claims that are made for the children's benefit rather than for either parent. These sit alongside any property dispute and are dealt with under Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989.

What can be claimed

A parent caring for a child may be able to ask the court for orders against the other parent to meet the child's needs. These can include providing a home for the child until they grow up, a lump sum for particular costs such as furniture or a car, and in some cases payments for school fees or other expenses. The home is usually provided for the child's benefit and often returns to the paying parent later.

Child maintenance

Regular child maintenance is normally dealt with through the Child Maintenance Service or a family based arrangement rather than the courts. In some cases, for example where the paying parent's income is very high or lives abroad, the court may have a role. Your solicitor can explain how maintenance and other claims fit together.

Arrangements for the children

Where children live and how they spend time with each parent is a separate question from money and property. Unmarried fathers usually have parental responsibility if they are named on the birth certificate for a child registered after December 2003. Many parents agree arrangements themselves or through mediation. Where they cannot, a parent can apply for a child arrangements order.

Claims for children are usually brought in the family court, while property disputes between the parents are brought in the civil courts. Sometimes both are running at the same time, and your solicitor will think about how they fit together so that you are not paying for the same work twice.

Separating with children is hard for everyone. Family solicitors are used to helping parents keep the focus on the children while sorting out the practical and financial side.

Cohabitation disputes can vary greatly in cost. A dispute that is settled after a few letters will cost far less than one that goes to a final hearing. Because the law is technical and the facts can be complex, it is important to get clear information about costs at the start.

How firms usually charge

Most firms charge by the hour for contested property disputes, sometimes with a fixed fee for an initial review of your position. Some offer fixed fees for particular stages, such as sending a letter of claim or preparing a cohabitation agreement. Barristers may be needed for advice or hearings, and their fees are usually extra.

Other costs to think about

  • Court fees, which change from time to time
  • Valuation of the property by a surveyor
  • Mediation fees if you use a mediator
  • Costs of obtaining old bank or mortgage records
  • The risk of paying the other side's costs if you lose at court

Ask the firm for a written estimate, what it covers and when it will be reviewed. Ask too about legal expenses insurance, as some home insurance policies include cover for certain disputes. Your solicitor can check whether any policy you have might help.

It is worth weighing the likely cost of a dispute against the amount at stake. Spending heavily to argue over a modest difference in shares may not make sense, while a claim to a substantial share in a valuable home may justify more. A solicitor can help you see the risks clearly, though the decision is always yours.

Not everyone who contacts a solicitor about cohabitation is in a dispute. Many couples want to protect themselves before problems arise, especially when buying a home together or moving into a home one partner already owns.

Cohabitation agreements

A cohabitation agreement is a written agreement between a couple who live together. It can record who owns what, how bills will be shared and what will happen to the home and belongings if you separate. It can also cover pets, cars and arrangements if one of you dies. For it to carry weight, each of you should give full information and ideally take independent advice.

Declarations of trust

When buying a home, a declaration of trust can set out the shares each of you will own. This can be especially useful where one person puts in a larger deposit or where money comes from family. It is usually prepared alongside the purchase by a conveyancing or family solicitor.

Wills

Cohabiting partners do not automatically inherit from each other if one dies without a will. A partner may be able to make a claim against the estate in some circumstances, but that is uncertain and can be distressing. Making wills is one of the simplest ways for cohabiting couples to protect each other.

Reviewing as life changes

An agreement made when you first move in may not suit you after children arrive or one of you stops work. Reviewing your arrangements at major life events helps them keep up with your relationship.

If you are about to buy a home together or move into a partner's home, a short conversation with a solicitor can save a great deal of worry later. Many people find that talking openly about money at the start actually strengthens their relationship.

Cohabitation disputes questions and answers

Is there such a thing as a common law spouse?
No. Living together doesn’t give the same rights as marriage in England and Wales.
Can I claim a share of a house in my partner’s name?
Possibly, depending on contributions and what was agreed or intended. A solicitor can assess your position.
Should we have a cohabitation agreement?
It can avoid disputes later by recording who owns what and what happens if you separate.
Which areas do you cover?
Family law enquiries in England and Wales.
I’m worried about my safety. Can you help?
If you are in danger, call 999. For confidential support, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free on 0808 2000 247, 24 hours a day.
Will my partner find out I made an enquiry?
No. We only share your details with the firm we introduce you to, and they contact you, not anyone else.
Do cohabiting couples have the same rights as married couples?
No. In England and Wales, living together does not give the same financial rights as marriage or civil partnership, however long you have been together. The idea of a common law spouse is a myth. Your rights depend mainly on property and trust law and on any agreements you made.
I have lived with my partner for years. Does that give me a claim?
The length of the relationship alone does not create a claim. What matters is how the property is owned, what each of you contributed and what was agreed or intended about ownership. A long relationship may mean there is more evidence of shared intentions, but each case depends on its facts.
Can I claim a share of a house in my partner's sole name?
Possibly. You may have a claim if there was a shared understanding that you would have an interest and you relied on it to your disadvantage. Paying towards the mortgage, the deposit or major improvements can be relevant. A solicitor will look at the evidence and explain your position. Evidence is often key.
We own our home jointly. Do we each get half?
Not always. It depends on whether you are joint tenants or tenants in common, whether there is a declaration of trust and what you intended. Joint owners are often presumed to share equally unless there is good reason to think otherwise, but that presumption can sometimes be displaced. Your solicitor will look at the documents and the history.
What is a declaration of trust?
It is a document that sets out how a property is owned and how proceeds will be divided if it is sold. If you signed one when you bought your home, it is usually the starting point in any dispute. Your conveyancing solicitor may hold a copy if you are unsure.
Does paying household bills give me a share in the home?
Not on its own, as a rule. Paying for food and bills while your partner paid the mortgage can sometimes be relevant, but courts tend to give more weight to direct payments towards buying the property. The overall picture and what you both intended also matter. A solicitor can explain how your contributions might be viewed.
My partner promised I would always have a home. Does that count?
It might. If a clear promise was made and you relied on it in a significant way, such as giving up your own home or working unpaid in a family business, the law may sometimes give you a remedy. These claims depend heavily on evidence, so try to keep any messages or records.
Can I stay in the home while we sort things out?
That depends on whether you own or rent it and in whose name. A joint owner usually has a right to live there. A partner with no legal ownership may have more limited rights. A solicitor can explain your position, and you should tell them if you have any safety concerns.
My partner moved into my home. Can they claim part of it?
A partner does not gain a share of your home simply by living there. A claim usually needs evidence of a shared intention that they would have an interest, and that they relied on it. Payments towards the mortgage or major works, or promises you made, could be relevant. A solicitor can explain the risk in your situation.
What is a TOLATA claim?
It is a claim under the Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996. It allows the court to decide who owns what share of a property and whether it should be sold. Most cohabitation property disputes are brought this way, in the civil courts rather than the family court.
Do we have to go to court?
No. Many disputes are settled through negotiation or mediation. Courts expect people to try to resolve things before starting proceedings, and the threat of paying the other side's costs encourages settlement. Court is usually a last resort. Settling also gives you more control over the outcome, because you agree terms rather than having them decided for you. Your solicitor can explain the options for resolving things without a hearing.
How long does a cohabitation dispute take?
It varies. Some settle within weeks after an exchange of letters. Contested court cases can take a year or more to reach a final hearing. Your solicitor can give you a better idea once they understand the facts and how the other side is responding. Keeping evidence organised and responding promptly can help avoid delay.
What evidence will I need?
Useful evidence often includes the title documents, any declaration of trust, mortgage statements, bank statements showing payments, and emails or messages about who would own what. Notes of important conversations can help too. Gather what you can, but do not take documents that belong only to your partner. Your solicitor will explain what is relevant.
Can the court order the house to be sold?
Yes, the court can order a sale and decide how the money is divided. It will consider factors such as the purpose for which the property was bought, the welfare of any children living there and the interests of anyone with a mortgage over it. It can also decide how sale costs are shared.
What if my partner refuses to sell?
A joint owner cannot usually force a sale without the other's agreement or a court order. If agreement is not possible, either owner can ask the court to decide. Your solicitor can explain the steps and how long they might take. In the meantime, both owners usually remain responsible for the mortgage. Some couples agree that one person will buy the other out instead of selling.
What happens to belongings like the car or furniture?
Generally, items belong to whoever bought them or was given them, though shared purchases can be harder to untangle. Most couples resolve this through discussion because the value rarely justifies court action. A solicitor can help you approach it sensibly. Cars on finance can be more complex, because the finance agreement is with the lender. Pets are usually treated as belongings in law, though many couples agree arrangements that suit everyone.
What is a letter of claim?
It is a letter sent before court proceedings that sets out the claim, the facts relied on and what is being asked for. The other side is given a reasonable time to reply. Courts expect this step, and failing to take it can affect who pays costs later. It is usually prepared by a solicitor.
Can I protect my interest while the dispute is going on?
In some cases a solicitor can register a restriction or notice at HM Land Registry, which can make it harder for the property to be sold or mortgaged without you knowing. Whether this is possible depends on the circumstances. Joint owners may also be able to change how they hold the property.
Can I claim money for our children?
Yes. Child maintenance is usually arranged through the Child Maintenance Service or by agreement. In addition, a parent can sometimes apply to the court for further orders for the child's benefit, such as housing or a lump sum, under Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989. These claims sit alongside any property dispute.
Can I stay in the home because of the children?
The court can sometimes order that a home is provided for a child until they finish their education, after which it may return to the other parent. This is for the child's benefit rather than the parent's. A solicitor can explain whether it is realistic in your case. Each case depends on the family's circumstances.
Does an unmarried father have parental responsibility?
An unmarried father usually has parental responsibility if he is named on the birth certificate of a child registered after 1 December 2003. Otherwise he can gain it by agreement with the mother or by court order. Parental responsibility is separate from questions about money. A solicitor can explain how it can be obtained if needed.
Can I claim maintenance for myself?
Generally no. Unlike married couples, cohabiting partners cannot usually claim maintenance for themselves when they separate. Any claim for money is normally made for the benefit of children. Your solicitor can explain what might be available in your situation. In some cases, orders for the children may indirectly help the parent who cares for them, for example by providing a home. A solicitor can explain how this might apply to you.
How are arrangements for the children decided?
Most parents agree arrangements themselves or with the help of mediation. If that is not possible, a parent can apply for a child arrangements order, usually after attending a mediation information meeting. The court's main concern is the child's welfare. Most parents agree without a court ever being involved. A solicitor can explain the process if agreement is not possible.
Does it matter if the children are not both of ours?
Claims under Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989 are usually made against a child's legal parent. If a child is not your partner's child, claims against them for that child are more limited. Every family is different, so a solicitor will want to understand who is in your household.
What is a cohabitation agreement?
It is a written agreement between people who live together. It can record who owns what, how costs are shared and what will happen if you separate. Each person should give full information and take independent advice so that the agreement is more likely to be respected. It can be reviewed as life changes.
Is a cohabitation agreement legally binding?
It can be treated as a contract if it is properly made, with clear terms and both people understanding what they signed. Arrangements for children can always be looked at again. A solicitor can explain how to make an agreement as robust as possible. Reviewing it after major changes helps it stay relevant.
Can we make an agreement after moving in together?
Yes. You can make a cohabitation agreement at any time. Many couples do so when something changes, such as buying a home, one partner investing money or having a child. An agreement made later can still record what you each contributed in the past and how you want things to work in future. The same safeguards apply, with each of you giving full information and ideally taking independent advice.
What happens if my partner dies?
A cohabiting partner does not automatically inherit if there is no will. You may be able to make a claim against the estate in some circumstances, but claims have strict time limits. A solicitor can explain your options, and it is wise to seek advice promptly. The usual time limit is six months from the grant of probate or letters of administration.
Do we need wills if we live together?
Without wills, your partner may not inherit anything from you. Jointly owned property held as joint tenants passes automatically to the survivor, but other assets may not. Many cohabiting couples make wills alongside a cohabitation agreement. Pensions and life insurance often pass according to nomination forms rather than a will, so it is worth checking those too. A solicitor can explain the options.
What does severing a joint tenancy mean?
If you own a home as joint tenants and one of you dies, the survivor automatically inherits the whole property. Severing the joint tenancy changes this to a tenancy in common, so each share can pass under a will. People sometimes do this after separating. A solicitor can explain the effect.
What kind of solicitor do I need?
Cohabitation disputes involve property and trust law as well as family law. Look for a solicitor with experience of cohabitation or TOLATA claims. The firm we introduce you to handles this kind of matter. Some disputes also need input from a barrister, especially if they reach court. Your solicitor will tell you if that is likely and what it might cost.

What should I bring to the first conversation?
It helps to have the property details, how it is owned, any declaration of trust or cohabitation agreement, and a rough list of who paid what. Details of any children and any letters from your partner or their solicitor are useful too. Do not worry if you do not have everything yet.
What is a prenuptial agreement?
It is a written agreement made before marriage that sets out how a couple's money and property would be dealt with if the marriage ended. It often protects assets one person brings into the marriage or expects to receive. The equivalent for civil partners is usually called a pre-registration agreement.
Are prenups legally binding in England and Wales?
Not automatically. A court dealing with a divorce still decides the finances. However, the courts will usually give effect to a prenup that each person entered freely, with a full understanding of its effect, unless it would be unfair to hold them to it. A properly prepared agreement can carry a great deal of weight.
Do only wealthy people need a prenup?
No. Prenups are common where there is family wealth, but many couples with modest assets make one too. Someone who owns a flat, has savings from before the marriage or has children from a previous relationship may want clarity. The decision is a personal one. A solicitor can explain whether an agreement would make a real difference for you.
Is it unromantic to ask for a prenup?
Many people worry about this. Couples who go through the process often say the open conversation about money brought them closer. A solicitor can explain how to raise the subject and how the process works, so that it feels practical rather than adversarial. Raising it early, well before the wedding, usually makes the conversation easier for both of you.
Can civil partners make a similar agreement?
Yes. Couples entering a civil partnership can make a pre-registration agreement, which works in the same way as a prenup. Civil partners can also make an agreement after registration, similar to a postnuptial agreement. The same safeguards apply, including full disclosure, independent advice for each person and signing well before the registration date. The court approaches these agreements on dissolution in the same way as a prenup on divorce.
Can a prenup protect a future inheritance?
A prenup can say how an inheritance received during the marriage should be treated. Inherited assets are often treated differently from those built up together, but a court will still consider whether both people's needs are met. A solicitor can explain how an inheritance might be dealt with. Keeping an inheritance separate during the marriage can also help.
Can a prenup protect my business?
A prenup can set out how a business owned before the marriage, or a share in a family business, should be treated. This is one of the most common reasons people make one. A valuation of the business may be needed as part of disclosure. The agreement can also deal with any growth in the business during the marriage and whether that is shared.
What happens if we do not have a prenup?
If the marriage ends, the court decides how to divide the finances using a set of factors, including needs, contributions and the length of the marriage. Assets built up during the marriage are often shared. Assets brought in from outside may be treated differently, but there is no certainty. A prenup lets you set out your own view in advance.
