The basics

Stock is paid for before it is sold, and the delay between the two is where the pressure builds. A gift retailer buying for Christmas in September, a fashion brand placing a minimum order with an overseas maker or a food wholesaler taking a pallet deal at a discount all pay out first and recover the money as customers buy. An unsecured loan gives the cash to place the order with no need to offer the stock or any property as security. The instalments begin straight away, so the question is whether sales will arrive in time to cover them.

Lenders look at how quickly your stock turns, your margins and your bank statements through previous peaks. For example, an online homeware seller with £800,000 of annual revenue might borrow £50,000 over 12 months to buy for the autumn season, and repay from the sales between October and December. A bulk purchase only makes sense when the supplier’s discount clearly outweighs the interest, and when the goods will sell before they go out of fashion, out of date or out of season.

What businesses use it for

Seasonal build-up

Buy ahead of a peak such as Christmas, summer or a school term, then repay as sales come in.

A bulk-buy discount

Take a supplier’s price break on a large order, provided the saving is bigger than the cost of the borrowing.

Minimum order quantities

Meet the minimum run a manufacturer or overseas supplier demands, which can be well beyond one month of sales.

Launching a new range

Buy the first batch of a new product line before the first sales show how fast it will move.

How it fits

Term and the stock cycle

Match the term to how long stock takes to sell, with some room to spare. A 12-month loan suits goods that clear in three to six months, while a six-month loan may suit fast sellers. Instalments that run on long after the goods have sold become a drag on later orders.

Check the margin first

Work out gross margin before you borrow. If stock sells for much more than it cost, the interest is easier to absorb. Thin-margin lines leave little room, and lenders will notice that in the accounts.

What lenders look at

Previous seasons’ bank statements, stock and creditor figures in the accounts, and whether the business has paid suppliers reliably. A purchase order or supplier quote helps show the money is going on stock.

Things to consider

Unsold stock still has to be paid for

The loan does not shrink if the goods sell slowly. Fashion, perishable and fast-changing technology lines carry the greatest risk, and a discount sale to clear stock can eat the margin that was meant to repay the loan.

A revolving facility may follow the cycle better

If you buy stock repeatedly through the year, a revolving credit facility lets you draw and repay as needed and pay interest only on what you use. A broker may suggest this, or stock finance, where it fits better.

Order deadlines can pass

Seasonal orders have supplier cut-off dates, and an application takes time to arrange. Apply well ahead, and mention the date to the broker at the start.

Questions and answers

Can I borrow to buy stock with an unsecured loan?
Yes. Many lenders will lend to a company for stock if its accounts and bank statements support the repayments. The stock is not taken as security. Some ask for a supplier quote. A broker can tell you whether a loan or a revolving facility looks more suitable for the way you buy.
How long should the term be?
Roughly as long as the stock takes to sell, with a little room for slow sales. For a seasonal build-up that is often between six and twelve months. Longer terms lower monthly payments but raise the total cost, and leave you repaying after the goods have gone.
What if the stock does not sell?
The loan still has to be repaid. Talk to the lender early rather than miss payments. This is why many businesses buy stock in stages rather than all at once, and why a lender will ask about your sell-through history during the application.
Is stock finance different?
Yes. Stock finance, sometimes called inventory finance, uses the stock itself as security and is usually arranged for particular goods, often higher-value items. An unsecured loan is more general. A broker can tell you whether stock finance is available for what you sell.
Will the lender pay my supplier directly?
Normally the money goes to your company’s bank account and you pay the supplier. Some lenders can pay a supplier directly on larger purchases. Ask the broker, particularly if you are buying from overseas and need to arrange payment in another currency.
