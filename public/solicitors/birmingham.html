Choosing a solicitor in Birmingham

The right firm for you depends on the kind of matter you have, not just on being close by. A conveyancing firm on the high street in Birmingham may not handle a contested licence review, and a family law specialist will not usually deal with a speeding charge. That is why we ask what your matter is about before we introduce anyone.

It is worth asking any firm how it charges before work starts. Many matters are handled for a fixed fee, while others are charged by the hour. A good firm will explain the likely total, what could make it change and what is included, and confirm it in writing.

You can check that a solicitor is regulated on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and that a licensed conveyancer is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Every firm we introduce in Birmingham and the West Midlands is regulated, and you are free to decide whether to instruct them.

How location is used

When you make an enquiry, we ask where you are, or where the property, court or premises is. We use that to introduce a firm that covers your area. For some matters that means a firm close to you. For others it means a firm that works across England and Wales and is used to handling matters wherever the client lives.

Using the local pages

Each town and city page gives a short overview of the areas we cover there, and links to pages for each type of solicitor. Those pages explain in general terms how things usually work for that kind of matter and what to have ready. When you are ready, the form takes about two minutes.

It is natural to want a solicitor who is close by. For some kinds of matter that is useful. For others, the location of the firm makes very little difference to the service you receive.

When local knowledge can help

Some matters involve a particular court, council or local authority. A motoring offence is usually heard at a magistrates' court in the area where the offence happened. A premises licence is applied for to the local council where the premises is, and the council's licensing policy and the views of local police and residents can matter. A family dispute may be heard at the local family court. In these cases a firm that knows the local procedures and people can be an advantage, and attending a hearing is easier if the firm is not far away.

When location matters less

Conveyancing, lease extensions and business debt recovery are now mostly handled by phone, email, post and online portals. Property searches and Land Registry applications are done electronically, county court claims for money are often issued online, and documents can be signed and returned without visiting an office. Many people use a firm in a different town and never meet in person, and are perfectly happy with the service.

Meeting in person

If meeting face to face matters to you, say so when you make your enquiry. Some people find it easier to discuss personal matters, such as a separation, in person. Others are more comfortable with video calls. Many firms offer both, and you can ask what the firm usually does on the first call.

Getting the balance right

In the end, experience in your type of matter, clear communication and fair fees usually matter more than the distance to the office. A nearby firm that rarely handles your kind of matter may not be the best choice, while a firm a few towns away that does it every day may suit you well. The firm we introduce handles your type of work and covers your area.

Area by area

Conveyancing firms routinely act for buyers and sellers all over England and Wales. What matters more is that the firm is on your lender's panel and responds quickly. Local knowledge can help with things like unusual local searches or properties affected by mining or flood risk, but a good conveyancer will know how to deal with those wherever the property is.

Lease extension work is mostly paperwork and negotiation with the freeholder's solicitor, supported by a valuation from a surveyor. The surveyor usually needs to know the local property market, but the solicitor can be based anywhere. If the matter goes to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), hearings are often held remotely or at a regional venue.

Family solicitors often deal with the family court that covers the area where you or your children live. Many hearings now take place by video, but some are in person. Some people prefer a firm they can visit, especially during a difficult separation.

Motoring and licensing cases are the most local. Magistrates' courts, council licensing committees and local police all have their own ways of working. A firm that regularly appears before them can be a real help, and it may be able to attend a hearing at short notice.

Business debt recovery is usually handled through letters, negotiation and, if needed, the county court. Many money claims are issued and managed online, so the firm's location matters less than its experience with commercial debts.

Wales

England and Wales share one legal system, so firms in England can usually act for clients in Wales and the other way round. Some differences do apply in Wales, such as Land Transaction Tax instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax on property purchases, and some housing and planning rules. If you would like to deal with a firm in Welsh, mention it in the notes and the firm will tell you whether it can help.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, so we do not cover matters there for conveyancing, family law, motoring offences, licensing, lease extensions or debt recovery. Immigration introductions are available across the whole of the UK.

Whether a firm is round the corner or at the other end of the country, it is worth checking it before you instruct it. This is easy to do and takes a few minutes.

Check the register

Solicitors and law firms in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can search for them on the SRA register. Licensed conveyancers are regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers, which has its own register. Regulated immigration advisers can be checked with their regulator, such as the SRA or the Immigration Advice Authority.

Look for the regulatory information

Regulated law firms must show certain information on their websites, including who regulates them and their registration number. Many firms display the SRA's digital badge, which links to the firm's entry on the register. If a firm's website does not say who regulates it, ask.

Check the people, not just the firm

Ask who will handle your matter day to day. That might be a solicitor, a chartered legal executive, a licensed conveyancer or a paralegal working under supervision. All of these can be appropriate. It is reasonable to ask about their experience and who supervises them.

Read the paperwork

Before any work starts, the firm should send a client care letter and terms of business. These set out the fees, who will do the work, how complaints are handled and, if you were introduced by Leaflegal, the fee the firm pays for the introduction. Read them carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

Every firm we introduce is regulated

Leaflegal only introduces firms that are regulated for the work they do. Checking for yourself is still a good habit, and a good firm will be happy for you to do so.

Warning signs

Be cautious if anyone offering legal help will not say who regulates them, asks you to pay large sums before explaining their fees in writing, promises a particular result or pressures you to decide on the spot. Be especially careful with emails asking you to send money for a property purchase to new bank details. Always check bank details by phone using a number you already know before sending money to a firm.

Does a local firm cost more or less

Legal fees vary between firms, and location is only one factor. Firms in some large cities may have higher overheads, but that does not always mean higher prices for a particular piece of work. Fixed fee work, such as many house purchases, can be priced similarly across the country. The best way to understand the cost is to ask the firm for a written estimate or quote.

Travel and attendance

Where a solicitor needs to attend a court or hearing, the cost of travel and waiting time may be included in a fixed fee or charged separately. A local firm may charge less for travel to a local court. Ask how attendance is charged so you can compare like for like.

Disbursements

On top of the firm's own fees there may be disbursements, such as court fees, search fees, Land Registry fees, licensing application fees set by the council, or a barrister's fee. Official fees change from time to time, and GOV.UK lists many of them. The firm will tell you which apply.

Comparing quotes

If you speak to more than one firm, check that each quote covers the same work and includes the same items. A lower headline fee may leave out things that another firm includes. Ask each firm to confirm what is and is not included.

Legal questions from people in Birmingham

One law firm that handles your type of matter, usually by phone. We don't pass your details to a list of firms.

Conveyancing, family law, motoring offences, licensing, lease extensions and business debt recovery in England and Wales, plus trademarks and related IP and introductions to regulated immigration lawyers across the UK.
Can you help with legal aid?
We introduce people looking for paid legal help. If you think you may qualify for legal aid, GOV.UK has a checker and can point you to a legal aid adviser.
What happens to my information?
We only share your details with the firm we introduce you to, and only if you agree. We never share the details of your matter with advertisers. Read our Privacy Policy for the full details.
What if I own the property with someone else?
All registered owners usually need to agree to the sale and sign the contract and transfer. If one owner has died, your conveyancer will need to see the death certificate and may need probate depending on how the property was owned. If owners disagree about selling, it is a different situation and legal advice is needed.
Can I use the same firm as my buyer?
Professional rules usually prevent one firm acting for both buyer and seller in a residential sale, because their interests conflict. There are limited exceptions, but in practice most transactions have a separate firm on each side. Your conveyancer will tell you if a conflict arises.
How detailed should my answers be on the forms?
Answer accurately and from your own knowledge. If you are not sure, say so rather than guessing, and do not leave out something you know about because you think it is minor. The buyer relies on your answers, and inaccurate replies can lead to claims after the sale. Your conveyancer can help with anything you find unclear.
Do I have to tell buyers about disputes with neighbours?
The property information form asks about disputes and complaints, including ones that have been resolved. If you have had a disagreement over boundaries, noise, parking or anything else, it usually needs to be disclosed. Your conveyancer can help you describe it accurately and in proportion.
What if I cannot find a building regulation certificate?
Tell your conveyancer early. They may be able to obtain a copy from the local authority's records, or explain options such as a regularisation application or indemnity insurance for the buyer. The right approach depends on the work, how long ago it was done and what the buyer and their lender will accept.
What is the fittings and contents form for?
It sets out exactly what is staying and what is going. Without it, there can be disagreements about light fittings, curtains, appliances, shelving and garden items. Once it forms part of the contract, you are expected to leave what you said you would leave, in the condition it was in.
Do I need to mention Japanese knotweed?
The property information form asks whether the property is affected by Japanese knotweed. If it is, or has been, it needs to be disclosed, along with any treatment plan in place. Buyers' lenders often want a management plan with an insurance backed guarantee, so having this ready can help.
Does the reason for the divorce affect the money?
Usually not. The court only takes conduct into account in rare cases where it would be unfair to ignore it, such as serious financial misconduct. Most of the time, who was to blame for the breakdown makes no difference to the settlement. Under no fault divorce, the reasons for the breakdown are not part of the divorce application either.
Are unmarried couples treated the same way?
No. Unmarried couples do not have the same financial claims when they separate, however long they lived together. Disputes about a shared home are usually decided under property law. There may be claims for the benefit of children. A solicitor can explain the difference. This is why some couples who live together choose to make a cohabitation agreement.
Can we agree a settlement without solicitors?
Some couples do reach agreement themselves, sometimes with a mediator. To become legally binding, the agreement still needs to be turned into a consent order and approved by the court. Many people ask a solicitor to check the agreement and draft the order, so that it does what they intend.
What is Form E?
Form E is the standard financial statement used in financial cases in England and Wales. It sets out your income, assets, debts, pensions and needs, with supporting documents. It is used in court proceedings and is often used voluntarily in negotiation too, so that both people have the same information.
What documents will I need?
Typically twelve months of bank statements, recent payslips or tax returns, pension valuations, mortgage statements, details of savings and investments, and business accounts if you have a business. Your solicitor will give you a checklist. Gathering documents early can save time and costs. If you do not have access to some documents, such as joint account statements, tell your solicitor, as there are ways of obtaining them.
What if my ex will not give full disclosure?
The court can order disclosure, and can draw conclusions about someone's finances if they fail to provide it. Costs orders can also be made against people who do not cooperate. Your solicitor can explain the options for obtaining the information. In serious cases, a settlement reached without full disclosure can later be set aside. Keep a note of anything you think is missing and share it with your solicitor.
Can I look at my partner's private documents?
Be careful. Taking or copying documents that belong to your partner, or accessing their emails or accounts without permission, can be unlawful and can harm your case. Tell your solicitor what you know or suspect, and they will explain the proper ways to obtain information. Documents you already have lawful access to, such as joint statements, are different.
Is drug driving a criminal offence?
Yes. It is a criminal offence and a conviction appears on your criminal record as well as your driving record. It can affect insurance, some jobs and travel to certain countries. Separately from the criminal case, if you are found to have been in possession of an illegal drug, that could be dealt with as a different offence.
How long do drugs stay in the blood?
It varies a great deal between drugs and between people, depending on things like the dose, how often the drug is used, body weight and metabolism. Some substances can remain in the blood for longer than people expect, even after they no longer feel any effect. There is no reliable rule of thumb, which is why many cases need careful scientific review.
What is the roadside drug test?
It is a preliminary test, usually a saliva swab on a handheld device, that screens for some common drugs such as cannabis and cocaine. A positive result usually leads to arrest and a blood test at the police station. The roadside result on its own is not normally the evidence of the level used to prosecute.
Can I refuse a blood test?
Refusing to give a blood sample without a reasonable excuse is an offence in itself, with penalties similar to drug driving. A reasonable excuse is usually medical, such as a genuine phobia of needles supported by evidence. Whether an excuse is reasonable is decided by the court. A solicitor can explain how this is assessed.
Why is my case taking so long?
Blood samples must be analysed by an accredited laboratory, and this can take weeks or months. You are usually released while you wait and are not charged until the results are back. The delay can be stressful, but it also gives time to gather documents, such as prescriptions, and to speak to a solicitor before any court date.
Can I get my own sample tested?
When blood is taken, you should be offered part of the sample. You can ask an independent laboratory to analyse it, usually through your solicitor, who can arrange secure collection and storage. Independent analysis has a cost and is not useful in every case. Your solicitor can explain whether it is likely to help in yours.
What if the level is only just over the limit?
Laboratory results come with a margin of uncertainty, and reports usually take this into account. Where a level is close to the limit, a solicitor may want to look closely at how the analysis was done and what the uncertainty is. Whether this makes a difference depends on the evidence in the particular case.
Do I need to vary my licence after a refurbishment?
If the refurbishment changes the layout shown on your licence plan, such as moving the bar, changing exits or adding a new area, the plan will need updating. Small changes may be dealt with by a minor variation. Bigger changes may need a full variation. Trading on an out of date plan can cause problems.
How long does a minor variation take?
The council must make a decision within a set number of working days from receiving the application, and the white notice must be displayed for a shorter period than a full variation. In practice a minor variation is usually much quicker than a full variation. If the council does not decide in time, the application is treated as refused.
How long does a full variation take?
There is a 28 day consultation period. If there are no unresolved representations, the variation is usually granted shortly after. If a hearing is needed, it is normally held within 20 working days of the end of the consultation. Allow time beforehand for preparing the application and any plans.
What notice do I need to display?
For a minor variation, a white notice is displayed at the premises for a set period. For a full variation, a blue notice is displayed for 28 consecutive days, and a notice must also be published in a local newspaper. Each has set requirements for size, wording and position.
Can I trade under the new terms while I wait?
No. Until the variation is granted, you must trade in line with your existing licence. For a one off event, a temporary event notice may sometimes help, within the limits that apply. A solicitor can explain the options if you have a fixed date in mind.
Who is consulted on a minor variation?
The council decides which responsible authorities to consult, depending on what is being changed. Other people can also make representations during the notice period. The council considers what is said and decides whether the change could adversely affect the licensing objectives.
Do I need a new plan?
Only if the variation changes the layout or the areas used for licensable activities. If it does, the plan must meet the same requirements as for a new licence, including showing exits, fire safety equipment and the licensed area. Many operators use a specialist to redraw the plan.
Does sending a letter commit me to going to court?
No. A letter sets out what you intend to do if the debt is not paid, but you can still decide not to issue a claim. It is sensible not to threaten steps you have no intention of taking, so your solicitor will discuss your plans with you before the letter is sent.
Will a letter damage my relationship with the customer?
It can feel like a big step, especially with a customer you value. Many solicitors write in a firm but professional tone that leaves room to keep trading once the debt is paid. You can talk to your solicitor about the tone you want, and about whether a phone call from you first might help.
What should the letter include?
Most letters name the creditor and debtor accurately, explain how the debt arose, set out the amount owed including any interest and compensation, give a deadline and explain what will happen if the debt is not paid. Copies of key documents such as invoices and terms are often enclosed. Your solicitor will tailor the contents to your debt.
Who should the letter be addressed to?
For a company, the letter is usually addressed to the company itself at its registered office, and often also sent to its trading address or to the person you have been dealing with. For a sole trader, it goes to them personally. Getting the right legal name and address helps avoid arguments later.
Can I add interest in the letter?
Often, yes. If your contract sets a rate of interest for late payment, that rate usually applies. If it does not, statutory interest under late payment law can usually be claimed on debts between businesses. Your solicitor will calculate the interest and show how it has been worked out.
Can I add compensation for the cost of chasing?
Between businesses, late payment law usually allows a fixed sum of compensation for each late invoice, with the amount depending on the size of the debt. In some cases reasonable recovery costs above that can also be claimed. Your solicitor will check whether your contract affects this and include what can properly be claimed.
Should the letter mention insolvency?
Only where it is appropriate. Threatening a winding up petition or bankruptcy over a debt that is genuinely disputed can be treated as improper and can lead to costs being awarded against the creditor. A solicitor will decide whether any mention of insolvency routes is suitable in your case.
Is an informal extension the same as a deed of variation?
Sometimes. An informal extension can be done by granting a brand new lease or by varying the existing lease to add years. Your solicitor will explain which method is proposed and how it affects things like ground rent rules and registration.
Is an informal extension common?
Yes, many lease extensions are agreed informally, especially where the freeholder is a small landlord or a company owned by the flat owners. Larger freeholders also agree informal deals, sometimes on standard terms. Whether it is the right route for you depends on the offer and your plans, which is something a solicitor can talk through with you.
My freeholder has written to me with an offer. What now?
Many people ask a solicitor to review the offer before replying. They can explain what the terms mean, how they compare with the statutory route and whether anything might cause problems later. A surveyor can also give a view on value. You do not have to accept, and you do not have to reply straight away.
What ground rent terms should I look out for?
Rents that double at fixed intervals, rents linked to inflation without a cap, and rents that are high compared with the value of the flat can all cause concern for lenders and buyers. Rules introduced in 2022 also limit ground rent on many new leases. Your solicitor will check the proposed rent against these rules.
Can the freeholder add new terms to my lease?
In an informal deal, the freeholder can propose any changes, and you can accept, reject or negotiate them. This is different from the statutory route, where only limited changes are allowed. Your solicitor will point out any new terms and explain their effect.
Can I ask for changes too?
Yes. An informal negotiation works both ways. Leaseholders sometimes ask for defects in the lease to be fixed, for permission for alterations or for clearer service charge terms. Whether the freeholder agrees is a matter for negotiation.
How long should the new lease be?
That is for you and the freeholder to agree. It helps to compare the offer with what the statutory route would give, which is 90 years added under the long standing rules and 990 years under the 2024 Act once in force. Your solicitor can explain how the length could affect value and future plans.
How far back does a search go?
A register search covers all live marks, including old registrations that are still in force. It may also pick up recently expired marks that could be restored. Unregistered use is harder to date, which is one reason the firm's judgement matters.
Should I search company names too?
Yes, especially in the UK. A company trading under a similar name may have unregistered rights even if it never filed a trademark. Professional searches usually include Companies House and look at whether those companies are actually trading.
Can I search for a brand name on social media and marketplaces myself?
Yes, and it is a sensible early step. Searching the main marketplaces, app stores and social platforms can show whether others trade under a similar name. It won't tell you about legal rights, so it works best alongside a register search and an opinion from a professional.
Why do search results include marks in other classes?
Because goods in different classes can still be similar. Clothing and bags sit in different classes but are often sold together, and so are software and online services. A good search looks across related classes as well as the ones you will file in.
What if the search finds an identical mark?
It depends on the goods and services. An identical mark for completely different goods may not be a problem. An identical mark for the same goods usually is. The firm will look at the details, including whether the earlier mark is in use, and explain your options.
What does moderate risk mean?
It usually means there are earlier marks close enough to cause concern, but there are arguments either way. You might go ahead, adapt the mark or narrow your goods. The firm will explain what drives the risk and what would reduce it, so you can decide.
Can I use a name if the earlier mark isn't being used?
If a registered mark hasn't been genuinely used for five years, it may be vulnerable to revocation. That can open up options, but it needs care, because owners can sometimes restart use and evidence of use isn't always visible online. The firm can investigate.
Does settlement affect my family?
It can, because family members often have their own immigration status and their own applications to think about. How your application relates to theirs depends on your circumstances. A regulated adviser can look at the whole family's position and explain how applications might be planned.
