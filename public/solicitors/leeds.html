Choosing a solicitor in Leeds

The right firm for you depends on the kind of matter you have, not just on being close by. A conveyancing firm on the high street in Leeds may not handle a contested licence review, and a family law specialist will not usually deal with a speeding charge. That is why we ask what your matter is about before we introduce anyone.

It is worth asking any firm how it charges before work starts. Many matters are handled for a fixed fee, while others are charged by the hour. A good firm will explain the likely total, what could make it change and what is included, and confirm it in writing.

You can check that a solicitor is regulated on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and that a licensed conveyancer is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Every firm we introduce in Leeds and West Yorkshire is regulated, and you are free to decide whether to instruct them.

How location is used

When you make an enquiry, we ask where you are, or where the property, court or premises is. We use that to introduce a firm that covers your area. For some matters that means a firm close to you. For others it means a firm that works across England and Wales and is used to handling matters wherever the client lives.

Using the local pages

Each town and city page gives a short overview of the areas we cover there, and links to pages for each type of solicitor. Those pages explain in general terms how things usually work for that kind of matter and what to have ready. When you are ready, the form takes about two minutes.

It is natural to want a solicitor who is close by. For some kinds of matter that is useful. For others, the location of the firm makes very little difference to the service you receive.

When local knowledge can help

Some matters involve a particular court, council or local authority. A motoring offence is usually heard at a magistrates' court in the area where the offence happened. A premises licence is applied for to the local council where the premises is, and the council's licensing policy and the views of local police and residents can matter. A family dispute may be heard at the local family court. In these cases a firm that knows the local procedures and people can be an advantage, and attending a hearing is easier if the firm is not far away.

When location matters less

Conveyancing, lease extensions and business debt recovery are now mostly handled by phone, email, post and online portals. Property searches and Land Registry applications are done electronically, county court claims for money are often issued online, and documents can be signed and returned without visiting an office. Many people use a firm in a different town and never meet in person, and are perfectly happy with the service.

Meeting in person

If meeting face to face matters to you, say so when you make your enquiry. Some people find it easier to discuss personal matters, such as a separation, in person. Others are more comfortable with video calls. Many firms offer both, and you can ask what the firm usually does on the first call.

Getting the balance right

In the end, experience in your type of matter, clear communication and fair fees usually matter more than the distance to the office. A nearby firm that rarely handles your kind of matter may not be the best choice, while a firm a few towns away that does it every day may suit you well. The firm we introduce handles your type of work and covers your area.

Area by area

Conveyancing firms routinely act for buyers and sellers all over England and Wales. What matters more is that the firm is on your lender's panel and responds quickly. Local knowledge can help with things like unusual local searches or properties affected by mining or flood risk, but a good conveyancer will know how to deal with those wherever the property is.

Lease extension work is mostly paperwork and negotiation with the freeholder's solicitor, supported by a valuation from a surveyor. The surveyor usually needs to know the local property market, but the solicitor can be based anywhere. If the matter goes to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), hearings are often held remotely or at a regional venue.

Family solicitors often deal with the family court that covers the area where you or your children live. Many hearings now take place by video, but some are in person. Some people prefer a firm they can visit, especially during a difficult separation.

Motoring and licensing cases are the most local. Magistrates' courts, council licensing committees and local police all have their own ways of working. A firm that regularly appears before them can be a real help, and it may be able to attend a hearing at short notice.

Business debt recovery is usually handled through letters, negotiation and, if needed, the county court. Many money claims are issued and managed online, so the firm's location matters less than its experience with commercial debts.

Wales

England and Wales share one legal system, so firms in England can usually act for clients in Wales and the other way round. Some differences do apply in Wales, such as Land Transaction Tax instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax on property purchases, and some housing and planning rules. If you would like to deal with a firm in Welsh, mention it in the notes and the firm will tell you whether it can help.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, so we do not cover matters there for conveyancing, family law, motoring offences, licensing, lease extensions or debt recovery. Immigration introductions are available across the whole of the UK.

Whether a firm is round the corner or at the other end of the country, it is worth checking it before you instruct it. This is easy to do and takes a few minutes.

Check the register

Solicitors and law firms in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can search for them on the SRA register. Licensed conveyancers are regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers, which has its own register. Regulated immigration advisers can be checked with their regulator, such as the SRA or the Immigration Advice Authority.

Look for the regulatory information

Regulated law firms must show certain information on their websites, including who regulates them and their registration number. Many firms display the SRA's digital badge, which links to the firm's entry on the register. If a firm's website does not say who regulates it, ask.

Check the people, not just the firm

Ask who will handle your matter day to day. That might be a solicitor, a chartered legal executive, a licensed conveyancer or a paralegal working under supervision. All of these can be appropriate. It is reasonable to ask about their experience and who supervises them.

Read the paperwork

Before any work starts, the firm should send a client care letter and terms of business. These set out the fees, who will do the work, how complaints are handled and, if you were introduced by Leaflegal, the fee the firm pays for the introduction. Read them carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

Every firm we introduce is regulated

Warning signs

Be cautious if anyone offering legal help will not say who regulates them, asks you to pay large sums before explaining their fees in writing, promises a particular result or pressures you to decide on the spot. Be especially careful with emails asking you to send money for a property purchase to new bank details. Always check bank details by phone using a number you already know before sending money to a firm.

Does a local firm cost more or less

Legal fees vary between firms, and location is only one factor. Firms in some large cities may have higher overheads, but that does not always mean higher prices for a particular piece of work. Fixed fee work, such as many house purchases, can be priced similarly across the country. The best way to understand the cost is to ask the firm for a written estimate or quote.

Travel and attendance

Where a solicitor needs to attend a court or hearing, the cost of travel and waiting time may be included in a fixed fee or charged separately. A local firm may charge less for travel to a local court. Ask how attendance is charged so you can compare like for like.

Disbursements

On top of the firm's own fees there may be disbursements, such as court fees, search fees, Land Registry fees, licensing application fees set by the council, or a barrister's fee. Official fees change from time to time, and GOV.UK lists many of them. The firm will tell you which apply.

Comparing quotes

If you speak to more than one firm, check that each quote covers the same work and includes the same items. A lower headline fee may leave out things that another firm includes. Ask each firm to confirm what is and is not included.

What if there is an old charge on my title?
Sometimes a mortgage or loan that was paid off years ago is still shown on the register because it was never formally removed. Your conveyancer will ask the old lender for proof that it was repaid and apply to remove it. This is usually straightforward but can take a little time.
Why do occupiers have to sign a form?
If an adult who is not an owner lives in the property, such as a partner, parent or grown up child, the lender wants to make sure that person cannot claim rights that rank ahead of the mortgage. The occupier is usually asked to sign a consent form, sometimes after taking independent advice.
What if my name on the register is different?
If you have changed your name since you bought, your conveyancer may ask for evidence, such as a marriage certificate or deed poll, and update the register at the same time as registering the new mortgage. It is a routine step but needs the correct documents.
Do I need to tell my landlord about a remortgage?
If your home is leasehold, the lease often requires you to give notice of any new mortgage to the landlord or managing agent, usually with a fee. Your conveyancer will check the lease and send the notice after completion. Some leases also require the landlord's consent before the new mortgage.
What if my lease is short?
Lenders have rules on how many years must remain on a lease, and a short lease can lead to a lower valuation or a refusal to lend. If your lease is getting short, the lender may want it extended first. Your conveyancer can explain the options, and a lease extension is a separate legal process.
What happens if the lender finds a problem with my title?
Your conveyancer will explain the issue and what the lender needs to resolve it. Common fixes include providing evidence, removing an old entry, obtaining a consent or arranging an insurance policy. If the problem cannot be fixed in time, the lender may delay completion or, rarely, withdraw its offer.
Can I remove my ex-partner from the mortgage when I remortgage?
Yes, this is common after a separation. It involves a transfer of equity, where your ex-partner transfers their share in the property to you, as well as the new mortgage in your sole name. The new lender must be satisfied you can afford the loan alone. Your ex-partner usually needs separate advice.
Can we turn our agreement into a court order?
Yes, in some cases. If parents reach agreement during court proceedings, the court can make a consent order recording it, provided it is satisfied the arrangements are in the child's interests. Outside proceedings, many parents simply record their agreement in a parenting plan, as the court only makes an order where that is better for the child.
At what age can my child decide where they live?
There is no fixed age in England and Wales. The court considers a child's wishes and feelings in light of their age and understanding. The views of a teenager are usually given more weight than those of a young child, but they are always one factor among several. The court always looks at the whole picture.
How are my child's views heard?
In court cases, a Cafcass officer or social worker usually speaks to the child in a way suited to their age, and reports their views to the court. Outside court, child inclusive mediation can give children a chance to be heard by a trained mediator without having to choose between parents.
My child does not want to see the other parent. What happens?
The reasons matter. The court tries to understand why a child feels this way and whether there are any safety concerns. Children's wishes are taken seriously but are not decisive on their own. A solicitor can explain how this is usually handled. Sometimes arrangements are rebuilt gradually, for example through shorter visits or indirect contact at first. A Cafcass officer may speak to the child.
How can we protect our children from the conflict?
Many parents find it helps to keep adult discussions away from the children, avoid asking children to pass messages, and make handovers calm and predictable. Parenting courses for separated parents can help. Your solicitor can point you to local support services. Children usually cope better with separation when they feel safe, are kept informed in a way suited to their age, and are not made to feel responsible for the adults' decisions.
Should I talk to my child about the court case?
Many parents find it helps to reassure children that they are loved and that the adults are sorting things out, without sharing details of the dispute or asking them to choose. Cafcass and other organisations publish guidance for parents on talking to children about separation. Your solicitor can point you to it.
Can I take my child on holiday abroad?
If a child arrangements order says the child lives with you, you can usually take them abroad for up to one month without the other parent's consent, unless the order says otherwise. Otherwise you generally need the written consent of everyone with parental responsibility, or the court's permission. Taking a child abroad without the right consent can be a criminal offence.
Does the type of vehicle I drive make a difference?
A totting up ban applies to your whole licence, so you cannot drive any vehicle during the ban, including vans, lorries or motorcycles if you hold those categories. Professional drivers with vocational licences may also face separate action from the traffic commissioner. Your solicitor can explain the criminal side, and your employer or licensing body can explain anything else.
Why do I have to go to court?
Courts do not normally ban someone without giving them the chance to attend and be heard. If your case started on paper, you will usually receive a notice inviting you to a hearing once the court sees that a ban is likely. Attending gives you the chance to put forward mitigation or an exceptional hardship argument. A solicitor can attend with you.
What happens at the totting up hearing?
The prosecutor sets out the offence and your record. The court confirms the points take you to 12 or more. If you are arguing exceptional hardship, you give evidence and may call witnesses, and the prosecutor can ask questions. The court then decides whether to ban and for how long, and deals with the fine.
Can I plead not guilty to the new offence?
Yes, if you dispute it. The court will then hold a trial. If you are found not guilty, no points are added and totting up does not apply. If you are convicted, the court goes on to consider a ban. A solicitor will review the evidence and explain the options before you decide how to plead.
Could the court give me a short ban instead?
Where a totting up ban applies, the court will usually deal with the case that way rather than imposing a short ban for the offence itself. The approach is set out in sentencing guidance. A solicitor can explain how the court is likely to treat your offence. The answer depends on the offence, the points it carries and the guidance that applies at the time of sentence.
Can I appeal a totting up ban?
There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court, normally within 21 days of sentence. You can sometimes apply for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is heard. An appeal needs proper grounds, and the Crown Court looks at the case afresh. A solicitor can tell you whether an appeal is realistic.
Do I need to bring anything to court?
It usually helps to bring the court papers, your driving licence details, and any documents that support what you want the court to know, such as letters from an employer. If you are arguing exceptional hardship, your solicitor will tell you what evidence and which witnesses are needed. Bringing details of your income helps the court set the fine.
Can I challenge interim steps?
Yes. The licence holder can make representations against the interim steps, and the council must then hold a hearing within a short time to consider them. This can be an opportunity to propose alternative measures that address the police concerns while allowing some trading to continue.
What happens at the full summary review hearing?
The full hearing must take place within 28 days of the council receiving the police application. The committee can take any of the steps available on an ordinary review and must also decide what to do about the interim steps. Thorough preparation in a short period is essential.
Is a closure order the same as a summary review?
No. Closure notices and orders are powers under separate anti-social behaviour laws, used by the police or council to close premises quickly, often with a court hearing within days. A summary review is a licensing process before the council. They can happen together, and a closure order can itself trigger a review. Both need urgent help.
Can my licence be revoked?
Yes. Revocation is the most serious outcome and is available on any review. Committees must decide what is appropriate to promote the licensing objectives, and many reviews end with conditions or management changes rather than revocation. National guidance treats some criminal activity, such as drug dealing or knowingly employing illegal workers, particularly seriously.
How long can a licence be suspended?
A committee can suspend a licence for up to three months. During a suspension, the premises cannot carry on the licensable activities covered by the suspension. Suspension is sometimes used as a deterrent or to give the operator time to put changes in place.
Can the committee remove my DPS?
Yes. Removing the DPS is one of the options available on a review, often where the committee has concerns about how the premises has been managed. The licence holder would then need to apply to name a new DPS before alcohol sales can resume.
Can I appeal the decision?
Yes. The licence holder can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. The review applicant and those who made relevant representations can appeal too. On an ordinary review, the decision usually does not take effect while an appeal is pending. A solicitor will explain the merits and costs risks.
Can the debtor pay in instalments instead?
The demand can be dealt with by securing or compounding the debt to the creditor's reasonable satisfaction, which can include an agreed payment plan. Whether to accept a plan is the creditor's decision. Your solicitor can help set out the terms in writing.
How long is a statutory demand valid for?
There is no simple expiry date, but a demand that is relied on long after it was served may be looked at critically, especially if circumstances have changed. If you intend to petition, it is usually sensible to do so within a reasonable time after the 21 days end. Your solicitor will advise on timing.
What if the company goes into administration?
If a company enters administration or another insolvency process, there are often restrictions on creditors taking action, including presenting a winding up petition. You would usually need to deal with the insolvency practitioner instead. Your solicitor will explain how this affects your debt.
Will I get paid first if I petition?
Not necessarily. In a liquidation, unsecured creditors usually share what is left after costs and certain preferred debts, regardless of who brought the petition. The petitioning creditor can usually recover its petition costs from the company's assets. The real leverage often comes before the hearing.
What if the debtor pays part of the debt?
A part payment reduces the debt but may not deal with the demand. If the balance owed by a company is still more than £750, a petition may still be possible. If it falls below the threshold, it may not. Your solicitor will look at what has been paid and explain the options for the remaining balance.
What if the debtor disputes the debt?
If the dispute is genuine and substantial, a statutory demand is usually not appropriate. A company can ask the court to stop a petition, and the creditor may be ordered to pay its costs. An individual can apply to set the demand aside. A court claim is usually the better way to resolve a real dispute.
Can a company set aside a statutory demand?
Not in the way an individual can. Instead, a company that disputes the debt can apply to the court for an order preventing the creditor from presenting a winding up petition. If the court finds a substantial dispute, the creditor is likely to face a costs order.
How does passing a notice to a buyer work?
Once a statutory notice has been served, its benefit can usually be assigned to a buyer at the same time as the flat is transferred. The buyer then takes over the claim and completes the extension. The assignment must be done correctly and at the right time. Both solicitors will agree the details, including who pays which costs.
Why will my lender not remortgage my flat?
Lenders usually require a minimum number of years on the lease, often counted from the end of the mortgage. If your lease is below that minimum, the lender may decline or set conditions. Requirements vary between lenders and change over time. A mortgage broker can explain the lending side, and a solicitor can explain the extension options.
I am buying a flat with a short lease. What can I do?
You can ask the seller to start a statutory extension and assign the notice to you on completion, or negotiate a price that reflects the cost of extending later. Your own solicitor will explain the options, check the lease carefully and tell you whether you are likely to be able to extend once you own the flat.
Do cash buyers buy short lease flats?
Some do, including investors who plan to extend the lease themselves. They usually take the cost and time of extending into account when deciding what to offer. Your estate agent can talk to you about the market, and your solicitor can explain the legal side of selling without extending.
Will a short lease delay my sale?
It can. Buyers' solicitors will raise questions, lenders may set conditions, and some buyers may want the extension completed first. Dealing with the lease early, before or soon after you put the flat on the market, usually reduces delays.
Will a short lease affect a buy to let mortgage?
Buy to let lenders usually have their own requirements about the remaining term of a lease, and these vary between lenders. A short lease may limit the lenders willing to refinance a rental flat. A mortgage broker can explain the lending options, and a solicitor can explain how an extension might change the position.
How much does it cost to extend a short lease?
There is the premium paid to the freeholder, your own solicitor's and surveyor's fees, possibly some of the freeholder's costs and other fees such as Land Registry charges. For a short lease the premium is usually the largest part. A surveyor's valuation is the best way to get a realistic figure for your flat.
What is an account of profits?
Instead of damages, a successful claimant can sometimes choose to recover the profits the infringer made from the infringing use. It can be useful where the infringer earned more than the claimant lost. Working out the profits can be complex.
Will the other side have to pay my legal costs?
If you win in court, you are usually awarded a contribution to your costs, but rarely all of them. In the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court recoverable costs are capped. Settlements often include an agreement about costs. The firm will explain what you might realistically recover.
Can I deal with infringement without going to court?
Yes, and most cases are. Letters, negotiation, platform takedowns, oppositions and domain dispute procedures all resolve disputes without court. Mediation is also available. Court proceedings are usually the last step, used when other routes have failed or speed is essential.
I've received a letter saying I infringe. What should I do?
Note the deadline, keep the letter and get advice before responding. The firm will assess the strength of the claim and help you reply in a way that protects your position. Many complaints are resolved through correspondence without court.
Do I have to stop using my brand immediately?
Not necessarily. It depends on how strong the claim is and what the risks are of carrying on. The firm may suggest continuing while the dispute is assessed, making changes or agreeing a phased rebrand. Each option carries different risks.
Can I challenge the other side's trademark?
Possibly. If their mark hasn't been used for five years, it may be revoked for non use. If it should never have been registered, it may be declared invalid. A successful challenge can remove or narrow the rights they rely on.
What if I was using my name first?
Earlier use can give you rights of your own and sometimes a defence, particularly if you have built up local or national goodwill before their mark was filed. The details matter, so evidence of your early use is very important.
My employer has its own adviser. Do I need one too?
Not necessarily. An employer's adviser acts for the employer, and some people are happy with that arrangement. Others want independent advice about their own position, especially for family matters. A regulated adviser can explain the difference and help you decide.
What if my job changes?
Changes to your job, employer or pay can matter for a work visa. If any change is proposed, speak to a regulated adviser before you agree to it. They can explain whether it affects your position and what, if anything, would need to happen. Keep copies of any letters about the change, such as a new contract or a letter confirming a pay rise or new job title.
What if I lose my job?
Losing your job can be worrying, and it can affect your immigration position. The timing can be important. Speak to a regulated adviser as soon as you know your job is ending, and keep copies of your contract, payslips and any letters from your employer.
Can my employer see what I tell my adviser?
If you instruct your own adviser, what you tell them is confidential under the rules of their regulator. If your employer has instructed the adviser, the position can be different, because the employer is the client. Ask at the start who the client is and how information will be shared, so that you understand the arrangement.
How much does a work visa cost?
There is usually an application fee and, for many people, the Immigration Health Surcharge, plus smaller costs such as translations. These amounts change from time to time. Check GOV.UK for the current figures, or ask your adviser for a full breakdown before you apply. If family members are applying too, the total can be much higher, so it is worth getting a full picture early.
How much will an adviser charge?
Many advisers charge a fixed fee for each application, with separate fees for family members. More complicated situations may be charged differently. Ask for the fee in writing, and check whether government fees are included, before you agree to anything.
Will my employer pay?
Some employers pay for visas and advisers, some pay part and some leave it to the worker. Ask your employer early what they will cover. Be wary of anyone who asks you to pay for a job itself, and check with a regulated adviser before paying.
