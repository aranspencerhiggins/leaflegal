Motoring offences explained

General information for people and businesses in Brighton. Pick a topic to read more.

Motoring law covers the offences that can be committed by anyone who drives, rides or keeps a vehicle on a road or in a public place. Some are minor and are dealt with by a fixed penalty in the post. Others lead to a court hearing, penalty points, a driving ban and in the most serious cases a prison sentence. Almost all motoring cases in England and Wales start in the magistrates' court, and most finish there too.

For many people the real worry is not the fine. It is the licence. Losing the right to drive can affect a job, a business, caring for a relative or getting children to school. That is why people often look for a solicitor who deals with motoring cases every day, rather than a general high street firm or no help at all.

A specialist motoring solicitor will usually start by reading the paperwork, checking the dates and looking at the evidence the police or prosecutor relies on. They will then explain in plain terms what the likely outcomes are, whether there is anything to challenge, and what the court will be looking at. If you are pleading guilty, they can put forward mitigation, which is information about you and the offence that may help the court reach a fair sentence.

Motoring cases also move quickly and to fixed dates. A notice may give you a short time to reply, and a court date is rarely moved without a good reason. People who get in touch with a solicitor early tend to have more time to gather documents, find witnesses or arrange evidence about how a ban would affect them. Leaving it until the morning of the hearing narrows what anyone can do.

Most motoring offences are endorsable. That means a conviction is recorded on your driving record with an offence code and, for many offences, a number of penalty points. The number of points depends on the offence and sometimes on how serious the court thinks it was. Some offences have a fixed number of points and others have a range.

How points add up

Points are counted under what is known as the totting up system. If you reach 12 or more points from offences committed within three years of each other, the court will usually disqualify you for at least six months. The minimum is longer if you have been banned before in the recent past. Points stay on your record for four years for most offences, and for eleven years for drink and drug driving related offences, even though only some of them count towards totting up.

Different kinds of ban

Before passing sentence, the court will look at your driving record. Points that are still live for totting up purposes are taken into account, so an offence that would normally mean three points can have a much bigger effect if you already have nine. This is one reason people often check their record before deciding how to respond to a notice.

A ban can happen in more than one way. Some offences, such as drink driving, carry an obligatory ban, so the court must disqualify unless there are special reasons not to. For other offences the court has a discretion to ban instead of adding points, often where the offence was serious, such as a very high speed. And then there is the totting up ban, which comes from the build up of points over time rather than from one offence on its own.

New drivers

Drivers in their first two years after passing their test have a lower limit. If they reach six points in that period, the DVLA will usually revoke their licence and they will need to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again. This happens automatically and is separate from the court's own powers.

Extended retests

For some of the most serious offences, such as dangerous driving, the court must order an extended retest. The driver cannot simply get their licence back at the end of the ban. They need to pass a longer, harder test first.

Motoring cases follow the criminal court process in England and Wales. The details vary with the offence, but most cases go through the same broad stages. A solicitor will tell you where your own case is and what comes next.

Notices and paperwork

For many offences caught on camera, the first thing you receive is a notice of intended prosecution and a request to name the driver. For offences where you were stopped, you may have been told at the roadside or charged at a police station. Cases then usually reach court by a postal requisition, a written charge with a Single Justice Procedure notice, or a charge sheet with a date to attend.

Single Justice Procedure

Many lower level motoring cases are dealt with by a single magistrate on the papers, without a hearing in open court. You are sent a notice and asked to respond within a set time, pleading guilty or not guilty and giving details of your finances. If you plead not guilty, or if a ban is being considered and you want to be heard, the case can be moved to a magistrates' court hearing.

First hearing

At the first hearing in the magistrates' court you are asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you there and then, or adjourn for reports or for a further hearing. If you plead not guilty, the court sets a trial date and gives directions about evidence.

Most hearings take place at the magistrates' court for the area where the offence happened, which may be some way from where you live. Hearings are usually listed for the morning or afternoon, and you may have to wait several hours to be called. Your solicitor can tell you what to expect on the day, where to go and what to bring.

Trial and sentence

At a trial, the prosecution has to prove the offence. Witnesses may give evidence and be cross examined. If you are found guilty, or have pleaded guilty, the court passes sentence using guidelines published by the Sentencing Council. A few serious offences, such as dangerous driving, can be sent to the Crown Court.

Appeals

After a magistrates' court decision there is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court, within a strict time limit. A solicitor can explain whether an appeal is possible and what it involves.

Motoring law is a specialist area. The rules on notices, time limits, evidence and sentencing are technical, and local courts each have their own way of doing things. Many people prefer a firm that handles motoring work as a main part of its practice.

Solicitors and barristers

It also helps to think about what you want from the firm. Some people want a quick review and a view on the likely outcome. Others want a solicitor to handle everything, from writing to the prosecutor to standing up in court. Being clear about this from the start makes it easier for the firm to give you an accurate fee.

Your solicitor may attend court themselves or instruct a barrister to represent you. Both are regulated. If a barrister is used, ask whether their fee is included in the price you have been given.

Regulation

Every firm we introduce is regulated, usually by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Regulated firms must be clear about costs, have a complaints procedure and hold insurance. You can check a firm on the regulator's online register.

Leaflegal introduces you to one firm, so you are not passed between lots of different companies. You are free to decide whether to instruct them.

Legal aid is rarely available for motoring cases, because it is usually only granted where there is a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately for help with a motoring matter.

How firms usually charge

Many motoring firms charge fixed fees for each stage, such as an initial review, a guilty plea hearing, an exceptional hardship hearing or a trial. Others charge by the hour for complex cases. Trials cost more than guilty pleas because of the preparation and court time involved. Some cases also need expert evidence, such as a report on a speed device or a blood analysis, which is usually an extra cost.

Court costs and fines

If you are convicted, the court will usually order a fine, a victim surcharge and a contribution to the prosecution's costs. Fines for many offences are linked to your income. These amounts are set by the court and are separate from your solicitor's fees.

When you compare quotes, look at what is included rather than just the headline number. A low fee for a guilty plea may not include travel to a distant court, a barrister's fee or work on a separate exceptional hardship argument. A good firm will confirm the fee and what it covers in writing before any work starts.

If you are acquitted

If you are found not guilty, you may be able to recover some of your legal costs from public funds. The amount that can be recovered is usually limited and is often less than what you paid, so ask the firm how this works before you start.

Motoring cases move to fixed deadlines, so having the right information ready when the solicitor calls helps them give you a clear picture quickly.

  • Any notice, letter, charge sheet or court papers you have received, with every page
  • The date of the offence and the date of any court hearing
  • Your current points, which you can check on the GOV.UK driving licence service
  • Details of any previous bans
  • How you use your car, for work, family or health reasons
  • Any dashcam footage, photos or names of witnesses
  • Details of prescribed medicines, if relevant

If you have a response deadline on a notice, tell the firm straight away. Missing a deadline can create a separate offence or lead to a case being decided without you.

Try to be open with the solicitor about what happened, even the parts you are unsure of. What you tell them is confidential, and they can only help if they have the full picture.

If you think a ban would cause problems for other people, such as an employer, a relative you care for or your own staff, it can help to note down who they are and how they depend on your driving. Your solicitor can then explain whether that information is relevant and what kind of evidence the court usually expects to see.

Is there legal aid for motoring offences?
Usually not, unless you are at risk of prison. We only introduce people looking for paid legal help.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm aims to contact you quickly, especially if you have a court date coming up. Please give a mobile number you can answer.
Will I have to retake my test?
The court can order you to take an extended driving test if it disqualifies you, although this is not automatic for careless driving. New drivers whose licence is revoked under the new driver rules must also retake the standard tests. A solicitor can explain whether a retest is a realistic possibility in your case.
Can I get legal aid?
Advice at the police station is free for everyone. For court, legal aid depends on a means test and on whether the interests of justice require it. Careless driving rarely carries a risk of prison, so legal aid is often refused. Most people instructing a solicitor for careless driving pay privately. The firm can explain what funding might be available to you.
How do solicitors charge for careless driving cases?
Many firms offer fixed fees for separate stages, such as advising before plea, representing you on a guilty plea, preparing an exceptional hardship application or conducting a trial. Trials cost more because of the preparation, witness work and time at court. Ask for a written breakdown of what is included before you agree to anything.
Will I have to pay prosecution costs?
If you are convicted, the court will usually order you to contribute to the prosecution's costs and to pay a victim surcharge as well as any fine. The amounts vary from case to case. If you are acquitted, you will not pay prosecution costs, and you may be able to claim back some of your own legal costs.
Does my motor insurance cover legal costs?
Some motor policies include legal expenses cover that may help with defending a motoring prosecution, though many exclude it or limit it. Check your policy documents and any separate legal expenses policy. If you have cover, the insurer may have its own panel of firms, so read the terms carefully.
Should I answer the police questions in writing?
Anything you say can be used as evidence. Some drivers answer written questions or attend a voluntary interview without advice and later wish they had spoken to a solicitor first. Leaflegal cannot tell you what to do. The firm we introduce you to can look at the questions and explain the possible consequences of how you respond.
What should I keep after an accident?
Keep any dashcam footage somewhere safe, along with photographs, the names of witnesses, the other driver's details and any letters from the police or insurers. Write down what happened while you still remember it clearly. Note the weather, road layout and lighting. Your solicitor will tell you what is useful.
Will the solicitor speak to my insurer?
Your insurer deals with the civil claim for the damage, while the solicitor deals with the criminal case. The two are separate, but what happens in one can affect the other. Tell your solicitor about any insurance claim and tell your insurer about the prosecution. Your solicitor can explain how the two interact.
What will the solicitor ask me?
Expect questions about the incident, the route, the conditions, your vehicle, any passengers and anything you said at the scene. They will ask about your driving record, how many points you have and how much you rely on driving. Having your court papers and any police letters to hand will help.
How long will the conviction affect my licence?
A dangerous driving conviction usually stays on your driving record for four years from the date of conviction. You will need to declare it to insurers, who may ask about convictions for longer than that. Premiums after a dangerous driving conviction are often much higher, and some insurers may decline cover. A specialist broker may be able to help once you are able to drive again.
Can I get legal aid?
Possibly. Police station advice is free for everyone. For court, legal aid depends on a means test and an interests of justice test. Because dangerous driving can lead to prison, the interests of justice test is often met. In the Crown Court you may have to pay a contribution depending on your finances. The firm can check your eligibility.
How much does a private defence cost?
It depends on the court, whether you plead guilty and whether experts and a barrister are needed. Many firms quote by stage so you know the cost of each part. A contested Crown Court trial is significantly more expensive than a magistrates' court plea. Ask for a written estimate and what would change it.
Who pays for expert reports?
If you are legally aided, expert fees can often be covered with the approval of the Legal Aid Agency. If you are paying privately, expert fees are usually an extra cost on top of the solicitor's fee. Your solicitor will explain why an expert is needed and what it is likely to involve before instructing one.
Can I get my costs back if I win?
If you are acquitted after paying privately, you may be able to recover some of your legal costs from public funds through a defendant's costs order. The amount is usually limited to legal aid rates, so it may not cover everything you paid. Your solicitor can explain what you might be able to claim.
What should I gather for my solicitor?
Bring your charge sheet or requisition, any bail papers, letters from the police and any dashcam footage or photographs you have. Write down your own account while it is fresh, including the route, speed, weather and anyone you remember seeing. Your solicitor will tell you what else may be useful.
Should I post about the incident online?
Anything you post can be found and used as evidence. People sometimes comment on social media without realising it may later be put to them in court. Leaflegal cannot advise you, but a solicitor can explain the risks of discussing the case with anyone other than your legal team.
Will my employer find out?
The police do not usually contact your employer, but court hearings are public and a ban may affect your ability to work. Some jobs require you to report a charge or conviction. Your solicitor can help you think about how the possible outcomes affect your work and what evidence about your job may help at sentence.
Is Leaflegal a law firm?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service. We do not give legal advice and we are not a law firm. We introduce you to one regulated firm that handles serious motoring cases, and any advice comes from them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.
Will a section 172 conviction affect my insurance?
Insurers usually ask about motoring convictions, and an MS90 conviction can affect premiums noticeably, sometimes more than a speeding conviction. You must answer insurers' questions honestly. A specialist broker may be able to help if you find cover difficult to obtain.
Will I also be prosecuted for the original offence?
If the driver has not been identified, the police usually cannot prosecute anyone for the original offence, such as speeding, because they do not know who to charge. That is why the section 172 offence carries more points than many original offences. If you are the driver and have named yourself, the original offence is dealt with in the usual way.
Does a company have to name the driver?
Yes. A company that is the registered keeper must identify the driver just as an individual must. The company should have a system so that notices reach the right person quickly and are answered within the deadline. A company that fails to respond can be prosecuted and fined.
Can a director be personally liable?
In some circumstances, yes. If a company commits the offence with the consent or connivance of a director or manager, or because of their neglect, that person can also be guilty. A solicitor can explain how this applies to your role and your business's procedures.
What records should a business keep?
Records showing who used each vehicle and when make it much easier to answer notices accurately. Examples include vehicle logs, key sign out sheets, telematics data and fuel card records. A solicitor can review your current approach and explain how it might be viewed if a defence of reasonable diligence were ever needed.
We lease our vans. Who receives the notice?
The lease company is often the registered keeper, but it usually passes the notice on to the business that leases the vehicle by providing details of the agreement to the police. Your business then has to answer it. Make sure the lease company has your correct address and that notices are dealt with promptly.
When is the best time to speak to a solicitor?
Many people find that advice is most useful before the 28 day deadline passes, while there is still time to respond properly. However, a solicitor can also help after a court notice has arrived or after a conviction you did not know about. The firm will look at your timeline and explain what can be done.
What should I have ready?
Gather the notice and any envelope, the dates you received or saw it, any response you sent and proof of posting or online confirmation. Include details of everyone who had access to the vehicle and what you have done to find out who was driving. Any court papers are important too.
Is Leaflegal a law firm?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service and does not give legal advice. We pass your enquiry to one regulated law firm that handles motoring cases. Any advice comes from that firm. You are free to decide whether to instruct them after speaking to them.
What does driving without insurance cover?
It covers using a vehicle on a road or other public place without a policy that covers that driver, that vehicle and that type of use. It also covers causing or permitting someone else to do so. So an owner who lends their car to someone who is not insured can face the same charge as the driver.
I thought I was insured. Is that a defence?
Usually not on its own, because the offence is one of strict liability. However, a genuine and reasonable belief that you were insured may support an argument for special reasons, which can allow the court not to give points. If you were an employee driving a work vehicle, a specific defence may apply. A solicitor will look at the facts.
Who has to prove I was insured?
In most cases it is for the driver to prove that there was a valid policy covering that use. That is why your policy documents, certificate and schedule are so important. If you say you were insured, gather everything you can and give it to your solicitor as early as possible.
What is the employee defence?
It is a statutory defence for some employees. It can apply where the vehicle did not belong to you and was not hired or lent to you, you were driving it in the course of your employment, and you did not know and had no reason to believe it was uninsured. Each part must be shown, and a solicitor can check whether your situation fits.
Can the vehicle owner be prosecuted too?
Yes. A person who causes or permits someone else to use a vehicle without insurance can be prosecuted, and the penalties are the same as for the driver. This often arises where a car is lent to a friend or family member, or where an employer allows staff to drive a vehicle that is not properly insured.
How long after the incident can I be prosecuted?
Insurance offences have different time limits from many other minor motoring offences. This can allow a prosecution to start some time after the incident, particularly where it took a while for the authorities to obtain the evidence. A solicitor can check whether your case was brought within the time allowed.
Does it matter that I was only driving a short distance?
The offence can be committed over any distance, even moving a car a few metres on a public road. However, in some cases the distance driven and the reason for driving, such as a genuine emergency, may be relevant to whether special reasons exist or to the penalty. A solicitor can explain how courts tend to view the circumstances in your case.
Does my policy let me drive other cars?
Some policies include driving other cars cover, but many do not, and where it is included it is often limited to third party cover for certain drivers. It may not apply to cars you own or that are registered to you. Check your certificate and policy wording. A solicitor can look at the exact terms if you have been charged.
My insurer cancelled my policy without telling me. What then?
Insurers usually have to follow the cancellation terms in the policy, including giving notice. Whether you were insured on the day depends on the dates and what the insurer did. Ask the insurer for copies of any cancellation letters and when they were sent. A solicitor can review these and explain how they affect your case.
Can I keep driving before my court date?
In most drink driving cases, yes. The ban normally starts on the day the court imposes it, not on the day you were stopped or charged. If you were bailed with conditions, check whether any of them affect driving. Many people use the time before the hearing to arrange other ways of getting to work and meeting family commitments.
How do I get my licence back?
When the ban ends, you usually need to apply to the DVLA for your licence to be returned. If you are classed as a high risk offender, you will need to pass a medical assessment arranged through the DVLA before the licence is returned. The DVLA sends information about this towards the end of the ban.
What is a high risk offender?
It is a category used by the DVLA for some drink driving offenders. It usually covers people with a reading of two and a half times the limit or more, people convicted of failing to provide a specimen, and people with two drink driving convictions within ten years. They must pass a medical assessment before their licence is returned.
Will my insurance go up after the ban?
Almost certainly. Insurers treat drink driving convictions seriously, and many will ask about them for several years. Some insurers decline cover, and specialist insurers may charge much more. Being honest with insurers when they ask is important, because failing to disclose a conviction can cause problems if you make a claim.
Can I drive abroad while banned in the UK?
A UK driving ban means you hold no valid licence during the ban, and an International Driving Permit relies on a valid licence. Different countries also have their own rules about recognising foreign bans. It is not something to assume. Check with the authorities of the country concerned and speak to a solicitor if you need to know more.
Can I appeal a drink driving ban?
There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court against conviction, sentence or both, within 21 days of sentence. In some cases you can apply for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is decided. Appeals need to have proper grounds. A solicitor can look at what happened and tell you whether an appeal is realistic.
Will my employer find out?
The court does not usually contact your employer. However, if you drive for work, your employer may check your licence or ask you to declare convictions, and some contracts require you to tell them. Certain professions also have reporting duties to a regulator. Your contract, staff handbook or professional body can tell you what applies to you.
How much does a drink driving solicitor cost?
Many firms offer a fixed fee for a guilty plea hearing, and a higher fee or hourly rate for a special reasons hearing or a trial. Expert evidence is usually extra. Fees vary between firms and areas. The firm will explain what is included and confirm it in writing before you agree to anything.
Is legal aid available?
Legal aid is not usually available for drink driving, because it depends mainly on whether there is a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately. If your case is serious enough that legal aid might be available, the solicitor can tell you and explain how to apply.
