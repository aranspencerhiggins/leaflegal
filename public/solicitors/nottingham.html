Choosing a solicitor in Nottingham

The right firm for you depends on the kind of matter you have, not just on being close by. A conveyancing firm on the high street in Nottingham may not handle a contested licence review, and a family law specialist will not usually deal with a speeding charge. It is worth asking any firm how it charges before work starts. Many matters are handled for a fixed fee, while others are charged by the hour. A good firm will explain the likely total, what could make it change and what is included, and confirm it in writing.

You can check that a solicitor is regulated on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and that a licensed conveyancer is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. It is natural to want a solicitor who is close by. For some kinds of matter that is useful. For others, the location of the firm makes very little difference to the service you receive.

When local knowledge can help

Some matters involve a particular court, council or local authority. A motoring offence is usually heard at a magistrates' court in the area where the offence happened. A premises licence is applied for to the local council where the premises is, and the council's licensing policy and the views of local police and residents can matter. A family dispute may be heard at the local family court. In these cases a firm that knows the local procedures and people can be an advantage, and attending a hearing is easier if the firm is not far away.

When location matters less

Conveyancing, lease extensions and business debt recovery are now mostly handled by phone, email, post and online portals. Property searches and Land Registry applications are done electronically, county court claims for money are often issued online, and documents can be signed and returned without visiting an office. Many people use a firm in a different town and never meet in person, and are perfectly happy with the service.

Meeting in person

If meeting face to face matters to you, say so when you make your enquiry. Some people find it easier to discuss personal matters, such as a separation, in person. Others are more comfortable with video calls. Many firms offer both, and you can ask what the firm usually does on the first call.

Getting the balance right

In the end, experience in your type of matter, clear communication and fair fees usually matter more than the distance to the office. A nearby firm that rarely handles your kind of matter may not be the best choice, while a firm a few towns away that does it every day may suit you well. The firm we introduce handles your type of work and covers your area.

Area by area

Conveyancing firms routinely act for buyers and sellers all over England and Wales. What matters more is that the firm is on your lender's panel and responds quickly. Local knowledge can help with things like unusual local searches or properties affected by mining or flood risk, but a good conveyancer will know how to deal with those wherever the property is.

Lease extension work is mostly paperwork and negotiation with the freeholder's solicitor, supported by a valuation from a surveyor. The surveyor usually needs to know the local property market, but the solicitor can be based anywhere. If the matter goes to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), hearings are often held remotely or at a regional venue.

Family solicitors often deal with the family court that covers the area where you or your children live. Many hearings now take place by video, but some are in person. Some people prefer a firm they can visit, especially during a difficult separation.

Motoring and licensing cases are the most local. Magistrates' courts, council licensing committees and local police all have their own ways of working. A firm that regularly appears before them can be a real help, and it may be able to attend a hearing at short notice.

Business debt recovery is usually handled through letters, negotiation and, if needed, the county court. Many money claims are issued and managed online, so the firm's location matters less than its experience with commercial debts.

Wales

England and Wales share one legal system, so firms in England can usually act for clients in Wales and the other way round. Some differences do apply in Wales, such as Land Transaction Tax instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax on property purchases, and some housing and planning rules. If you would like to deal with a firm in Welsh, mention it in the notes and the firm will tell you whether it can help.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, so we do not cover matters there for conveyancing, family law, motoring offences, licensing, lease extensions or debt recovery. Immigration introductions are available across the whole of the UK.

Whether a firm is round the corner or at the other end of the country, it is worth checking it before you instruct it. This is easy to do and takes a few minutes.

Check the register

Solicitors and law firms in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can search for them on the SRA register. Licensed conveyancers are regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers, which has its own register. Regulated immigration advisers can be checked with their regulator, such as the SRA or the Immigration Advice Authority.

Look for the regulatory information

Regulated law firms must show certain information on their websites, including who regulates them and their registration number. Many firms display the SRA's digital badge, which links to the firm's entry on the register. If a firm's website does not say who regulates it, ask.

Check the people, not just the firm

Ask who will handle your matter day to day. That might be a solicitor, a chartered legal executive, a licensed conveyancer or a paralegal working under supervision. All of these can be appropriate. It is reasonable to ask about their experience and who supervises them.

Read the paperwork

Before any work starts, the firm should send a client care letter and terms of business. These set out the fees, who will do the work, how complaints are handled and, if you were introduced by Leaflegal, the fee the firm pays for the introduction. Read them carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

Every firm we introduce is regulated

Warning signs

Be cautious if anyone offering legal help will not say who regulates them, asks you to pay large sums before explaining their fees in writing, promises a particular result or pressures you to decide on the spot. Be especially careful with emails asking you to send money for a property purchase to new bank details. Always check bank details by phone using a number you already know before sending money to a firm.

Does a local firm cost more or less

Legal fees vary between firms, and location is only one factor. Firms in some large cities may have higher overheads, but that does not always mean higher prices for a particular piece of work. Fixed fee work, such as many house purchases, can be priced similarly across the country. The best way to understand the cost is to ask the firm for a written estimate or quote.

Travel and attendance

Where a solicitor needs to attend a court or hearing, the cost of travel and waiting time may be included in a fixed fee or charged separately. A local firm may charge less for travel to a local court. Ask how attendance is charged so you can compare like for like.

Disbursements

On top of the firm's own fees there may be disbursements, such as court fees, search fees, Land Registry fees, licensing application fees set by the council, or a barrister's fee. Official fees change from time to time, and GOV.UK lists many of them. The firm will tell you which apply.

Comparing quotes

If you speak to more than one firm, check that each quote covers the same work and includes the same items. A lower headline fee may leave out things that another firm includes. Ask each firm to confirm what is and is not included.

How does a statutory lease extension work?
Under the long standing rules, a qualifying flat owner can serve a formal notice on the freeholder asking for a new lease. The extension usually adds 90 years to the remaining term and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn, in return for a premium. A surveyor values the premium and solicitors deal with the paperwork. The 2024 Act is set to change some of these rules.
Can the seller extend the lease before I buy?
Sometimes. The seller can start a statutory claim by serving notice and then assign the benefit of that notice to you on completion, so the extension carries on in your name. Alternatively the seller can finish the extension before the sale. Your conveyancer will explain how this would affect the timetable and costs.
Do I have to wait before extending the lease myself?
In the past, a new owner usually had to own the flat for two years before making a statutory claim. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 has changed this and other eligibility rules. Your conveyancer will explain the current position and whether it makes a difference to your plans.
Does a short lease mean I should not buy?
Not necessarily. Some buyers choose a flat with a shorter lease because the price reflects it, and then extend the lease. Others prefer to avoid the extra cost and uncertainty. It depends on your lender, your budget and your plans. Your conveyancer can explain the legal position, and a specialist surveyor can estimate what an extension might cost, but the decision is yours.
Why will my conveyancer ask about cladding and fire safety?
Since the Grenfell Tower fire, lenders and buyers want to know about fire safety in blocks of flats. Your conveyancer will ask the seller and managing agent about the external walls, any remediation works and any certificates. The answers can affect whether a lender will lend and whether costs could fall on leaseholders.
What protection does the Building Safety Act give leaseholders?
The Building Safety Act 2022 limits or removes what some leaseholders can be charged for fixing certain historical building safety defects. Whether a lease qualifies depends on things like the height of the building and how many properties the leaseholder owns. Your conveyancer will check what information is available and explain how the rules may apply.
What is a leaseholder deed of certificate?
It is a document the seller completes to show whether their lease qualifies for protection under the Building Safety Act. The freeholder may also provide a landlord's certificate. These help show who is responsible for paying for certain safety works. Your conveyancer will ask for them where they apply.
What is the difference between the conditional order and the final order?
The conditional order is the middle stage. It confirms the court sees no reason why you cannot divorce, and it allows a financial order to be approved. The final order is the document that legally ends the marriage. You remain married until the final order is made. Keep both documents safe.
Should I wait to apply for the final order?
Many people do, but it depends on your circumstances. Solicitors often discuss delaying the final order until a financial order is approved, to protect pension and other rights that depend on being married. Your solicitor will explain whether this matters in your case. You should not feel rushed, but very long delays can cause their own complications, so discuss the timing with your solicitor.
What happens if neither of us applies for the final order?
You stay married. If more than 12 months pass after the conditional order, the applicant will usually need to explain the delay when they apply. If the applicant does not apply, the respondent can usually apply after a further three months have passed. Some people discover years later that they were never finally divorced. A solicitor can help complete the process.
Can we stop the divorce if we get back together?
Yes. You can withdraw the application at any point before the final order is made. If you are unsure, your solicitor can explain how to pause or end the process. Some couples choose to pause the process while they try to reconcile, rather than withdrawing it straight away. Counselling services can help couples who want to try again.
When can I remarry?
Only once the final order has been made. Remarrying before resolving your finances can also limit your own ability to make financial claims later, so many people take advice before setting a date. If you are planning a wedding, allow enough time for the final order to be made, as court processing times can vary.
Can the 20 week period be shortened?
Only in very limited circumstances. The court can shorten the 20 week period if the applicant shows a good reason, but this is rare and is decided case by case. In most divorces the full 20 weeks applies. A solicitor can explain whether there is any realistic basis for asking.
Does the divorce decide who gets the house?
No. The divorce ends the marriage but does not divide property. That is dealt with through a financial agreement or financial court proceedings. Until then, ownership stays as it is on the title deeds, whatever happens with the divorce. If you do not own the home but live there, you may be able to protect your right to stay. A solicitor can explain how.
I am a new driver. Will I lose my licence?
If you are within two years of passing your test, six or more points will lead the DVLA to revoke your licence. As an IN10 carries at least six points, a conviction will usually have that effect unless the court disqualifies you instead or finds special reasons not to endorse. You would then need to retake your theory and practical tests.
Can I appeal?
You can appeal to the Crown Court against conviction or sentence. Time limits are short, usually 21 days from sentence. If you were convicted without knowing about the case, there may be other ways to reopen it. A solicitor can explain your options and the risks involved.
Could I be banned instead of getting points?
Yes. The court can choose to disqualify you instead of endorsing your licence with points. This is more likely where the offence is serious, for example where a vehicle was used without insurance for a long time or there was an accident. Some drivers who would otherwise face a totting up ban or revocation ask the court to consider a short ban instead, and the court decides whether that is appropriate.
The police seized my car. How do I get it back?
You will usually need to show that you are the owner or keeper, show valid insurance for the vehicle and pay the release and storage charges. The police or storage provider can tell you the current charges and deadline. If the vehicle is not claimed in time it may be disposed of, so act quickly.
Can I get the seizure charges back?
Usually only in limited circumstances, such as where the seizure was not lawful. Even if you later show you had a defence to the charge, recovering seizure costs is not straightforward. A solicitor can look at how the seizure happened and explain whether there is any basis for challenging it.
What if I had an accident while uninsured?
The victim may be compensated by the Motor Insurers' Bureau, which may then seek to recover the money from you personally. This is separate from the criminal case. Tell your solicitor about any accident, as it affects how serious the court considers the offence to be and may raise other issues.
Will a conviction affect my future insurance?
Yes. Insurers treat no insurance convictions seriously and premiums often rise substantially for several years. Some insurers may decline to offer cover. You must declare convictions when asked. Specialist brokers may be able to help find cover after a conviction.
What is a shadow licence?
A shadow licence is a second premises licence for the same building, usually held by the landlord or pub company. It protects their interest if the tenant's licence is lost. A buyer or tenant should know whether a shadow licence exists, as it can affect their position. A solicitor can explain how it works in your deal.
Should the licence match how the business trades?
Yes. If a seller has been trading later than the licence allows, using areas not on the plan or ignoring conditions, a buyer may be taking on a business that cannot lawfully trade in the same way. Finding this out before completion gives the buyer the chance to negotiate or ask for the problem to be fixed.
What happens if the licence holder dies?
The licence usually lapses on the death of the holder. In certain cases, someone connected to them or with an interest in the premises can give an interim authority notice within a short window, which keeps the licence alive for a limited period while a transfer is arranged. Time limits are strict, so act quickly.
What happens if the licence holder becomes insolvent?
A licence held by an individual who becomes bankrupt, or a company that goes into certain insolvency processes, can lapse. Insolvency practitioners and landlords often need to act quickly, using an interim authority notice or a transfer, to protect the licence. A solicitor can explain the options and deadlines.
Can a lapsed licence be saved?
In some cases, yes, if an interim authority notice or a reinstatement transfer is made within the strict time limits. After those limits pass, the licence is usually lost and a new application is needed. If you think a licence may have lapsed, speak to a licensing solicitor straight away.
Do I need to tell the council if my address changes?
Yes. A premises licence holder must notify the council of a change of name or address, and a DPS must notify changes to their own details. There is usually a small fee. Keeping these details up to date avoids problems with notices and correspondence going missing.
What if a licence is surrendered by mistake?
Sometimes a tenant surrenders a licence when leaving, not realising the landlord or a new tenant needs it. A transfer with reinstatement may be possible within a short period after the surrender, if the right person applies. Time limits are strict, so speak to a licensing solicitor immediately if this happens.
How long does the debtor have to respond?
It depends on who the debtor is. For a company or other organisation, a reasonable period is expected, often around 14 days in a straightforward debt. Where the debtor is an individual, including a sole trader, the debt protocol usually gives them 30 days. Your solicitor will set the right deadline.
What if the debtor asks for more time?
A creditor can decide whether to agree. If the debtor is an individual who says they are getting debt advice, the protocol expects the creditor to allow a reasonable extra period. For companies, a short extension can sometimes lead to payment. Your solicitor can advise on how to respond in your case.
Can I issue a claim before the deadline ends?
Generally not, unless there is a good reason such as a limitation deadline or a real risk that assets will disappear. Issuing early can undermine the purpose of the letter and may affect costs. If time is short, tell your solicitor so they can plan accordingly.
Does sending a letter stop the limitation clock?
No. Only issuing a claim at court stops time running for limitation purposes. A written acknowledgement of the debt by the debtor, or a part payment, can restart the clock. If your debt is close to six years old, tell your solicitor straight away.
Is the deadline counted in working days or calendar days?
Letters before action usually give a period in days and a specific date, so there is no confusion. Your solicitor will choose a date that allows the period expected by the rules, taking into account how and when the letter will be delivered. A clear date in the letter avoids arguments about when the deadline ended.
What happens if the debtor pays?
If the debtor pays everything claimed, including any interest and compensation, the matter ends there. If they pay only the invoice amount, you can decide whether to pursue the rest. Your solicitor will confirm receipt and close the file or discuss the balance with you.
What if the debtor disputes the debt?
Your solicitor will review the reasons given. Some disputes are genuine and need to be resolved, perhaps through negotiation. Others are not supported by the evidence. The solicitor will explain how the dispute affects your options, including whether a court claim is still the right next step.
What is a section 13 notice?
It is the formal initial notice that starts a collective enfranchisement claim. It identifies the building and any extra land, names the participating owners, proposes a price and gives the freeholder a date to reply that is at least two months away. It has to be accurate and served correctly, so it is normally prepared by a solicitor.
What can the freeholder do after receiving our notice?
The freeholder can ask for information about the participants, require a deposit and arrange access so its surveyor can value the building. It then serves a counter notice accepting the claim, disputing it, or proposing a different price and terms. In some cases it can ask to lease back certain parts, such as commercial units.
What if we cannot agree the price?
Either side can apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to decide the terms. The application must be made within a fixed period after the counter notice. Many claims still settle before a hearing, but the application keeps the claim alive while negotiations continue.
How long does it take?
Organising the group and the funding can take several months before any notice is served. After that, if the price is agreed, completion often follows within several months. A contested claim that goes to the tribunal can take a year or more. Your solicitor will give you an estimate once they understand your building.
What if our freeholder cannot be found?
Where a freeholder cannot be traced, there is a separate court process that can allow the owners to buy the freehold without the freeholder's involvement. The price is usually set by the tribunal and paid into court. It takes longer, but it can resolve a building that has been left without a landlord.
What is a leaseback?
In some cases the freeholder can require the new owners to grant it a lease of certain parts, such as commercial units or flats not held by qualifying tenants. This can reduce the price. The 2024 Act also introduces new rules allowing leaseholders to require leasebacks, as part of the reforms being brought in over time.
Can we withdraw once the notice is served?
The group can usually withdraw, but under the long standing rules it may have to pay certain costs the freeholder has incurred up to that point, and there is normally a waiting period before a new notice can be served. The participation agreement should say how such costs would be shared. Your solicitor will explain the consequences before any decision is taken.
Can we use an adviser in a different part of the UK?
Yes. Immigration rules apply across the whole of the UK, and many advisers work remotely. You do not need to choose a firm near where you live, although some couples like to meet in person. Ask the firm how it usually works. What matters most is that the adviser is regulated, experienced in partner matters and easy for you both to talk to.
What if we are unhappy with the service?
Regulated firms must have a complaints procedure, and you can ask for a copy at the start. If a complaint is not resolved, you may be able to take it further, for example to the Legal Ombudsman or to the adviser's regulator. The firm should explain how this works.
Can the adviser help if one of us does not speak English well?
Some firms have advisers who speak other languages, and some can arrange interpreters. It is important that both partners understand what is being explained and what is being signed. Mention any language needs when you enquire, and ask the firm what it can offer.
How much does a partner visa cost?
There is usually an application fee and, for most applicants, the Immigration Health Surcharge, plus costs such as translations and travel to appointments. These amounts change from time to time and can add up to a significant sum. Check GOV.UK for current figures or ask your adviser for a breakdown. If children are applying as well, the total can be considerably higher.
How much do advisers charge?
Many advisers offer a fixed fee for a partner application, which may vary depending on where you apply from and how much work is involved. Ask for the fee in writing, and check what is covered and whether government fees are included, before you agree to anything.
Can we pay in stages?
Some firms allow fees to be paid in stages, and others ask for payment upfront. Government fees are usually paid when the application is made. Ask the firm what payment arrangements it offers before you commit, so you can plan your budget. Remember that renewals and later applications will usually have their own fees too.
