Trademarks explained

General information for people and businesses in London. Pick a topic to read more.

A trademark is the sign customers use to recognise your business. It is usually a name or a logo, but it can also be a slogan, a product name, a shape, a colour combination or even a sound. In the UK, trademarks are registered at the UK Intellectual Property Office, often called the UK IPO, and one registration covers England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Registering a trademark gives you the exclusive right to use it for the goods and services listed in the registration. That right makes it much easier to stop copycats, to take down lookalike listings online and to license or sell your brand later. Without a registration you may still have some protection through the law of passing off, but it is harder and usually more expensive to rely on.

Trademarks sit alongside other kinds of intellectual property. Copyright protects original creative work such as writing, photographs, artwork, music and software. Design rights protect the look of a product. Many businesses need help with more than one of these, which is why the firms we introduce handle copyright and designs as well as trademarks.

Who does trademark work

Trademark work is not a reserved legal activity, so in principle anyone can file an application. In practice most businesses use one of two kinds of regulated professional. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and can handle filings, disputes and court claims. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg, the regulator for patent and trade mark attorneys, and specialise in searching, filing and UK IPO proceedings such as oppositions.

Leaflegal only introduces trademark enquiries to firms regulated by one of those two bodies. Both sorts of firm work by phone, email and video, so the firm does not need to be near you. Because there is one UK register, we can introduce you wherever you are in the UK.

There is rarely only one way to deal with a brand question. The right route depends on what you sell, where you trade, how long you have used the name and how much risk you are prepared to accept. The firm will look at your own situation, but it helps to know the usual options before the first call.

Protecting a brand you already use

If you have been trading under a name for a while without registering it, the usual first step is a clearance search to see whether anyone has earlier rights, followed by an application for the classes that match what you sell. Using a name for years does not stop someone else registering something similar, so many owners file as soon as they know the brand is staying.

Launching something new

Before you commit to packaging, signage or a website, a search can tell you whether the name is likely to be free to use and register. If the search shows a problem, it is far cheaper to change the name at that stage. Some businesses file an application before launch so that the filing date is secured early.

When someone else is using a similar brand

Options range from a polite letter to a formal cease and desist letter, a takedown request to an online marketplace, an opposition at the UK IPO or a court claim. Many disputes settle without a hearing, sometimes through a coexistence agreement in which both sides agree how they will use their marks.

When you are on the receiving end

If you receive a letter, an opposition or a takedown notice, the firm can tell you how strong the other side's case looks and what your options are. Those may include changing the name, narrowing what you sell under it, negotiating a timetable to rebrand or defending your position.

Most trademark firms are small specialist practices or IP teams within larger law firms. Some focus on filings and portfolios, while others spend more time on disputes. When the firm contacts you, it is worth asking a few questions to check they are a good fit.

  • Are you a solicitor or a chartered trade mark attorney, and who regulates you?
  • How much of your work is UK trademark filing and how much is disputes?
  • Do you work with businesses of my size and in my sector?
  • Will you search before filing, and what will the search cover?
  • How do you charge, and what is included in a fixed fee?
  • Who will actually do the work and how will you keep me updated?

You can check a solicitor on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register and a chartered trade mark attorney on the IPReg register. Both registers are free to search online.

Solicitor or attorney

For a straightforward filing, a chartered trade mark attorney or a trademark solicitor can both do the job well. If a dispute looks likely to go to court, a solicitor can take the claim forward, and many attorney firms work closely with solicitors for that stage. Tell us in the form if you already know there is a dispute, so the introduction fits.

Firms outside the UK

If you also need protection abroad, ask whether the firm files international applications through the Madrid system and works with local agents in the countries you need. Most UK trademark firms do this as a matter of routine.

Trademark costs come in two parts. The first is the official fee charged by the UK IPO, which depends on how many classes your application covers. The second is the professional fee charged by the firm for its advice and work. Official fees change from time to time, so check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask the firm.

Many firms quote a fixed fee for a clearance search, a fixed fee for preparing and filing a UK application, and a further fixed fee if the examiner raises an objection that needs a reply. Renewals and recording a change of owner are also often fixed fee work.

Disputes are harder to price at the outset because they depend on how the other side responds. Firms usually charge in stages, for example a fee for reviewing the evidence and sending a letter, and a separate estimate for an opposition or court claim. Ask for an estimate for each stage and for an idea of the costs you could have to pay the other side if you lose.

Ways to keep costs down

  • Choose only the classes you really need now
  • Keep dated evidence of when and where you use your brand
  • Reply quickly to the firm's questions and to any official deadlines
  • Consider whether a negotiated settlement could save the cost of a hearing

Most trademark problems are avoidable with a little planning. These are the issues firms see most often, and why they matter.

Choosing a descriptive name

Names that describe the product, its quality or where it comes from are tempting because customers understand them straight away. They are also the names most likely to be refused, and the hardest to enforce against competitors who use similar words. A name with a twist, or an invented word, is usually easier to protect.

Filing too narrowly or too widely

An application that only covers part of what you sell can leave gaps that a competitor can use. An application that claims far more than you will ever sell can attract objections and become vulnerable to a non use challenge after five years. The wording of the goods and services is one of the most important parts of the application.

The wrong owner

Marks are sometimes filed in the name of a director or owner personally, or a company that later stops trading, when the business intends to own the brand. Investors and buyers often check this during due diligence. Putting it right usually means recording an assignment, which is simpler before there is a dispute or a sale.

Ignoring letters and deadlines

Examiner reports, oppositions and renewal reminders all have fixed deadlines. Letters from other brand owners usually have a deadline too. Leaving them until the last minute reduces your options, and missing them can mean losing rights altogether.

Scam invoices

After you file, your details appear on the public register. Businesses often receive official looking letters offering to publish or monitor their mark for a large fee. These are not from the UK IPO. If you are unsure about a letter, ask the firm before you pay anything.

Trademark matters run to official timetables set by the UK IPO, and many of the deadlines are fixed. Knowing roughly how long each stage takes helps you plan launches, investment rounds and marketing.

  • A clearance search usually takes a few days to a couple of weeks, depending on how wide it is
  • The UK IPO usually examines a new application within a few weeks of filing
  • Accepted applications are published for two months, which can be extended to three, so that others can oppose
  • If nobody opposes, registration usually follows around four months after filing
  • An opposition commonly adds many months, and longer if it goes all the way to a decision
  • A registration lasts ten years from the filing date and can be renewed again and again

Disputes outside the UK IPO move at their own pace. A cease and desist letter usually gives the other side a short deadline to reply, often around two weeks. Court claims take much longer, which is one reason many disputes settle early.

Deadlines that matter

The deadlines to watch most closely are the date to reply to an examiner's report, the end of the opposition period for an application you want to challenge, the deadline in any opposition you are defending and the renewal date for each registration. Missing some of these can mean losing rights altogether, so tell the firm about any date on your paperwork.

You don't need to prepare anything before you fill in our form. When the firm contacts you, these things will help them give useful advice quickly.

  • The name, logo or slogan, and a picture of how you use it
  • A short description of what you sell or plan to sell
  • When you started using the brand and where
  • Any trademark application or registration numbers
  • Any letters, notices or emails you have received, with their dates
  • Links or screenshots showing a third party's use, if there is a dispute
  • Where you trade now and where you might trade in future

If you are dealing with copying, keep a record of what you find with the date you found it. Screenshots that show the web address and the date are especially helpful. Avoid contacting the other side in anger before you have spoken to the firm, because what you say can matter later.

Trademarks in London, questions and answers

We introduce you to a firm that covers London. Some have offices nearby, and others work remotely across the UK. The firm will tell you how they work when they contact you.

Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Do I need to search before I apply?
It is not compulsory, but it is usually wise. The UK IPO does not refuse an application just because a similar earlier mark exists. Instead, the owner of that mark may oppose. A search helps you avoid paying for an application that is likely to be challenged, or a brand that you may later have to change.
Can I apply for a name I haven't started using yet?
Yes. You can apply for a mark you intend to use. You need a genuine intention to use it for the goods and services listed, and if you don't use it within five years of registration it becomes vulnerable to being revoked.
Who should own the trademark?
Usually the business that will use the brand, such as your limited company. Some owners file in their own name and license the company to use it, which can have tax or planning reasons. The firm can explain the options. Getting this right early saves work during investment or a sale.
What makes a mark distinctive?
A mark is distinctive if customers would see it as a sign of where the goods come from rather than a description of them. Invented words and words unrelated to the goods are strongest. Descriptive words, laudatory terms like best or premium and common shapes are weakest. The firm can tell you where your mark sits.
Should I file a word mark or a logo first?
If budget allows only one, many firms suggest the word mark because it protects the name whatever the styling. If the name itself is weak but the logo is distinctive, a logo mark may be easier to register. The firm will explain the trade off for your brand.
Can I trademark my own name?
Often yes, if it is distinctive for your goods or services and nobody has earlier rights in the same or a similar name. Common surnames can be harder. Others with the same name may still be able to use it honestly in some circumstances.
How long before launch should I file?
As early as you can once you are confident in the name. Your rights run from the filing date, so filing before launch reduces the risk that someone else files first or starts using something similar. It also gives you time to deal with any objection before your marketing starts.
Is it worth registering if I am a small business?
Many small businesses find it worthwhile, because a brand is often one of their most valuable assets and copying can hurt a small business more than a large one. A registration also makes it much easier to deal with copies on online marketplaces. The firm can help you decide what level of protection makes sense for your budget.
How do I choose the right classes?
Start from what you sell and plan to sell. The firm will map that to the right classes and draft wording that covers your business properly. Many businesses need two or three classes. Some need more. Each extra class adds to the official fee, so it is worth focusing on the most important ones.
Is the free UK IPO search enough?
It is a useful first check and costs nothing. It is good at finding identical marks on the UK register but is less reliable at finding marks that sound or look similar, and it doesn't cover company names, unregistered use or most international marks. For a launch or a filing, most businesses use a professional search as well.
What is a clearance search?
A clearance search checks whether a mark is clear to use and register, by looking for earlier marks and other rights that could cause a problem. It usually covers trademark registers, company names and use in the market. A professional then reviews the results and gives you a view on the risk.
What is the difference between a search and an opinion?
The search gathers the results. The opinion explains what they mean for you, which earlier marks matter, how serious the risk is and what you could do about it. Raw results without an opinion can be hard to interpret, so most businesses ask for both.
Can a search guarantee my brand is safe?
No search can guarantee that nobody will object. Registers change daily, unregistered rights are hard to find and judging similarity involves opinion. A good search greatly reduces the risk and helps you make an informed decision, but it is not a promise.
Does a search cover logos as well as names?
It can, but you need to ask for it. Logo searches use different tools and classification codes, so they are usually a separate part of the search. If your logo is central to the brand, ask the firm to include it.
How far back does a search go?
A register search covers all live marks, including old registrations that are still in force. It may also pick up recently expired marks that could be restored. Unregistered use is harder to date, which is one reason the firm's judgement matters.
Should I search company names too?
Yes, especially in the UK. A company trading under a similar name may have unregistered rights even if it never filed a trademark. Professional searches usually include Companies House and look at whether those companies are actually trading.
Can I search for a brand name on social media and marketplaces myself?
Yes, and it is a sensible early step. Searching the main marketplaces, app stores and social platforms can show whether others trade under a similar name. It won't tell you about legal rights, so it works best alongside a register search and an opinion from a professional.
Why do search results include marks in other classes?
Because goods in different classes can still be similar. Clothing and bags sit in different classes but are often sold together, and so are software and online services. A good search looks across related classes as well as the ones you will file in.
What is an examination report?
It is a letter from the UK IPO explaining why the examiner thinks your application cannot be accepted as filed. It sets out the objection and gives a deadline to respond. Many objections can be overcome by argument, evidence or changes to the list of goods and services, so it is worth getting advice before giving up.
How long do I have to respond to an objection?
The deadline is shown on the report and is usually around two months. Extensions may be possible in some circumstances. If you don't respond in time, the application may be refused. Passing the report to a firm promptly gives them the most time to prepare a good response.
Can I change my mark to overcome an objection?
Only in limited ways. You can't make a significant change to the mark after filing. Sometimes a minor amendment is allowed, but often the answer is to argue the point, narrow the goods or file a fresh application for a stronger version of the mark. The firm will explain the options.
What does it mean if my mark is descriptive?
It means the examiner thinks customers would see the mark as describing the goods or services, for example their type, quality or purpose, rather than as a brand. Descriptive marks are usually refused for the goods they describe. They may be acceptable for other goods where the meaning doesn't apply.
Can evidence of use overcome an objection?
Yes, sometimes. If a mark lacks distinctiveness on its face but customers have come to recognise it as your brand through use, detailed evidence can overcome the objection. It usually needs to show substantial use across the UK over time, so it is not always possible for newer brands.
What if the examiner refuses my application?
You may be able to ask for a hearing, appeal the decision or file a new application for a different version of the mark. The right step depends on the reasons for refusal and how important the mark is to you. The firm will explain the choices and their costs.
Can I talk to the examiner?
Yes, in many cases. Firms often call the examiner to understand the objection and test a possible solution before filing a written response. A short conversation can sometimes resolve a point that would otherwise take several letters.
Does an objection mean my brand is unsafe to use?
Not necessarily. An objection is about whether the mark can be registered. A mark refused as descriptive may still be usable, but you may find it hard to stop others using similar words. The firm can explain what the objection means for your business in practice.
How long do I have to oppose a trademark?
Two months from the date the application is published in the Trade Marks Journal. You can extend this to three months by filing a notice of threatened opposition within the first two months. After that, the chance to oppose is gone and you would need to apply to cancel the mark once it is registered.
What counts as trademark infringement?
Using a sign that is identical or similar to a registered mark, for identical or similar goods or services, in a way likely to cause confusion, without consent. Use must be in the course of trade. For marks with a reputation, use that takes unfair advantage of or harms that reputation can also infringe. Whether a particular use infringes depends on the facts.
Does the other side have to have copied deliberately?
No. Infringement doesn't depend on intention. A business can infringe even if it chose its name innocently without knowing about the earlier mark. Intention can affect the remedies and how a court views the case, but it is not needed for infringement.
What is passing off?
Passing off protects unregistered brands. The owner must show goodwill in the business, a misrepresentation by the other side that leads or is likely to lead customers to think the goods are connected, and damage as a result. It needs more evidence than a registered trademark claim.
Is using a similar logo infringement?
It can be. Logos are compared as a whole, looking at visual, aural and conceptual similarity. A logo that copies the distinctive parts of a registered logo, for similar goods, may infringe even if some details are different. The firm will compare them carefully.
Can I stop someone using my brand for different goods?
Usually only if the goods are similar, or if your mark has a reputation and their use would take unfair advantage of it or harm it. Different goods often mean no infringement, but well known brands can get wider protection.
Is it infringement to mention a competitor's brand?
Not always. Honest comparative advertising, describing compatibility and genuine resale of branded goods can be allowed within limits. Using a competitor's mark in a way that suggests a connection or takes unfair advantage can cross the line. The details matter.
Can a trademark be infringed outside the UK?
A UK registration protects against use in the UK. Use abroad is governed by the law of that country. Online sales targeted at UK customers can count as use in the UK, even if the seller is based overseas.
What is a mark with a reputation?
A mark that is known by a significant part of the relevant public for the goods or services it covers. Such marks can be protected against use on dissimilar goods where that use takes unfair advantage of, or harms, the mark's reputation or distinctive character. Proving reputation needs evidence.
Does selling genuine second hand goods infringe?
Generally no. Once genuine goods have been put on the market in the UK or the European Economic Area by or with the consent of the owner, reselling them is usually allowed. There are exceptions, for example where the goods have been changed or the resale damages the reputation of the mark.
What does cease and desist mean?
It means stop and do not start again. A cease and desist letter asks the recipient to stop a particular activity, such as using a name or copying content, and usually to confirm in writing that they won't do it again.
Is a cease and desist letter legally binding?
No. It is a formal demand, not a court order. The recipient doesn't have to comply, but ignoring a well founded letter can lead to a court claim, and a court may take into account how each side behaved when deciding costs.
Do I need a solicitor to send a cease and desist letter?
You can write your own, but in IP disputes the unjustified threats rules mean a badly worded letter can expose you to a claim. A letter from a solicitor or trademark attorney also tends to be taken more seriously. Many businesses use a professional for this reason.
What is an unjustified threat?
It is a threat to sue someone for infringing a trademark, design or patent where the threat turns out not to be justified. In some circumstances the person threatened can sue for a declaration, an injunction and damages. Certain communications are permitted, and professionals know how to stay within the rules.
Do the threats rules apply to copyright?
The statutory unjustified threats rules apply to trademarks, designs and patents, not copyright. Copyright letters still need care, because exaggerated or inaccurate claims can damage your position and your reputation, but the specific threats regime does not apply.
Can I send a cease and desist letter by email?
Yes. Many letters are sent by email as well as post. The method matters less than the content, the recipient and keeping a record of when it was sent and received.
How long should the deadline be?
It should be reasonable in the circumstances. Around fourteen days is common. A shorter deadline may be justified where harm is ongoing or urgent. Unreasonably short deadlines can look aggressive and may count against the sender later.
What is a letter before claim?
It is a letter sent before court proceedings begin, setting out the claim in detail and inviting a response. Court rules expect parties to exchange information before suing. A cease and desist letter can serve as the first step, and a fuller letter before claim may follow if the dispute continues.
What should my cease and desist letter say?
It should identify your rights, describe the other side's activity, explain why it infringes or causes a problem, set out what you want them to do and give a deadline. It should be accurate and proportionate. The firm will tailor it to the situation.
