Lease extensions explained

General information for people and businesses in London. Pick a topic to read more.

If you own a flat in England or Wales, you almost certainly own it on a lease. A lease is a long agreement that gives you the right to live in and sell your home for a fixed number of years. The building itself, and the land it sits on, is usually owned by someone else, called the freeholder or landlord. When the lease runs out, the flat goes back to the freeholder.

That is why the number of years left on a lease matters so much. A lease that started at 99 years might feel endless when it is new, but after a few decades it becomes a real issue. Buyers, mortgage lenders and surveyors all look closely at the remaining term, and a shorter lease usually means a lower value and a more expensive extension.

Lease extension and enfranchisement law gives many leaseholders legal rights to deal with this. Flat owners can often extend their own lease, and groups of flat owners can sometimes buy the freehold of their building together. Some leaseholders also need help changing terms in a lease that is badly drafted, out of date or putting buyers off.

Freeholds are owned by all sorts of people and organisations. Some belong to individuals or small family companies, some to large investment companies that own thousands of freeholds, and some to councils, housing associations or the flat owners themselves. Day to day management is often handled by a managing agent. Who owns your freehold can affect how quickly things move and how negotiations go, which is something a solicitor will look at early on.

This is a specialist area. The rules are detailed, the deadlines are strict and the valuation side needs a surveyor who knows leasehold work. A notice served on the wrong person or with the wrong information can cost time and money. That is why most people use a solicitor who handles this kind of work regularly.

The law is changing

The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 makes major changes to leasehold law, but it is being brought into force in stages. Some parts already apply, while others need further regulations before they take effect. This means the right process, the length of the new lease and the way the price is worked out can depend on when you start. A solicitor will confirm which rules apply to you at the time you instruct them.

There are several ways to deal with a lease that is getting shorter or a lease that is causing problems. Which one suits you depends on your building, your freeholder, your plans and how the law applies at the time. These are the main routes a solicitor is likely to talk through with you.

A statutory lease extension

Many flat owners have a legal right to a new lease under the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993. Under the long standing rules, this adds 90 years to the remaining term and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn, which in practice means nothing. The freeholder cannot refuse if you qualify, and disputes about the price can go to a tribunal. The 2024 Act will change the length of a statutory extension and the way the premium is calculated, and these changes are being brought in over time. Find out more on our statutory lease extension page.

An informal lease extension

Some freeholders are willing to agree an extension outside the statutory process. This can be quicker and sometimes cheaper in fees, but the terms are whatever the two sides agree. Informal offers sometimes include a ground rent or other terms that a buyer or lender later dislikes, so it is common to have a solicitor review any offer before accepting it.

Buying the freehold together

Where enough flat owners in a building want to act together, they may have a right to buy the freehold. This is called collective enfranchisement. Once they own the freehold, they can grant themselves long leases and control how the building is run.

Changing the lease

Sometimes the problem is not the length of the lease but what it says. A lease might have a missing clause, a wrong plan, unclear repair duties or a ground rent that rises sharply. These terms can be changed by agreement with the freeholder in a deed of variation, and in some cases through a tribunal.

Dealing with it on a sale

If you are selling, there may be ways to deal with a short lease as part of the sale, such as starting the statutory process and passing the benefit of the notice to your buyer. Your solicitor will explain how that works and whether it fits your timescales.

Leaflegal does not tell you which option to choose. The firm we introduce you to will look at your lease and your circumstances and explain what is open to you.

Leasehold work is technical and deadline driven, so experience matters more than in many other kinds of property work. A general conveyancer may be excellent at buying and selling homes but rarely serve a lease extension notice. Here are some things people often look for when choosing a firm.

Regular leasehold experience

Ask how many lease extensions or enfranchisement claims the firm handles and who will do the work. A team that does this every week will know the common pitfalls, the usual tactics of large freeholders and the tribunal process if it gets that far.

Working with surveyors

The price for a lease extension or a freehold purchase is based on a valuation. A good firm works closely with specialist leasehold surveyors and can explain how the surveyor's report feeds into the notice and the negotiation. Some firms will suggest a surveyor, but you are free to choose your own.

Clear communication

These matters can take months. It helps to know who your contact is, how often you will hear from them and what you need to do at each stage. Ask the firm how it keeps clients updated.

Regulation and insurance

Solicitors in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and must carry professional indemnity insurance. Every firm Leaflegal introduces is regulated. You can check a firm's details on the regulator's website before you instruct it.

Transparent pricing

Ask what is included in the fee, what counts as an extra and what you might have to pay towards the freeholder's costs. A good firm will set this out in writing before any work starts.

Leasehold matters usually involve several different costs. Knowing what they are at the start makes it easier to compare quotes and plan your budget.

The premium

The premium is the price paid to the freeholder for the new lease or the freehold. It is usually the largest cost. Under the long standing rules it depends on factors such as the value of the flat, the years left on the lease, the ground rent and, for leases below 80 years, a share of what is called marriage value. The 2024 Act changes how premiums are worked out, including removing marriage value, but those parts depend on further regulations and are being introduced over time. A solicitor and surveyor will confirm what applies when you start.

Your own legal and valuation fees

You pay your own solicitor and surveyor. Many firms offer a fixed fee for a straightforward lease extension, with hourly rates for anything that becomes contested. Valuation fees vary with the property and the surveyor.

The freeholder's costs

Under the long standing statutory process, a leaseholder pays certain reasonable costs of the freeholder, such as their legal and valuation costs, but not their costs of going to a tribunal. The 2024 Act changes this so that each side generally pays its own costs, with some exceptions, but this change is part of the reforms being brought in over time. Your solicitor will explain the current position.

Other costs

There may be Land Registry fees, fees to your mortgage lender, and in some cases Stamp Duty Land Tax on a larger premium. Rates and thresholds change, so your solicitor will check what applies. If a dispute goes to a tribunal, there may be fees and further professional costs.

How long a lease matter takes depends on the route, the freeholder and whether the price is agreed. These are general ranges, and your solicitor will give you a more accurate picture once they see your lease.

Statutory lease extensions

Once the notice is served, the freeholder has a set period to reply with a counter notice. The date is stated in your notice and must be at least two months away. If the premium is agreed, the new lease can often be completed within a few months of serving the notice. If it is not agreed, there are strict deadlines for applying to the tribunal, and a contested case can take a year or longer.

Informal extensions

An informal extension can sometimes be completed more quickly, because there are no statutory waiting periods. In practice it still depends on how fast the freeholder responds and how long it takes to agree the terms.

Collective enfranchisement

Buying the freehold usually takes longer, because several flat owners have to organise themselves first. Getting participants together, setting up a company and agreeing funding can take months before any notice is served.

Lease variations

A simple variation agreed with a cooperative freeholder can be done fairly quickly. If the freeholder will not agree and a tribunal application is needed, it will take longer.

Planning around a sale

Many people only look at their lease when they decide to sell or remortgage. Starting early gives you more choice. If time is short, ask your solicitor what can be done in parallel with the sale.

Whatever the route, delays most often come from waiting for information, such as a copy of the lease, details of the freeholder or a response from a managing agent. Gathering what you can at the start, and replying quickly when your solicitor asks for something, helps keep things moving.

You do not need to have everything ready before you make an enquiry. These are the things a solicitor is likely to ask for, and having them to hand can speed up the first conversation.

  • A copy of your lease, or the title number of your flat
  • Roughly how many years are left on the lease
  • The name and address of your freeholder and any managing agent
  • Recent ground rent and service charge demands
  • Your mortgage lender's details, if you have a mortgage
  • Any letters or offers from the freeholder
  • Whether you are planning to sell or remortgage, and when
  • For a building wide claim, the number of flats and how many owners are interested

If you cannot find your lease

Most leases are registered at HM Land Registry, and a copy can usually be obtained online for a small fee. Your solicitor can do this for you. Older leases are sometimes not held in full, and the firm will explain how to deal with that.

Leasehold flats in England and Wales.
Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Do I need to have owned my flat for two years?
Not any more. The two year ownership requirement used to apply, but the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025. This means many people can start the process soon after buying. Your solicitor will confirm that the requirement does not apply to you before serving a notice.
Does it matter how long my lease has left?
You can usually extend however many years remain, as long as the lease was originally granted for more than 21 years. However, the number of years left affects the price under the long standing valuation rules. A lease with fewer than 80 years left can cost noticeably more to extend because marriage value applies.
Can I extend if I sublet my flat?
Letting your flat to a tenant does not usually stop you using the statutory right. You remain the leaseholder, and you can still serve a notice. Your solicitor may need to know about the tenancy, as the surveyor might need access to value the flat.
Can I extend if the flat is owned through a company?
In many cases, yes. The rules about company owners have changed over time and depend on the circumstances. Your solicitor will check whether a company owner qualifies before any notice is served.
My flat is above a shop. Can I still extend?
Usually, yes. The right to extend is available to qualifying flat owners whether or not the building contains commercial units. The stricter rules about commercial space mainly affect buying the freehold of the whole building rather than extending an individual lease.
Can I extend if I own the share of freehold?
If you already own a share of the freehold, you may be able to grant yourself a longer lease by agreement with the other freeholders, which is often simpler than the statutory route. A solicitor will look at how your freehold is held and explain the options.
What if there are two landlords?
Some flats have a head lease between the freeholder and the flat owner, so there are two or more landlords. The statutory process deals with this by involving a competent landlord who runs the claim, with the premium split between the different landlords. Your solicitor will identify everyone involved.
What is in a section 42 notice?
The notice includes details of the leaseholder and landlord, a description of the flat, details of the lease, the premium the leaseholder proposes and any other amounts, the terms proposed for the new lease, and the date by which the landlord must reply. The details must be accurate, and your solicitor will check them against the lease and the title before serving it.
How long does the freeholder have to respond?
The notice states a date by which the freeholder must serve a counter notice. That date must be at least two months after the notice is given. In the meantime, the freeholder can ask for proof of title, ask for a deposit and arrange to inspect the flat for valuation.
What is an informal lease extension?
It is a new or longer lease agreed by negotiation between you and your freeholder, rather than through the statutory process set out in the 1993 Act. The terms are whatever you both agree. Once the deal is signed, the new lease is registered at HM Land Registry like any other lease.
Is an informal extension legally binding?
Once the new lease or deed of variation has been signed and completed, it is legally binding in the same way as any other lease. Before that point, either side can usually walk away. Heads of terms and letters agreeing the main points are not normally binding on their own.
Who can offer an informal extension?
Any freeholder can offer one, and any leaseholder can ask. Some large freeholders send out offers when leases reach a certain length. Smaller freeholders may agree one if asked. There is no obligation on the freeholder to offer or accept an informal deal.
Why would a freeholder offer one?
Freeholders have their own reasons. An informal deal can save them time and the uncertainty of a statutory claim, and it may allow them to keep a ground rent or add terms they prefer. Knowing this can help you understand why an offer looks the way it does.
Do I qualify for an informal extension?
There are no qualifying conditions in the same way as for the statutory right. If the freeholder is willing, an informal extension can usually be agreed. This is one reason it was sometimes used by owners who had not yet owned their flat for two years, a requirement that used to apply to the statutory route until the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025.
Is an informal extension the same as a deed of variation?
Sometimes. An informal extension can be done by granting a brand new lease or by varying the existing lease to add years. Your solicitor will explain which method is proposed and how it affects things like ground rent rules and registration.
Is an informal extension common?
Yes, many lease extensions are agreed informally, especially where the freeholder is a small landlord or a company owned by the flat owners. Larger freeholders also agree informal deals, sometimes on standard terms. Whether it is the right route for you depends on the offer and your plans, which is something a solicitor can talk through with you.
My freeholder has written to me with an offer. What now?
Many people ask a solicitor to review the offer before replying. They can explain what the terms mean, how they compare with the statutory route and whether anything might cause problems later. A surveyor can also give a view on value. You do not have to accept, and you do not have to reply straight away.
What ground rent terms should I look out for?
Rents that double at fixed intervals, rents linked to inflation without a cap, and rents that are high compared with the value of the flat can all cause concern for lenders and buyers. Rules introduced in 2022 also limit ground rent on many new leases. Your solicitor will check the proposed rent against these rules.
How many flat owners need to take part?
Under the current rules, the number of participating qualifying tenants must be at least half of the total number of flats in the building. In a building with just two flats, both owners must take part. Your solicitor will count the flats and check who qualifies, because getting the numbers wrong can make the notice invalid.
Does our building have to be purpose built?
No. Converted houses and purpose built blocks can both qualify, as long as the building is self contained and meets the other conditions. Some small converted buildings with a resident landlord are excluded, so your solicitor will check whether that applies to your building.
We have shops on the ground floor. Can we still buy the freehold?
Possibly. Under the long standing rules, a building does not qualify if more than 25 per cent of its internal floor area is non residential. The 2024 Act raises this to 50 per cent, and that change is being brought in over time. Your solicitor and surveyor will measure the commercial parts and confirm which limit applies.
Do owners who let out their flats count?
Yes. Owners who let their flats to tenants can usually be qualifying tenants and can take part. What matters is that they hold a long lease of the flat. An owner who lives elsewhere, even abroad, can still participate.
Can we include the garden or car park?
Often, yes. Land and facilities that the flat owners are entitled to use under their leases, such as gardens, car parks and bin stores, can usually be included in the claim. Your solicitor will check the title plans and the leases to identify what can be bought.
What if one owner holds several flats?
Ownership of several flats can affect who counts as a qualifying tenant under the rules, and the position has been changed over time. Your solicitor will look at who owns each flat and how that affects the numbers.
What is a resident landlord exclusion?
Some small buildings are excluded from collective enfranchisement where the building is a conversion with only a few units and the freeholder, or an adult member of their family, has lived in one of the flats as their main home for a qualifying period. The rules are detailed, and your solicitor will check whether they apply before the group spends money on the claim.
Do all the owners have to agree?
No. Only the required number of qualifying owners need to take part. Owners who do not want to join can stay out, keep their current leases and have the company as their landlord afterwards. It is still worth involving as many as possible, as this spreads the cost.
How do we decide who pays what?
The group agrees this between themselves, usually in a participation agreement. Common methods include sharing according to the value each flat adds to the price or by floor area. Flats with shorter leases can account for a larger share under the long standing valuation rules. Your solicitor can explain the options but cannot choose for you.
What counts as a short lease?
There is no fixed legal definition. Many people treat a lease with fewer than 80 or 90 years left as short, because that is when lenders, buyers and valuers start to pay closer attention. Leases with 60 years or less are usually seen as a significant issue. A solicitor can tell you exactly how many years are left on yours and what that means in practice.
How do I check how long is left on my lease?
Your lease sets out the start date and the original term. Subtract the time that has passed since the start date to find what remains. Note that the start date in the lease is not always the date it was signed. If you do not have the lease, a copy can usually be obtained from HM Land Registry, and a solicitor can check it for you.
Why does 80 years matter?
Under the long standing valuation rules, once a lease has 80 years or fewer left, the freeholder is entitled to half of the marriage value when it is extended. This can make the extension noticeably more expensive. The 2024 Act removes marriage value, but this depends on regulations and is being brought in over time, so a solicitor will confirm whether the 80 year point still affects you.
What happens when a lease runs out completely?
In some circumstances the law allows the occupier of a flat to stay on as a tenant when a long lease ends, but usually paying a market rent and without owning a lease they can sell. The flat's capital value effectively goes back to the freeholder. This is rare, because most owners extend well before it happens.
I inherited a flat with a short lease. What are my options?
Personal representatives can often start a statutory lease extension on behalf of the estate within a set period after the grant of probate or letters of administration. They can also sell the flat as it is, or sell with the benefit of a notice. A solicitor can explain the options and the time limits for the estate.
Does a short lease affect my service charge?
No, the service charge is set by the terms of the lease and the costs of running the building, not by how many years are left. However, if you extend informally, the new lease could change some terms. Under the statutory route, only limited changes to the existing terms are allowed.
Does the ground rent make a short lease worse?
It can. Under the long standing valuation rules, part of the premium compensates the freeholder for losing its ground rent, so a higher or rising ground rent usually means a higher premium. Some short leases also have ground rent terms that worry lenders. A surveyor can explain how your ground rent affects the cost of extending.
Can I extend a lease with very few years left?
Usually, yes. The statutory right to extend does not depend on how many years remain, as long as the lease was originally granted for more than 21 years and you qualify. However, the shorter the lease, the higher the premium tends to be under the long standing rules. A surveyor can give you an idea of the likely cost.
Should I extend now or wait for the reforms?
That depends on your circumstances and is a decision for you. Waiting may mean the lease falls further and becomes more expensive under the current rules, and it may not fit plans to sell or remortgage. The timing and detail of the reforms can also change. A solicitor can explain the factors involved so you can make an informed choice.
What is a deed of variation?
It is a legal document that changes the terms of an existing lease. It is signed by the leaseholder and the freeholder, and sometimes by other parties such as a management company. Once completed, it is registered at HM Land Registry so that anyone looking at the title can see the lease and the changes made to it.
Why would my lease need changing?
Common reasons include a wrong or missing plan, unclear repair or insurance clauses, service charge shares that do not add up, missing rights to use shared parts and onerous ground rent terms. Often the problem only comes to light when a buyer's solicitor or a lender reviews the lease. A solicitor will read your lease and explain whether a variation would help.
Can a lease be changed after it has been signed?
Yes. A lease is a contract, and the parties can agree to change it at any time. In some cases a tribunal or court can also order a change. The change must be properly documented and registered to be effective against future buyers and lenders.
Who needs to agree to a variation?
Usually the leaseholder and the freeholder, plus any management company that is a party to the lease. Mortgage lenders on both sides may also need to consent. If the change affects other flats, such as service charge shares, other leaseholders may need to be involved.
Is a lease variation the same as a lease extension?
No. A variation changes the terms of the existing lease, while an extension gives a longer lease. They can be combined, as a new lease on an extension can sometimes fix defects at the same time. Your solicitor can explain whether doing both together would make sense for you.
Can Leaflegal tell me if my lease is defective?
No. Leaflegal does not give legal advice or review leases. We introduce you to a regulated firm, and the solicitor there can read your lease and explain any problems.
Is a licence different from a variation?
Yes. A licence is a one off permission from the freeholder, for example to carry out particular alterations, and it leaves the lease terms unchanged. A variation permanently changes what the lease says. If you only need permission for something specific, a licence may be simpler. Your solicitor will explain which is appropriate.
What is a doubling ground rent?
It is a ground rent that doubles at fixed intervals, such as every ten or fifteen years. Over a long lease this can lead to very high rents. Lenders and buyers are often cautious about these terms. Many owners with doubling ground rents look at varying the lease or extending it to deal with the problem.
My lease plan is wrong. Does that matter?
It can. Buyers and lenders want to be sure what is included in the lease, such as the flat itself, a parking space or a garden. A wrong or unclear plan can hold up a sale or remortgage. A solicitor can check the plan against the title and suggest how it might be corrected.
