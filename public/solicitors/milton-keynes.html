Choosing a solicitor in Milton Keynes

The right firm for you depends on the kind of matter you have, not just on being close by. A conveyancing firm on the high street in Milton Keynes may not handle a contested licence review, and a family law specialist will not usually deal with a speeding charge. That is why we ask what your matter is about before we introduce anyone.

It is worth asking any firm how it charges before work starts. Many matters are handled for a fixed fee, while others are charged by the hour. A good firm will explain the likely total, what could make it change and what is included, and confirm it in writing.

You can check that a solicitor is regulated on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and that a licensed conveyancer is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. It is natural to want a solicitor who is close by. For some kinds of matter that is useful. For others, the location of the firm makes very little difference to the service you receive.

When local knowledge can help

Some matters involve a particular court, council or local authority. A motoring offence is usually heard at a magistrates' court in the area where the offence happened. A premises licence is applied for to the local council where the premises is, and the council's licensing policy and the views of local police and residents can matter. A family dispute may be heard at the local family court. In these cases a firm that knows the local procedures and people can be an advantage, and attending a hearing is easier if the firm is not far away.

When location matters less

Conveyancing, lease extensions and business debt recovery are now mostly handled by phone, email, post and online portals. Property searches and Land Registry applications are done electronically, county court claims for money are often issued online, and documents can be signed and returned without visiting an office. Many people use a firm in a different town and never meet in person, and are perfectly happy with the service.

Meeting in person

If meeting face to face matters to you, say so when you make your enquiry. Some people find it easier to discuss personal matters, such as a separation, in person. Others are more comfortable with video calls. Many firms offer both, and you can ask what the firm usually does on the first call.

Getting the balance right

In the end, experience in your type of matter, clear communication and fair fees usually matter more than the distance to the office. A nearby firm that rarely handles your kind of matter may not be the best choice, while a firm a few towns away that does it every day may suit you well. The firm we introduce handles your type of work and covers your area.

Area by area

Conveyancing firms routinely act for buyers and sellers all over England and Wales. What matters more is that the firm is on your lender's panel and responds quickly. Local knowledge can help with things like unusual local searches or properties affected by mining or flood risk, but a good conveyancer will know how to deal with those wherever the property is.

Lease extension work is mostly paperwork and negotiation with the freeholder's solicitor, supported by a valuation from a surveyor. The surveyor usually needs to know the local property market, but the solicitor can be based anywhere. If the matter goes to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), hearings are often held remotely or at a regional venue.

Family solicitors often deal with the family court that covers the area where you or your children live. Many hearings now take place by video, but some are in person. Some people prefer a firm they can visit, especially during a difficult separation.

Motoring and licensing cases are the most local. Magistrates' courts, council licensing committees and local police all have their own ways of working. A firm that regularly appears before them can be a real help, and it may be able to attend a hearing at short notice.

Business debt recovery is usually handled through letters, negotiation and, if needed, the county court. Many money claims are issued and managed online, so the firm's location matters less than its experience with commercial debts.

Wales

England and Wales share one legal system, so firms in England can usually act for clients in Wales and the other way round. Some differences do apply in Wales, such as Land Transaction Tax instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax on property purchases, and some housing and planning rules. If you would like to deal with a firm in Welsh, mention it in the notes and the firm will tell you whether it can help.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, so we do not cover matters there for conveyancing, family law, motoring offences, licensing, lease extensions or debt recovery. Immigration introductions are available across the whole of the UK.

Whether a firm is round the corner or at the other end of the country, it is worth checking it before you instruct it. This is easy to do and takes a few minutes.

Check the register

Solicitors and law firms in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can search for them on the SRA register. Licensed conveyancers are regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers, which has its own register. Regulated immigration advisers can be checked with their regulator, such as the SRA or the Immigration Advice Authority.

Look for the regulatory information

Regulated law firms must show certain information on their websites, including who regulates them and their registration number. Many firms display the SRA's digital badge, which links to the firm's entry on the register. If a firm's website does not say who regulates it, ask.

Check the people, not just the firm

Ask who will handle your matter day to day. That might be a solicitor, a chartered legal executive, a licensed conveyancer or a paralegal working under supervision. All of these can be appropriate. It is reasonable to ask about their experience and who supervises them.

Read the paperwork

Before any work starts, the firm should send a client care letter and terms of business. These set out the fees, who will do the work, how complaints are handled and, if you were introduced by Leaflegal, the fee the firm pays for the introduction. Read them carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

Every firm we introduce is regulated

Warning signs

Be cautious if anyone offering legal help will not say who regulates them, asks you to pay large sums before explaining their fees in writing, promises a particular result or pressures you to decide on the spot. Be especially careful with emails asking you to send money for a property purchase to new bank details. Always check bank details by phone using a number you already know before sending money to a firm.

Does a local firm cost more or less

Legal fees vary between firms, and location is only one factor. Firms in some large cities may have higher overheads, but that does not always mean higher prices for a particular piece of work. Fixed fee work, such as many house purchases, can be priced similarly across the country. The best way to understand the cost is to ask the firm for a written estimate or quote.

Travel and attendance

Where a solicitor needs to attend a court or hearing, the cost of travel and waiting time may be included in a fixed fee or charged separately. A local firm may charge less for travel to a local court. Ask how attendance is charged so you can compare like for like.

Disbursements

On top of the firm's own fees there may be disbursements, such as court fees, search fees, Land Registry fees, licensing application fees set by the council, or a barrister's fee. Official fees change from time to time, and GOV.UK lists many of them. The firm will tell you which apply.

Comparing quotes

If you speak to more than one firm, check that each quote covers the same work and includes the same items. A lower headline fee may leave out things that another firm includes. Ask each firm to confirm what is and is not included.

What is indemnity insurance?
It is a one off insurance policy that covers the financial risk of a specific legal problem, such as a missing building regulation certificate or a lack of a formal right of way. It does not fix the problem itself. Your conveyancer will explain what a policy covers and its limits.
What is the report on title?
Before you sign the contract, your conveyancer will usually send you a written report. It summarises the property's title, the results of the searches, the seller's answers, the key terms of the contract and anything unusual. Read it carefully and ask about anything you do not understand, as it is your chance to raise concerns before you are committed.
When do I pay my deposit?
Your deposit is usually transferred to your conveyancer shortly before exchange, so that it is ready to be sent to the seller's conveyancer when contracts are exchanged. Your firm will tell you how much is needed and by when. Allow time for the bank transfer to clear and confirm the account details by phone.
What happens if my mortgage valuation is low?
If the lender values the property at less than the agreed price, it may lend less than you hoped. You might renegotiate the price with the seller, put in more of your own money or look at other lenders. Your broker can help with the lending side, and your conveyancer can explain how any price change is handled.
Can family members help with the deposit?
Yes, this is common. Lenders and conveyancers need to know about any gift, and the person giving it will usually sign a gift letter and provide identity and bank statements. If the money is a loan or comes with conditions, it must be disclosed, as it can affect your mortgage.
What if my mortgage offer expires?
Mortgage offers are valid for a limited time. If your purchase is delayed, you may need to ask your lender for an extension or a new offer. Keep your broker informed about the expected timetable, and tell your conveyancer if the offer is close to expiring.
What is a completion statement?
It is a document from your conveyancer showing the money coming in, such as your mortgage and your own funds, and the money going out, such as the purchase price, fees, disbursements and tax. It tells you exactly how much you need to send before completion.
What if I have already signed but now regret it?
If the order has not yet been approved, speak to a solicitor quickly, as it may still be possible to withdraw consent before the judge approves it. Once approved, it is much harder to change. A solicitor can explain where you stand based on how far the process has gone.
Will a judge always approve our order?
Not always. The judge checks that the agreement falls within a broad range of fair outcomes. Most well prepared orders are approved, but a judge may ask questions or suggest changes if something looks unbalanced or unclear. A short explanation of the background often resolves their concerns. Your solicitor will prepare it with approval in mind.
What is the statement of information form?
It is a short form that summarises each person's finances and circumstances, such as their income, assets, housing plans and whether they plan to remarry. The judge uses it to check whether the agreement is fair. It must be accurate and complete. Both people usually sign it, or it can be completed separately. Errors or gaps are a common reason for orders being returned.
How long does approval take?
It depends on the court's workload, which changes over time. Your solicitor can tell you the current timescale. The conditional order must also have been made before the consent order can be approved. Approval can be quicker where the paperwork is complete and the order is clearly drafted. Orders that are returned for corrections take longer, so it is worth getting the details right first time.
What happens if the judge asks for a hearing?
Both people, or their solicitors, are usually asked to attend a short hearing to answer the judge's questions. This is often a chance to explain why the agreement suits your family. It does not mean the agreement has failed. Hearings of this kind are usually short. Your solicitor can attend with you or on your behalf and help explain the agreement.
Can the judge change our agreement?
The judge cannot rewrite your agreement without your consent. They can refuse to approve it, ask for more information or suggest changes. If changes are needed, both people need to agree before the order is approved. In practice most judges simply ask for an explanation or a small change in wording. Your solicitor will discuss any request with you before anything is agreed.
Can the consent order be submitted before the conditional order?
The papers can be prepared and agreed in advance, and some couples submit them so that they are ready. The judge cannot approve the order until the conditional order has been made. Your solicitor will explain the timing that works best with your divorce. Preparing early can avoid delay. Ask the firm.
What are special reasons?
Special reasons are circumstances connected to the offence that allow a court not to impose an obligatory ban, or to impose a shorter one. Examples raised in court include a spiked drink, a genuine medical emergency or a very short drive. They are not about personal hardship. They need evidence and a separate hearing, and courts are careful about accepting them.
Can I keep driving before my court date?
In most drink driving cases, yes. The ban normally starts on the day the court imposes it, not on the day you were stopped or charged. If you were bailed with conditions, check whether any of them affect driving. Many people use the time before the hearing to arrange other ways of getting to work and meeting family commitments.
How do I get my licence back?
When the ban ends, you usually need to apply to the DVLA for your licence to be returned. If you are classed as a high risk offender, you will need to pass a medical assessment arranged through the DVLA before the licence is returned. The DVLA sends information about this towards the end of the ban.
What is a high risk offender?
It is a category used by the DVLA for some drink driving offenders. It usually covers people with a reading of two and a half times the limit or more, people convicted of failing to provide a specimen, and people with two drink driving convictions within ten years. They must pass a medical assessment before their licence is returned.
Will my insurance go up after the ban?
Almost certainly. Insurers treat drink driving convictions seriously, and many will ask about them for several years. Some insurers decline cover, and specialist insurers may charge much more. Being honest with insurers when they ask is important, because failing to disclose a conviction can cause problems if you make a claim.
Can I drive abroad while banned in the UK?
A UK driving ban means you hold no valid licence during the ban, and an International Driving Permit relies on a valid licence. Different countries also have their own rules about recognising foreign bans. It is not something to assume. Check with the authorities of the country concerned and speak to a solicitor if you need to know more.
Can I appeal a drink driving ban?
There is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court against conviction, sentence or both, within 21 days of sentence. In some cases you can apply for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is decided. Appeals need to have proper grounds. A solicitor can look at what happened and tell you whether an appeal is realistic.
How much is the council fee?
The fee depends on the rateable value band of the premises, and there is an annual fee once the licence is granted. Very large venues may pay more. Fees change from time to time, so check the current figures with the council or on GOV.UK.
How much will a solicitor charge?
Many firms quote a fixed fee for preparing and submitting the application, with a separate fee for attending a hearing if one is needed. More complex applications, such as late venues in busy areas, may cost more. Ask for a written quote and check whether plans and advertising are included.
Does Leaflegal charge for the introduction?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The licensing firm we introduce you to pays us a fee, and that does not change what you pay them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.
How long does a premises licence last?
Most premises licences last indefinitely, as long as the annual fee is paid and the licence is not surrendered, revoked or lapsed. A licence can be granted for a limited period if that is what was applied for, such as for a seasonal event. Your solicitor can explain anything specific to your licence.
What are the mandatory conditions?
All licences that permit alcohol sales carry certain mandatory conditions. These include having a DPS with a personal licence, every sale being made or authorised by a personal licence holder, an age verification policy, offering free tap water, making small measures available and rules on irresponsible promotions and minimum pricing. Your solicitor can explain each one.
What if I do not pay the annual fee?
If the annual fee is not paid when due, the council must usually suspend the licence, which means you cannot carry on licensable activities. There are limited exceptions where the failure is due to an administrative error or a dispute. Keeping a note of the due date avoids a lot of trouble.
What if the business changes later?
If you want different hours, a new layout or different activities later, you will usually need to vary the licence. If someone else takes over the business, the licence will need to be transferred. A solicitor can help with these changes when the time comes.
What happens to the directors?
When a company is wound up by the court, the Official Receiver investigates the reasons for its failure and the conduct of its directors. If there is evidence of misconduct, action can follow, such as disqualification. The creditor is not responsible for this, but may be asked for information.
Who pays the costs of the petition?
If a winding up order is made, the petitioner's costs are usually paid from the company's assets before ordinary creditors. If the company pays to avoid an order, the creditor will often ask for its costs too. If the petition fails because the debt is disputed, the creditor may have to pay the company's costs.
Can a winding up order be reversed?
In limited circumstances, a company or another interested party can apply to the court to rescind or stay a winding up order, usually very soon after it is made. This is uncommon. Your solicitor will explain what this might mean if it arises in your case.
What if the company goes into administration instead?
A company facing a petition sometimes enters administration to protect itself while it tries to rescue the business or sell it. Administration usually brings a moratorium that stops creditors from continuing a petition without permission. Your claim would then normally be dealt with by the administrator. Your solicitor will explain how this affects you.
Can the directors set up a new company?
Directors of a liquidated company can sometimes set up a new business, but there are restrictions on reusing the old company's name, and the Official Receiver will look at their conduct. A creditor cannot usually pursue the new company for the old one's debts unless, for example, a director signed a personal guarantee. Your solicitor can explain the position.
How much does a winding up petition cost?
There is a court fee and a deposit for the Official Receiver, plus smaller costs for service and advertisement, and the solicitor's fees. Some firms offer fixed fees for an undefended petition. Fees are set by the government and change from time to time, so ask the firm for current figures.
Is the deposit refunded?
The deposit is meant to cover some of the Official Receiver's costs if an order is made. If the petition is dismissed or withdrawn, the deposit can often be returned. Your solicitor will explain the current position on refunds.
Is the premium fixed when I serve my notice?
The valuation is generally carried out as at the date the notice is served. This is one reason the timing of the notice matters, as later changes in the market or in the number of years left do not usually affect the figure. Your surveyor will explain how this applies to you.
Does the freeholder get to inspect my flat?
After receiving the notice, the freeholder is usually entitled to reasonable access so its surveyor can value the flat. Your solicitor will help arrange a suitable time. Refusing reasonable access can cause problems with the claim, so it is best to cooperate with a properly arranged visit.
Do I have to pay a deposit?
The freeholder can ask for a deposit after receiving your notice. The amount is set by the rules and relates to the premium you proposed. It is usually held by the freeholder's solicitor and credited towards the price on completion. Your solicitor will tell you if one is requested and how much it is.
How is the premium worked out?
Under the long standing rules, the premium reflects the loss of ground rent to the freeholder, the value of the freeholder's right to get the flat back when the lease ends and, for leases with 80 years or fewer, half of the marriage value. A specialist surveyor works out these figures using market evidence. The 2024 Act changes the method over time.
Why is 80 years such an important figure?
Under the long standing valuation rules, marriage value only applies once a lease has 80 years or fewer remaining. Crossing that line can add a large amount to the premium. The 2024 Act removes marriage value, but this is part of the reforms still being brought in. Your surveyor can explain whether 80 years matters for you.
Can I negotiate the premium?
Yes. Most statutory claims settle by negotiation between the two surveyors. Your surveyor will use market evidence and their experience to argue for a fair figure. If agreement is not possible, the tribunal can decide.
Does my ground rent affect the price?
Yes. The freeholder is giving up its future ground rent, so the higher it is, and the faster it rises, the more the freeholder can claim. Ground rents that double regularly can have a noticeable effect on the premium.
Can I talk to the examiner?
Yes, in many cases. Firms often call the examiner to understand the objection and test a possible solution before filing a written response. A short conversation can sometimes resolve a point that would otherwise take several letters.
Does an objection mean my brand is unsafe to use?
Not necessarily. An objection is about whether the mark can be registered. A mark refused as descriptive may still be usable, but you may find it hard to stop others using similar words. The firm can explain what the objection means for your business in practice.
How long do I have to oppose a trademark?
Two months from the date the application is published in the Trade Marks Journal. You can extend this to three months by filing a notice of threatened opposition within the first two months. After that, the chance to oppose is gone and you would need to apply to cancel the mark once it is registered.
What is a notice of threatened opposition?
It is a short form that tells the applicant you are considering opposing and extends your deadline to three months. It costs little and is often used to open settlement talks. The applicant is told it has been filed, so it acts as an early warning.
Who can oppose my application?
Anyone can oppose on some grounds, such as descriptiveness or bad faith. Oppositions based on earlier marks or earlier rights must usually be brought by the owner of those rights. In practice most oppositions come from owners of similar marks.
What happens if I ignore an opposition?
If you don't file a counterstatement by the deadline, the application is usually treated as abandoned. You may also lose the opportunity to argue your case. Even if you plan to give up, getting advice can help you limit any costs and plan a sensible next step.
Can I continue using my brand during an opposition?
An opposition is about whether your mark can be registered, not directly about whether you can use it. However, an opponent who thinks your use infringes their rights may also take separate action. The firm can tell you how much risk continued use carries in your case.
I am worried about something in my past. Should I mention it?
Advisers can only help with what they know, and what you tell a regulated adviser is confidential. Raising a concern at the start gives them the chance to explain how it might be relevant. Advisers deal with worries like this regularly and will not judge you.
I do not have my parents' documents. Can I still get help?
Yes. Many people start with only partial records. Advisers can explain what other records might help and where they might be found, such as official registers or archives. Gathering old records can take time, so it helps to mention gaps early.
Should I wait before contacting an adviser?
That is up to you. Some people find it helpful to speak to an adviser well before they plan to apply, so there is time to gather documents and understand the process. An early conversation does not commit you to anything.
Do I need translations of foreign documents?
Documents not in English or Welsh usually need a certified translation for Home Office purposes. Your adviser can explain what kind of translation is needed and may be able to recommend a translator. Translation costs are usually separate from adviser fees.
Should I keep copies of what I send?
It is sensible to keep copies of every document and form, as well as any letters you receive. Your adviser will keep their own file, but your own copies can be useful in future, for example for passport applications.
What if I have changed my name?
Many people have used different names over the years, for example after marriage or because of different spellings in different countries. Advisers see this often. Having any documents that show a change of name, such as a marriage certificate or deed poll, can help the adviser understand your records.
How much does a citizenship application cost?
There is a Home Office fee, which changes from time to time and is listed on GOV.UK, plus your adviser's fee. There may be later costs too, such as a first British passport. Ask your adviser for a full estimate in writing.
