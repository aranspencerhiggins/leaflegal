Licensing explained

General information for people and businesses in Birmingham. Pick a topic to read more.

Licensing law decides who can sell alcohol, put on entertainment, serve hot food late at night or carry passengers for hire. In England and Wales most of this work is handled by local councils acting as licensing authorities. For pubs, bars, restaurants, shops, clubs and venues the main law is the Licensing Act 2003. For taxis and private hire vehicles it is a separate set of older laws, along with each council's own policy.

These permissions matter because trading without the right licence, or breaking the conditions on one, can be a criminal offence. A licence can also be one of the most valuable things a hospitality business owns. A bar with a late licence in a busy town centre is worth far more than the same building without one, and a taxi driver without a badge cannot earn a living.

Licensing is a specialist field. The procedures are detailed, the deadlines are strict and a lot depends on local policy and on the relationship between the business and bodies such as the police and environmental health. Many people only ever deal with it once or twice, which is why they often look for a solicitor who handles licensing work every day.

The four licensing objectives

Every decision under the Licensing Act 2003 is made by reference to four licensing objectives. These are the prevention of crime and disorder, public safety, the prevention of public nuisance and the protection of children from harm. Councils, the police and residents have to tie any objection to one or more of these, and the council has to make its decision with them in mind.

Understanding the objectives is the key to most licensing work. A well prepared application shows how the business will promote each of them. A strong response to an objection shows why the concern raised either does not relate to the objectives or can be dealt with by sensible conditions.

Councils are expected to aim to permit licensable activities where they can, while promoting the objectives. In practice that means most applications are granted, often with conditions, and refusal or revocation is usually kept for cases where conditions would not be enough. That does not mean any particular application will succeed, and local policy can make a big difference.

Licensing work falls into a handful of main types. Each has its own page on this site with more detail, but here is a short guide to help you work out which one fits.

New premises licences

A premises licence lets a particular place carry on licensable activities such as selling alcohol, providing regulated entertainment or serving hot food and drink between 11pm and 5am. A new application is needed for a new venue, or for existing premises that have never been licensed or whose licence has lapsed. It involves an operating schedule, a plan of the premises, public notices and a consultation period.

Variations

Once a premises has a licence, changes to hours, layout, activities or conditions usually need a variation. Some small changes can go through a simpler minor variation process. Larger changes follow a process much like a new application.

Transfers and DPS changes

When a business is sold or the operator changes, the premises licence usually has to be transferred to the new holder. When the manager named as designated premises supervisor leaves, a new one has to be put in place. These are routine applications, but timing matters, especially on the sale of a business.

Reviews

A review is where the police, another responsible authority or a member of the public asks the council to look again at a licence because of problems linked to the licensing objectives. The council can do anything from taking no action to revoking the licence. Reviews are often the most serious and urgent kind of licensing matter.

Personal licences

A personal licence is held by an individual and allows them to authorise the sale of alcohol from licensed premises. Questions often arise where the applicant has a criminal conviction, or where a holder is convicted and the council considers suspending or revoking the licence.

Taxi and private hire licences

Drivers, vehicles and operators are licensed by the local council, or by Transport for London in London. Councils can refuse, suspend or revoke licences, sometimes with immediate effect, and drivers often need help quickly to protect their income.

Most general high street firms do not offer licensing work, so it helps to find a firm that does it regularly. Licensing law is closely tied to local practice. Every council has its own statement of licensing policy, and some areas have special policies for busy nightlife districts. A firm that knows how licensing committees work, and how the police licensing teams approach things, can make a real difference to how smoothly a matter goes.

Things worth asking a firm

  • How often they deal with the kind of matter you have
  • Whether they have appeared before your council's licensing committee
  • Who will actually do the work and who will attend any hearing
  • Whether they offer a fixed fee, and what it covers
  • How they handle talks with the police and other authorities
  • How quickly they can act if there is a hearing date or deadline

It is also worth thinking about how the firm communicates. Licensing work often involves short notice requests from the police or council, so you want a firm that replies quickly and explains things in plain terms. For multi site operators, ask whether the firm can handle licences across different councils and keep track of renewals, annual fees and DPS changes for you.

Some licensing solicitors also work with licensing consultants, acoustic experts, security advisers or architects who draw licensing plans. For bigger applications or reviews, the firm may suggest using one of these specialists. Ask what they think is needed and what it would cost before you agree.

Solicitors and other advisers

Licensing is not a reserved legal activity, so some help is offered by non-lawyer consultants. Leaflegal only introduces regulated law firms. A solicitor is regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, carries insurance and is bound by rules on conduct and confidentiality. They can also represent you in the magistrates' court if a matter goes to appeal.

Licensing often overlaps with other areas. A business sale may involve commercial property and company work. A planning issue may sit alongside a licensing application. A taxi driver facing a licensing hearing may also have a related criminal or motoring matter. The firm can tell you if any of these apply and whether they can help.

Do not worry if you are not sure what kind of help you need. Describe the problem in your own words on the form and the firm will work out which process applies.

Licensing costs come in two parts. There are the fees charged by the council, and there are the fees charged by the solicitor if you choose to use one.

Council fees

Premises licence fees under the Licensing Act 2003 are set nationally and usually depend on the rateable value of the premises. There is an application fee and, once granted, an annual fee. Variations, transfers, DPS changes and personal licence applications each have their own fee. Taxi and private hire fees are set locally by each council and can vary a great deal between areas. Fees change from time to time, so check the current figures with your council or on GOV.UK.

Solicitor fees

Many licensing firms offer fixed fees for routine applications such as transfers, DPS changes, minor variations and straightforward new premises licences. Work that depends on how others respond, such as contested applications, reviews and appeals, is often charged on an hourly basis or as a staged fee, with a separate fee for attending a hearing.

Ask the firm what is included. For example, does the fee cover drafting the operating schedule, arranging the newspaper notice, talking to the police and attending a hearing if one is needed? Are council fees, plan drawing and advertising costs included or extra? A good firm will confirm its fees in writing before work starts.

For businesses with several sites, some firms offer retainers or annual packages covering routine changes and compliance checks. These can be more predictable than paying for each piece of work separately, but compare what is included with what you are likely to need.

Other costs to think about

  • Professional licensing plans drawn to the required standard
  • Newspaper advertising for new and full variation applications
  • Accredited training for personal licence applicants
  • Specialist reports such as noise or crowd safety assessments
  • Costs in the magistrates' court if a decision is appealed

Licensing has fixed periods set by law, and councils have to follow them. This means some parts of the process cannot be rushed, however urgent your opening date is.

Applications with a consultation period

New premises licence applications and full variations have a consultation period during which responsible authorities and members of the public can make representations. If none are made, the licence is usually granted shortly afterwards. If representations are made and cannot be resolved, a hearing is arranged, which adds several weeks.

Quicker applications

Temporary event notices have their own deadlines, measured in working days before the event. A late notice gives less time but is limited in number and can be stopped outright by a police or environmental health objection. If an event date is fixed, it is worth checking the deadlines well in advance.

Minor variations, transfers and DPS changes are usually quicker. Transfers and DPS changes can often be given immediate effect on application, so trading can continue while the council processes them.

Reviews and urgent matters

Reviews follow a set timetable that leads to a hearing. Summary reviews brought by the police after serious crime or disorder move much faster, with interim steps considered within days. Taxi licence suspensions can take effect immediately in some cases.

Appeals

Most licensing decisions can be appealed to the magistrates' court, but the time limit is short. In many cases it is 21 days from being told of the decision. If you think you may want to appeal, speak to a solicitor as soon as you receive the decision.

Councils differ in how quickly they process paperwork and list hearings. A local licensing firm will usually know what to expect in your area.

The biggest cause of delay is usually a problem with the paperwork, such as a plan that does not meet the requirements, a notice that was not displayed for the full period or a newspaper advert placed late. If any of these go wrong, the application may have to start again, which resets the clock. Building in some spare time before an opening date is sensible.

Getting organised before you speak to a firm will save time and can reduce costs. You do not need everything, but the more the solicitor can see at the start, the better they can explain where you stand.

It also helps to think about what really matters to you. For a new venue, is it the closing time, the right to have live music or permission for an outdoor area? For a review, is it keeping the licence at any cost or protecting particular hours? Knowing your priorities helps the solicitor focus on what counts.

When do I need a new licence instead of a variation?
A variation cannot be used to vary the premises substantially. If a building is being completely rebuilt, extended into a different property or changed so much that it is effectively new premises, a fresh application may be required. Where the line falls is a matter of judgement, and a solicitor can advise on your project.
Can I change the DPS through a variation?
Changing the designated premises supervisor uses a separate, specific application, not a minor or full variation. It is usually quicker and can take immediate effect. Our page on transfers and DPS changes explains more about it.
Do I need to vary my licence after a refurbishment?
If the refurbishment changes the layout shown on your licence plan, such as moving the bar, changing exits or adding a new area, the plan will need updating. Small changes may be dealt with by a minor variation. Bigger changes may need a full variation. Trading on an out of date plan can cause problems.
How long does a minor variation take?
The council must make a decision within a set number of working days from receiving the application, and the white notice must be displayed for a shorter period than a full variation. In practice a minor variation is usually much quicker than a full variation. If the council does not decide in time, the application is treated as refused.
How long does a full variation take?
There is a 28 day consultation period. If there are no unresolved representations, the variation is usually granted shortly after. If a hearing is needed, it is normally held within 20 working days of the end of the consultation. Allow time beforehand for preparing the application and any plans.
What notice do I need to display?
For a minor variation, a white notice is displayed at the premises for a set period. For a full variation, a blue notice is displayed for 28 consecutive days, and a notice must also be published in a local newspaper. Each has set requirements for size, wording and position.
Can I trade under the new terms while I wait?
No. Until the variation is granted, you must trade in line with your existing licence. For a one off event, a temporary event notice may sometimes help, within the limits that apply. A solicitor can explain the options if you have a fixed date in mind.
Who is consulted on a minor variation?
The council decides which responsible authorities to consult, depending on what is being changed. Other people can also make representations during the notice period. The council considers what is said and decides whether the change could adversely affect the licensing objectives.
Do I need a new plan?
Only if the variation changes the layout or the areas used for licensable activities. If it does, the plan must meet the same requirements as for a new licence, including showing exits, fire safety equipment and the licensed area. Many operators use a specialist to redraw the plan.
Can I trade while the transfer is processed?
If the current holder has consented and the application asks for immediate effect, you are treated as the licence holder from the time the council receives the application. That lets you trade without a gap. If the police later object and the transfer is refused, the licence goes back to the previous holder.
Can the police object to a transfer?
Yes, but only in exceptional circumstances where they believe the transfer would undermine the crime prevention objective. Home Office Immigration Enforcement can object on grounds about illegal working. Objections must be made within 14 days. If one is made, the council holds a hearing.
Will there be a right to work check?
Yes, if the transfer applicant is an individual. The council must check that they have the right to work in the UK, and documents usually go with the application. A licence held by an individual can also lapse if they stop being entitled to work in the UK. Companies are treated differently.
Can a licence be transferred between companies in the same group?
Yes. If a group reorganises and a different company will run the premises, a transfer to that company is usually needed so that the licence holder matches the business operating the site. The process is the same as any other transfer. Your solicitor can check whether a transfer is needed or whether the existing holder can stay in place.
What does a designated premises supervisor do?
The DPS is the person named on the licence as having day to day responsibility for the premises. They must hold a personal licence. They are the main point of contact for the police and council, and they are expected to be closely involved in how alcohol sales are managed. There is no rule that they must be there at all times.
Who applies to change the DPS?
The premises licence holder makes the application, with a consent form signed by the new DPS. A copy goes to the police and the outgoing DPS must be notified. The application can ask for immediate effect so that the new DPS takes over straight away.
What happens if my DPS leaves without notice?
If the DPS removes themselves, alcohol cannot lawfully be sold until a new DPS is in place. The licence holder should apply to name a replacement as quickly as possible, with immediate effect. Keeping a second personal licence holder on staff can help cover this kind of gap.
Can one person be DPS at more than one premises?
Yes, the law does not prevent it. However, the DPS is expected to be able to take responsibility for each site, and the police may have concerns if someone is named for many premises they rarely visit. Some councils address this in their policies. A solicitor can explain local expectations.
Can the police object to a new DPS?
Yes, in exceptional circumstances, if they believe the person would undermine the crime prevention objective. They must object within 14 days. A hearing then takes place. A solicitor can represent the licence holder and the proposed DPS at the hearing.
Can a resident apply for a review on their own?
Yes. Any person can apply, as long as the grounds relate to the licensing objectives. The council will consider whether the application is frivolous, vexatious or repetitive before accepting it. Reviews brought by residents often concern noise and nuisance, and the outcome depends on the evidence like any other review.
What is a review after a closure order?
If a magistrates' court makes certain closure orders in respect of licensed premises, the council is required to review the licence. This happens automatically and follows its own timetable. Because a closure order means there has already been serious concern, these reviews need careful preparation.
Can I stop a review from going ahead?
Once the council accepts an application, it cannot usually be stopped by the licence holder. The applicant can withdraw it, which sometimes happens if concerns are resolved. More often, the outcome is shaped by agreeing proposals with the applicant and authorities, which can then be presented to the committee at the hearing.
How long does a review take?
There is a 28 day period for representations after the application is advertised. The hearing is then normally held within 20 working days of the end of that period. Summary reviews brought by the police are much quicker, with interim steps considered within 48 hours and a full hearing within 28 days.
Can I keep trading during a review?
Usually yes. On an ordinary review, the licence continues as normal until the decision and, in most cases, until any appeal period has passed. In a summary review, the council can impose interim steps, such as suspension, that take effect immediately. A solicitor can explain the position in your case.
What documents will I receive?
You should receive a copy of the review application from the applicant. Before the hearing, the council usually sends a report with the application, the representations and any supporting evidence. If you think documents are missing, your solicitor can ask the council or the applicant for them.
Who sits on the licensing sub-committee?
A sub-committee is usually made up of three councillors from the council's licensing committee, supported by a licensing officer and a legal adviser. The councillors make the decision. They are expected to keep an open mind and decide on the evidence and the licensing objectives.
Can new evidence be produced at the hearing?
Documentary evidence usually needs to be provided before the hearing day. New material on the day can be allowed only if all parties agree. This is one reason why careful preparation in advance is so important. Your solicitor will plan when and how to send in your evidence.
Do I have to attend the hearing myself?
You do not have to, but it often helps if the licence holder, the DPS or the person running the premises attends. The committee may want to ask them how the premises will be run in future. If you have a solicitor, they will usually speak for you and prepare you for any questions.
How long does a personal licence take?
If there is nothing to declare and the application is complete, the council will usually process it within a few weeks, although times vary between councils. If you declare a relevant conviction, the police have time to consider objecting, and a hearing may follow, which adds more time.
Does a personal licence expire?
No. Personal licences no longer need renewing and last indefinitely unless they are surrendered, forfeited, suspended or revoked. If you have an older licence with an expiry date printed on it, that date no longer applies. Your licence remains valid.
Can I use my licence anywhere?
Yes. A personal licence granted by any council in England and Wales can be used at licensed premises anywhere in England and Wales. It is not tied to a particular premises or employer, so you keep it if you change jobs.
Do I need the right to work in the UK?
Yes. Applicants must have the right to work in the UK, and the council will check this. A personal licence can lapse if the holder's right to work ends. If your immigration status is time limited, the licence may be linked to that. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Can I get a personal licence with a criminal record?
Often yes. Many people with past convictions hold personal licences. Spent convictions are generally not relevant. If you have an unspent relevant conviction, the police can object and the council may hold a hearing. The outcome depends on the offence, how long ago it was and your circumstances since.
What is a relevant offence?
Relevant offences are listed in the Licensing Act 2003. They include certain offences involving alcohol, drink driving, violence, dishonesty, drugs, some sexual offences, certain immigration offences and offences under licensing law. Equivalent offences abroad can also count. A solicitor can check whether a particular conviction is relevant.
Do I have to declare a drink driving conviction?
Drink driving offences are relevant offences, so an unspent drink driving conviction must usually be declared on an application. Whether the conviction is spent depends on the sentence and the date. If it is spent, it generally does not need to be declared. A solicitor can check your position.
What if I do not declare a conviction?
Knowingly or recklessly making a false statement on an application is an offence. It can also lead to the licence being challenged later and damage your credibility. If you are unsure whether something needs to be declared, it is better to check with a solicitor before you apply.
Will the police always object?
No. The police consider each declared conviction and decide whether granting the licence would undermine the crime prevention objective. Many applications with older or minor convictions go through without objection. Where they do object, a hearing allows you to explain your circumstances.
Can I get a licence with a criminal record?
It depends on the offence, how long ago it was and your record since. Most councils have a policy setting out how long a person should normally be free of certain types of offence. Serious violent or sexual offences usually lead to refusal. Each case is still considered individually. A solicitor can look at your history and the policy.
Can I drive for a private hire operator licensed in a different area?
For private hire, the driver, vehicle and operator generally all need to be licensed by the same council. Hackney carriages can be pre-booked to go to other areas. The rules on cross-border working are complex, and a solicitor or the council can explain how they apply to your work.
What does fit and proper mean?
It is the test councils use to decide whether someone can hold a driver or operator licence. There is no fixed definition, but the focus is on passenger safety and whether the person can be trusted. Councils look at convictions, complaints, driving record and conduct. They do not need a criminal conviction to decide someone is not fit and proper.
Do I need to renew my licence?
Yes. Driver, vehicle and operator licences are granted for set periods and must be renewed. Renewal is not automatic, and the council will look again at your record. Applying in good time avoids a gap during which you cannot work. Check your council's renewal deadlines.
Can I transfer my badge to a new council?
No. A badge issued by one council cannot simply be transferred to another. If you want to work in a different area, you usually need to apply to that council and meet its requirements, which may include its own knowledge test and checks. Your history with the first council will usually need to be declared.
Why might the council suspend or revoke my licence?
Reasons include a conviction for an offence involving dishonesty, indecency or violence, breaches of taxi and private hire law, complaints about conduct, driving offences and points, or any other reasonable cause. The council must act fairly and give reasons. What counts as reasonable cause depends on the facts.
Can my licence be suspended immediately?
Yes, if the council decides that the interests of public safety require it. The suspension or revocation then takes effect straight away and you must stop working. You can still appeal. Without an immediate effect decision, the licence usually continues until the appeal period ends or any appeal is decided.
Can the council act before a police investigation finishes?
Yes. The council applies its own test focused on passenger safety, and it does not have to wait for a criminal case to finish. It can act on information from the police or complaints even if no charge is brought. A solicitor can help you respond while being careful about any related criminal case.
Will points on my driving licence affect my badge?
They can. Many councils treat a certain number of points, or particular offences such as using a mobile phone while driving, as a reason to review a licence. A driving ban will usually mean you cannot drive for hire. If you are facing a motoring prosecution, think about the effect on your badge too.
