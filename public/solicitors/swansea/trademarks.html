Trademarks explained

General information for people and businesses in Swansea. Pick a topic to read more.

A trademark is the sign customers use to recognise your business. It is usually a name or a logo, but it can also be a slogan, a product name, a shape, a colour combination or even a sound. In the UK, trademarks are registered at the UK Intellectual Property Office, often called the UK IPO, and one registration covers England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Registering a trademark gives you the exclusive right to use it for the goods and services listed in the registration. That right makes it much easier to stop copycats, to take down lookalike listings online and to license or sell your brand later. Without a registration you may still have some protection through the law of passing off, but it is harder and usually more expensive to rely on.

Trademarks sit alongside other kinds of intellectual property. Copyright protects original creative work such as writing, photographs, artwork, music and software. Design rights protect the look of a product. Many businesses need help with more than one of these, which is why the firms we introduce handle copyright and designs as well as trademarks.

Who does trademark work

Trademark work is not a reserved legal activity, so in principle anyone can file an application. In practice most businesses use one of two kinds of regulated professional. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and can handle filings, disputes and court claims. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg, the regulator for patent and trade mark attorneys, and specialise in searching, filing and UK IPO proceedings such as oppositions.

Leaflegal only introduces trademark enquiries to firms regulated by one of those two bodies. Both sorts of firm work by phone, email and video, so the firm does not need to be near you. Because there is one UK register, we can introduce you wherever you are in the UK.

There is rarely only one way to deal with a brand question. The right route depends on what you sell, where you trade, how long you have used the name and how much risk you are prepared to accept. The firm will look at your own situation, but it helps to know the usual options before the first call.

Protecting a brand you already use

If you have been trading under a name for a while without registering it, the usual first step is a clearance search to see whether anyone has earlier rights, followed by an application for the classes that match what you sell. Using a name for years does not stop someone else registering something similar, so many owners file as soon as they know the brand is staying.

Launching something new

Before you commit to packaging, signage or a website, a search can tell you whether the name is likely to be free to use and register. If the search shows a problem, it is far cheaper to change the name at that stage. Some businesses file an application before launch so that the filing date is secured early.

When someone else is using a similar brand

Options range from a polite letter to a formal cease and desist letter, a takedown request to an online marketplace, an opposition at the UK IPO or a court claim. Many disputes settle without a hearing, sometimes through a coexistence agreement in which both sides agree how they will use their marks.

When you are on the receiving end

If you receive a letter, an opposition or a takedown notice, the firm can tell you how strong the other side's case looks and what your options are. Those may include changing the name, narrowing what you sell under it, negotiating a timetable to rebrand or defending your position.

Most trademark firms are small specialist practices or IP teams within larger law firms. Some focus on filings and portfolios, while others spend more time on disputes. When the firm contacts you, it is worth asking a few questions to check they are a good fit.

  • Are you a solicitor or a chartered trade mark attorney, and who regulates you?
  • How much of your work is UK trademark filing and how much is disputes?
  • Do you work with businesses of my size and in my sector?
  • Will you search before filing, and what will the search cover?
  • How do you charge, and what is included in a fixed fee?
  • Who will actually do the work and how will you keep me updated?

You can check a solicitor on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register and a chartered trade mark attorney on the IPReg register. Both registers are free to search online.

Solicitor or attorney

For a straightforward filing, a chartered trade mark attorney or a trademark solicitor can both do the job well. If a dispute looks likely to go to court, a solicitor can take the claim forward, and many attorney firms work closely with solicitors for that stage. Firms outside the UK

If you also need protection abroad, ask whether the firm files international applications through the Madrid system and works with local agents in the countries you need. Most UK trademark firms do this as a matter of routine.

Trademark costs come in two parts. The first is the official fee charged by the UK IPO, which depends on how many classes your application covers. The second is the professional fee charged by the firm for its advice and work. Official fees change from time to time, so check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask the firm.

Many firms quote a fixed fee for a clearance search, a fixed fee for preparing and filing a UK application, and a further fixed fee if the examiner raises an objection that needs a reply. Renewals and recording a change of owner are also often fixed fee work.

Disputes are harder to price at the outset because they depend on how the other side responds. Firms usually charge in stages, for example a fee for reviewing the evidence and sending a letter, and a separate estimate for an opposition or court claim. Ask for an estimate for each stage and for an idea of the costs you could have to pay the other side if you lose.

Most trademark problems are avoidable with a little planning. These are the issues firms see most often, and why they matter.

Choosing a descriptive name

Names that describe the product, its quality or where it comes from are tempting because customers understand them straight away. They are also the names most likely to be refused, and the hardest to enforce against competitors who use similar words. A name with a twist, or an invented word, is usually easier to protect.

Filing too narrowly or too widely

An application that only covers part of what you sell can leave gaps that a competitor can use. An application that claims far more than you will ever sell can attract objections and become vulnerable to a non use challenge after five years. The wording of the goods and services is one of the most important parts of the application.

The wrong owner

Marks are sometimes filed in the name of a director or owner personally, or a company that later stops trading, when the business intends to own the brand. Investors and buyers often check this during due diligence. Putting it right usually means recording an assignment, which is simpler before there is a dispute or a sale.

Ignoring letters and deadlines

Examiner reports, oppositions and renewal reminders all have fixed deadlines. Letters from other brand owners usually have a deadline too. Leaving them until the last minute reduces your options, and missing them can mean losing rights altogether.

Scam invoices

After you file, your details appear on the public register. Businesses often receive official looking letters offering to publish or monitor their mark for a large fee. These are not from the UK IPO. If you are unsure about a letter, ask the firm before you pay anything.

Trademark matters run to official timetables set by the UK IPO, and many of the deadlines are fixed. Knowing roughly how long each stage takes helps you plan launches, investment rounds and marketing.

  • A clearance search usually takes a few days to a couple of weeks, depending on how wide it is
  • The UK IPO usually examines a new application within a few weeks of filing
  • Accepted applications are published for two months, which can be extended to three, so that others can oppose
  • If nobody opposes, registration usually follows around four months after filing
  • An opposition commonly adds many months, and longer if it goes all the way to a decision
  • A registration lasts ten years from the filing date and can be renewed again and again

Disputes outside the UK IPO move at their own pace. A cease and desist letter usually gives the other side a short deadline to reply, often around two weeks. Court claims take much longer, which is one reason many disputes settle early.

Deadlines that matter

The deadlines to watch most closely are the date to reply to an examiner's report, the end of the opposition period for an application you want to challenge, the deadline in any opposition you are defending and the renewal date for each registration. Missing some of these can mean losing rights altogether, so tell the firm about any date on your paperwork.

  • The name, logo or slogan, and a picture of how you use it
  • A short description of what you sell or plan to sell

  • Any trademark application or registration numbers
  • Any letters, notices or emails you have received, with their dates

If you are dealing with copying, keep a record of what you find with the date you found it. Screenshots that show the web address and the date are especially helpful. Avoid contacting the other side in anger before you have spoken to the firm, because what you say can matter later.

Trademarks in Swansea, questions and answers

Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Is it infringement to mention a competitor's brand?
Not always. Honest comparative advertising, describing compatibility and genuine resale of branded goods can be allowed within limits. Using a competitor's mark in a way that suggests a connection or takes unfair advantage can cross the line. The details matter.
Can a trademark be infringed outside the UK?
A UK registration protects against use in the UK. Use abroad is governed by the law of that country. Online sales targeted at UK customers can count as use in the UK, even if the seller is based overseas.
What is a mark with a reputation?
A mark that is known by a significant part of the relevant public for the goods or services it covers. Such marks can be protected against use on dissimilar goods where that use takes unfair advantage of, or harms, the mark's reputation or distinctive character. Proving reputation needs evidence.
Does selling genuine second hand goods infringe?
Generally no. Once genuine goods have been put on the market in the UK or the European Economic Area by or with the consent of the owner, reselling them is usually allowed. There are exceptions, for example where the goods have been changed or the resale damages the reputation of the mark.
What should I do first if someone copies my brand?
Gather evidence, including dated screenshots and samples, and get advice before contacting the other side. The firm will check your rights and suggest the best first step. Acting quickly can matter, because long delay can sometimes weaken your position.
Should I contact the other business myself?
It is usually better to take advice first. A friendly message may work, but a poorly worded one can trigger an unjustified threats claim or reveal weaknesses in your position. The firm can help you decide the right tone and content.
Can I get an urgent injunction?
In clear and urgent cases, a court can grant an interim injunction to stop infringing use quickly, before a full trial. Courts expect the claimant to act promptly and may require an undertaking to pay damages if the injunction turns out to be wrong. The firm can tell you whether this is realistic.
What can I recover if I win?
Remedies can include an injunction, damages or the infringer's profits, delivery up or destruction of infringing goods, and a contribution to your legal costs. The amount depends on the evidence of loss or profit. The firm will give you a realistic view.
How long do I have to bring a claim?
Claims for damages are generally subject to a six year limitation period from the infringing acts. However, waiting a long time before acting can make it harder to get an injunction and can weaken your case in other ways. It is best not to delay.
Can I send a cease and desist letter by email?
Yes. Many letters are sent by email as well as post. The method matters less than the content, the recipient and keeping a record of when it was sent and received.
How long should the deadline be?
It should be reasonable in the circumstances. Around fourteen days is common. A shorter deadline may be justified where harm is ongoing or urgent. Unreasonably short deadlines can look aggressive and may count against the sender later.
What is a letter before claim?
It is a letter sent before court proceedings begin, setting out the claim in detail and inviting a response. Court rules expect parties to exchange information before suing. A cease and desist letter can serve as the first step, and a fuller letter before claim may follow if the dispute continues.
What should my cease and desist letter say?
It should identify your rights, describe the other side's activity, explain why it infringes or causes a problem, set out what you want them to do and give a deadline. It should be accurate and proportionate. The firm will tailor it to the situation.
Who should I send the letter to?
Usually the business responsible for the infringing activity, such as the manufacturer, importer or the business trading under the name. Sending threats to retailers or customers carries more risk under the unjustified threats rules. The firm will advise on the right recipient.
What if they ignore my letter?
You can send a follow up, take other steps such as platform takedowns or an opposition, or consider court proceedings. The right next step depends on the strength of your case and how much the issue matters to your business. The firm will explain your options.
Can I ask for compensation in the letter?
Yes, you can ask for damages or costs, but it is often better to focus on stopping the activity first. Demanding large sums early can make settlement harder. The firm will suggest what is realistic in your case.
Should I send a letter if my trademark isn't registered?
You may still have rights in passing off or copyright. The letter needs to be carefully framed around the rights you actually have, and you will need evidence of goodwill. The firm will advise on whether a letter is sensible and how to word it.
Can a cease and desist letter make things worse?
It can, if it is inaccurate, threatens the wrong person or provokes the other side to challenge your rights. That is why it helps to check your own position first. A carefully judged letter is usually the most effective first step.
Does renewing change my rights?
No. Renewal simply continues the registration for another ten years. It doesn't widen the goods and services or change the mark. If you need wider protection, you need a new application.
Should I renew a mark I don't use any more?
It depends. Keeping an unused mark costs renewal fees and it becomes vulnerable to revocation after five years without use. Sometimes it is still worth keeping for defensive or future reasons. The firm can help you decide.
Can I renew early to get it out of the way?
You can renew from six months before the expiry date, but not earlier. The renewal runs for ten years from the expiry date, so renewing early within that window doesn't shorten your protection.
Is the renewal date the same as the registration date?
No. The ten year period runs from the filing date of the application, not from the date the mark was registered. Check the filing date on the register or your certificate when working out when renewal is due.
Can a renewal be refused?
Renewal is an administrative step and is not examined again on its merits. As long as the request is made in time with the correct fee, the registration is renewed. Problems usually arise only from missed deadlines or unpaid fees.
My trademark has expired. Is it lost?
Not necessarily. If you are within six months of expiry, you can still renew with a late fee. If the mark has been removed, you may be able to ask for restoration within six months of removal. After that, you would need to file a new application.
How does restoration work?
You ask the UK IPO to restore the registration, explain why it wasn't renewed and pay the fees. The UK IPO considers whether the failure to renew was unintentional. Restoration is not automatic, so a clear and honest explanation matters.
Can someone else register my mark while it has lapsed?
They might try. If your registration has been removed, a third party could apply for the same or a similar mark. Restoring quickly, or filing a new application, reduces the risk. You may also have unregistered rights if you have continued to use the mark.
Should I file a new application as well as asking for restoration?
For an important mark, firms sometimes recommend both, so that you have a fallback if restoration is refused. The new application will have a new filing date and will be examined and published like any other. The firm will advise on the best approach.
What is substantial copying?
Infringement requires copying of the whole or a substantial part of a work. Substantial is judged by quality as much as quantity, so copying a small but important part can still infringe. The firm will compare the works.
Is copyright the same in every country?
No, but most countries protect works from other member countries of the main international treaties, so UK works are usually protected abroad automatically. The details, such as duration and exceptions, vary.
Is software protected by copyright?
Yes. Computer programs are protected as literary works, and preparatory design material is also protected. The underlying ideas and functionality are not protected by copyright, but the code and its structure can be.
Is artificial intelligence output protected by copyright?
This is a developing area. UK law has a specific rule for computer generated works without a human author, but how it applies to modern AI tools is uncertain and under review. If AI generated content is important to your business, ask the firm about the current position.
Who owns work I paid a freelancer to create?
Usually the freelancer, unless they have signed a written assignment transferring copyright to you. You may have an implied right to use the work for its intended purpose, but not necessarily to adapt it or stop others using it. An assignment fixes this.
Who owns work created by my employees?
Usually the employer, if the work was created in the course of employment, unless there is an agreement saying otherwise. Work created outside the job is usually the employee's. Contracts of employment often make this clear.
Can I get the copyright transferred after the work is done?
Yes, if the creator agrees. A written assignment signed by the creator can be made at any time. It is easier before there is a dispute or a sale. The firm can prepare the document.
What are moral rights?
Rights that stay with the creator, including the right to be identified as the author and to object to derogatory treatment of the work. They can't be transferred but can be waived in writing, which many commissioning contracts include.
Does an agency own the logo it designed for me?
Unless your agreement assigns copyright to you, the agency or the designer may own it. This can cause problems if you want to register the logo as a trademark or change it. It is worth checking your terms.
What is individual character?
A design has individual character if the overall impression it gives an informed user differs from the overall impression of earlier designs. The informed user is someone familiar with the products in that sector, more observant than an average consumer.
Can I protect part of a product?
Yes. A registered design can cover part of a product, such as the handle of a kettle or the sole of a shoe. The images show which part is protected, often by drawing other parts in dotted lines.
Is a design right the same as a trademark?
No. A design right protects the look of a product. A trademark protects a sign that tells customers who the product comes from, such as a name or logo. Some shapes can be both, but the rules and tests are different.
Can I register a design I have already shown publicly?
Often yes, if you apply within 12 months of first disclosing it. After that, your own disclosure can stop the design being registered. If you plan to show a product, it is best to speak to a firm first.
What images do I need?
Clear images showing the design from enough angles to make it understood, often several views. Line drawings, photographs or renderings can be used. The images define the protection, so they need to be prepared carefully.
Does the UK IPO check whether my design is new?
Not in detail. The UK IPO mainly checks formal requirements and does not search earlier designs before registering. A registered design can be challenged later if it was not new, so it is worth being confident about your design.
Can I register several designs together?
Yes. You can file multiple designs in one application, which can reduce costs. Each design is treated separately for protection and renewal.
Can I delay publication of my registered design?
Yes. You can ask for publication to be deferred, which can help if you want to keep the design confidential until launch. The firm can explain the time limits.
Do I need protection abroad?
A UK registered design only covers the UK. You can register in other countries, including through the Hague system for international designs. If you manufacture or sell abroad, consider where copies are most likely.

More questions people ask

What is conveyancing?
Conveyancing is the legal work involved in transferring the ownership of property, or registering a mortgage against it. It includes checking the title, carrying out searches, preparing or reviewing the contract, handling the money and registering the change at HM Land Registry. It applies to buying, selling, remortgaging and changing who owns a home.
Do I legally have to use a conveyancer?
It is possible to do your own conveyancing in some situations, but it is rare and difficult. If you have a mortgage, your lender will almost always insist that a regulated firm acts for it. Most people use a professional because the legal and financial risks of getting things wrong are high and hard to put right later.
What is the difference between freehold and leasehold?
Freehold means you own the building and the land it stands on with no time limit. Leasehold means you own the right to occupy the property for a set number of years under a lease, with a landlord who owns the freehold. Leasehold property usually comes with ground rent, service charges and extra checks during conveyancing.
What is HM Land Registry?
HM Land Registry keeps the official record of who owns most land and property in England and Wales. After a purchase, remortgage or transfer, your conveyancer applies to update the register. Some older properties are still unregistered, and the first sale or mortgage usually triggers an application for first registration, which takes more work.
What are searches?
Searches are enquiries made to the local authority, water companies and other bodies to find out about things that affect a property but may not be obvious from viewing it. They can reveal planning history, road adoption, drainage, flood risk, environmental issues and mining in some areas. Lenders usually require them when you buy with a mortgage.
When does a sale or purchase become legally binding?
In England and Wales, a sale or purchase only becomes binding when contracts are exchanged. Before that, either side can withdraw without penalty, even late in the process. Once contracts are exchanged, pulling out usually means losing the deposit or facing a claim for losses, so the timing of exchange matters a great deal.
What is the difference between a survey and a valuation?
A mortgage valuation is carried out for the lender to check the property is worth enough to lend against. It is not a detailed inspection and may not tell you about defects. A survey is commissioned by the buyer and looks at the condition of the property in more depth. Your conveyancer deals with the legal side and does not inspect the building, so many buyers arrange a survey as well.
What is a chain?
A chain is a series of linked sales and purchases that depend on each other. If you are selling to someone who is also selling, and you are buying from someone who is buying elsewhere, you are in a chain. Everyone in it usually needs to exchange contracts and complete on the same day, so a delay anywhere can affect everyone else.
