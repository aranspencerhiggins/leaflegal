Family law explained

General information for people and businesses in Manchester. Pick a topic to read more.

Family law deals with the legal side of relationships, separation and children. It covers how a marriage or civil partnership is ended, how money and property are divided, where children live and how they spend time with each parent, and the agreements couples make before or during a relationship. In England and Wales most of this work is handled by the Family Court, although the large majority of families settle things without a judge ever making a decision.

People usually come to family law at a difficult time. A relationship may have just ended, or it may have been ending slowly for years. There may be children who need stability, a home that one or both people want to keep, and worries about money. It is normal to feel overwhelmed. A family solicitor deals with these situations every day and can help you understand where things stand and what the next steps usually look like.

Family law is also about planning ahead. Couples who are about to marry, move in together or buy a home sometimes take advice to record what they intend, so that there is less to argue about if things go wrong later.

If you are worried about your safety

If you or your children are at risk of harm from a partner or family member, your safety comes first. In an emergency call the police on 999. You can also contact a domestic abuse support service such as the National Domestic Abuse Helpline, which is free and confidential. A family solicitor can explain the protective orders the court can make, but they are not an emergency service.

Paid help and legal aid

Legal aid is only available for some family matters, usually where there is evidence of domestic abuse or a risk to a child, and it depends on your income and savings. GOV.UK has a tool to check whether you might qualify. Leaflegal introduces people who are looking for paid help from a private family law firm. We are not a law firm and we do not give legal advice.

  • Dissolution, which ends a civil partnership in the same way

Children

  • Applying for or responding to a child arrangements order
  • Disputes about schooling, medical treatment, changing a name or moving away

There is more than one way to sort out a family matter. Going to court is usually the last resort, and the courts expect people to think about other options first. A solicitor will explain which routes might suit your situation, and many families use more than one.

Agreeing between yourselves

Some couples talk things through and reach an agreement on their own, then ask a solicitor to check it and put it into the right legal form. This can be the quickest and cheapest route where both people are calm, honest about their finances and broadly agree.

Solicitor negotiation

Each person has their own solicitor, who exchanges information and proposals by letter or email. The solicitors can help keep discussions focused and make sure nothing important is missed. Most financial settlements are reached this way or through mediation.

Mediation

A trained, neutral mediator helps both of you talk and work towards an agreement. The mediator does not take sides or make decisions. Before applying to court about children or finances, you will usually need to attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting, known as a MIAM, unless an exemption applies. Mediation may not be suitable where there has been domestic abuse.

Collaborative law

Each person has a specially trained collaborative lawyer, and everyone meets together to work things out. Both people usually sign an agreement not to go to court, so if the process breaks down they need to instruct new solicitors.

Arbitration and private hearings

An arbitrator, usually an experienced family lawyer, makes a decision that both people agree in advance to accept. Some couples also pay for a private early neutral evaluation, where an experienced lawyer gives a view on the likely outcome. These options can be quicker and more private than court, though both people pay the arbitrator or evaluator.

Court

Where agreement is not possible, or where someone needs urgent protection, either person can apply to the Family Court. Court proceedings follow set stages and can take many months. Even once a case is started, many families still reach agreement before the final hearing.

One of the first questions people ask is how long it will all take. The honest answer is that it depends on the type of matter, how busy the local court is, and above all how much the two people can agree. Here is a general picture of how timing usually works in England and Wales.

Divorce and dissolution

These have fixed minimum waiting periods set by law. There must be at least 20 weeks between the application being issued and the applicant asking for the conditional order. After the conditional order there is a further wait of six weeks and one day before the final order can be applied for. In practice many divorces take longer, often because people wait to sort out their finances first.

Financial matters

If both people agree early and give full information, a financial settlement can sometimes be reached and approved within a few months. Where negotiation takes longer, or the case goes to court, it can take a year or more. Court cases usually pass through a first appointment, a settlement meeting with a judge and, if needed, a final hearing.

Children matters

Many parents agree arrangements within weeks with help from mediation or solicitors. If a court application is made, the court will usually hold a first hearing and may ask for safeguarding checks and reports. Some cases settle at the first hearing. Others take many months, particularly where there are safety concerns that need to be looked into.

Urgent matters

Where someone needs protection from abuse, or a child is at risk of being taken abroad without consent, the court can act very quickly, sometimes on the same day. Tell the firm straight away if your matter is urgent.

What can slow things down

  • One person not replying or not providing financial information
  • Waiting for valuations of property, pensions or a business
  • Court backlogs in some areas
  • Disagreements that need a judge to decide
  • Changes in circumstances, such as a job loss or a house sale falling through

A solicitor will give you an idea of likely timescales once they understand your situation, and will keep you updated as things progress.

Family matters are personal, and you will be sharing a lot with the solicitor you choose. It helps to feel comfortable with them as well as confident in their experience. Here are some things people often consider.

Regulation

Solicitors in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Every firm we introduce is a regulated law firm. Regulation means the firm has to follow professional rules, hold insurance and have a complaints procedure.

Family law experience

Family law has its own procedures and its own court. A firm that does family work every day will know how the local courts run, what judges expect and how to keep costs proportionate. Some solicitors are members of Resolution, an organisation of family lawyers committed to a non confrontational approach. Some also hold accreditation as a specialist in particular areas such as complex finances or children.

Approach

Ask how the solicitor usually works. Some will try hard to settle things through negotiation or mediation, while being ready to go to court if needed. Others are trained in collaborative law. A good family solicitor will be honest about what is realistic and will keep the focus on reaching a workable outcome.

Clear pricing

Ask for an estimate in writing before you agree to anything. A good firm will explain how they charge, what is included and what might cost more.

Questions you might ask

  • Who will deal with my matter day to day?
  • How do you charge, and what is the likely total?
  • How often will you update me?
  • Do you offer mediation, collaborative law or arbitration?
  • What do you need from me to get started?

Family law costs vary a great deal. A straightforward, agreed divorce costs far less than a contested financial case that goes to a final hearing. The biggest factor is usually how much the two people can agree on.

How firms charge

Many family firms offer a fixed fee for well defined work, such as handling an uncontested divorce or drafting a consent order. Work that is harder to predict, such as negotiating a financial settlement or running court proceedings, is usually charged by the hour or in stages, with an estimate for each stage. Some firms offer a first meeting at a fixed price.

Court and other fees

The court charges fees for applications, such as starting a divorce or asking the court to approve a consent order. Other costs might include mediation, property or pension valuations, and barristers if a case goes to a hearing. Court fees change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask the firm. If you have a low income, you may be able to get help with court fees.

Keeping costs down

  • Gather your documents before the first meeting
  • Put your questions in one email rather than several
  • Be open about your finances from the start
  • Think about mediation or other ways of agreeing
  • Ask for regular updates on costs so there are no surprises

In some financial cases where one person has much less access to money than the other, the court can order the wealthier person to pay towards the other's legal fees. A solicitor can explain whether this might apply.

You do not need to have everything ready before you make an enquiry. A family solicitor will guide you through what they need. But a little preparation can make the first conversation more useful and help keep costs down.

Useful information to gather

  • Your marriage or civil partnership certificate, if you have one
  • The dates you married, separated and started living together
  • Your children's names and dates of birth
  • A rough list of property, savings, pensions, debts and income for both of you
  • Any letters, emails or court papers you have received
  • Any agreement you have already reached, even informally

Thinking about what matters to you

It can help to write down what matters most to you, such as staying in the home, keeping the children's routine, or having a clean financial break. Your solicitor will help you understand which of these are likely to be achievable.

Looking after yourself

Separation is stressful. Many people find it helps to talk to friends, family, a GP or a counsellor alongside the legal process. Family solicitors can often recommend local support services, including counselling and parenting programmes.

Keeping records safe

Keep copies of important documents somewhere private and secure. If there are safety concerns, think about who can see your emails, phone and post. Support services can help with safety planning.

Words you may hear

Family law has its own vocabulary, and some terms changed when no fault divorce arrived in 2022. You may still see older words in letters or online, so here is a short guide.

  • Applicant and respondent, the person who applies to court and the person who responds
  • Conditional order, the middle stage of a divorce, previously called the decree nisi
  • Final order, which legally ends the marriage, previously called the decree absolute
  • Financial remedy, the legal term for a financial settlement decided or approved by the court
  • Disclosure, sharing full details of your finances with the other person
  • Parental responsibility, the legal rights and duties a parent has for a child
  • Child arrangements order, a court order about where a child lives and who they spend time with
  • Clean break, an outcome where former partners have no ongoing financial claims against each other

Your solicitor will explain any term you are unsure about. It is always fine to ask them to put something more simply.

Family law in Manchester, questions and answers

Family law enquiries in England and Wales.
I’m worried about my safety. Can you help?
If you are in danger, call 999. For confidential support, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free on 0808 2000 247, 24 hours a day.
Will my partner find out I made an enquiry?
No. We only share your details with the firm we introduce you to, and they contact you, not anyone else.
Do we have to go to court?
Usually not. Consent orders are normally dealt with on paper by a judge, without a hearing. A short hearing is only listed if the judge has questions that cannot be answered in writing. This keeps costs down and means most couples never see the inside of a courtroom. The judge reads the order and the statement of information, and approves it if satisfied it is broadly fair.
Can a consent order deal with children's arrangements?
A financial consent order deals with money and property. Arrangements for where children live and spend time are separate. If parents want a court order about children, that is made in different proceedings, and the court only makes one if it is better for the child than no order. Your solicitor can explain how both fit together.
Can one solicitor act for both of us?
No. A solicitor can only act for one person, because your interests may be different. Often one solicitor drafts the order and the other person takes independent advice from a different firm before signing. This helps protect both people and makes the order less likely to be challenged later. The judge may also be more comfortable approving an order where both people have had the chance to take independent advice.
My ex's solicitor has sent me a draft order. What should I do?
Read it carefully and consider taking independent advice before you sign. Their solicitor acts only for your former partner and cannot advise you. A solicitor can explain what the order means, check it reflects what you agreed and point out anything that might have been missed. Do not feel rushed into signing.
What is a clean break order?
It is a consent order, or part of one, that ends each person's financial claims against the other. It usually also prevents claims against each other's estate after death. A clean break gives certainty, but it is not suitable for everyone, for example where ongoing maintenance is needed. Your solicitor will explain whether it suits you.
Can child maintenance be included?
Yes, if both parents agree. Child maintenance included in a consent order can usually be replaced by a Child Maintenance Service calculation after a year if either parent applies to the service. A solicitor can explain how this works. Including it can give parents more certainty about what will be paid. Child maintenance is separate from time with the children, and one does not depend on the other.
What information is needed to draft the order?
The solicitor will need details of what you have agreed, a summary of both people's assets, debts, income and pensions, your divorce case number, and details of any property and pension providers. If you reached agreement in mediation, the mediator's summary is very helpful. Do not worry if you do not have everything at first. Your solicitor will tell you what is missing.
Can a mediation agreement become a consent order?
Yes. Mediators usually produce a written summary of the proposals reached, along with a summary of each person's finances. A solicitor can use this to draft a consent order and check whether anything important needs to be added or clarified. A mediator's summary is not binding on its own, so it is important that the consent order reflects it accurately. Each person may also want independent advice before signing.
What is an undertaking?
It is a formal promise to the court that is recorded in the order. Common examples include promising to try to remove the other person from the mortgage or to pay certain bills. Breaking an undertaking can have serious consequences, so make sure you understand any you agree to give.
Can I change my child's surname?
If more than one person has parental responsibility, a child's surname is not usually changed without the agreement of all of them or the court's permission. The court looks at what is best for the child. A solicitor can explain how this works. A child's own views may also be relevant, depending on their age and understanding.
Does it matter who the children lived with before we separated?
The court looks at the child's welfare now and in the future, including the likely effect of any change. Who has been the main carer in the past is often relevant to that, but it does not decide the outcome on its own. A solicitor can explain how the court is likely to view your family's history.
Do I have to try mediation first?
Usually you must attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting before applying to court. There are exemptions, such as evidence of domestic abuse, concerns about a child's safety, or genuine urgency. You do not have to continue with mediation if it is not suitable. The mediator will assess whether mediation is suitable for your family.
What is Cafcass?
Cafcass is the service that advises the family courts in England about children's welfare. In Wales the equivalent is Cafcass Cymru. They carry out safeguarding checks, speak to parents and sometimes children, and may write reports for the court. They are independent of both parents. Their role is to focus on the child's welfare.
What is a section 7 report?
It is a report the court can ask for about a child's welfare. It is usually prepared by a Cafcass officer or a local council social worker. It may include interviews with parents and the child and recommendations about arrangements. The court considers it but makes its own decision. Reports can take some weeks.
Will my child have to go to court?
Children very rarely attend court in these cases. Their views are usually gathered by a Cafcass officer or social worker and passed to the court. In some cases an older child may be given the chance to write to or meet the judge, but this is not common. The aim is to keep children out of the dispute.
How long does a court case take?
It varies a great deal. Some cases settle at the first hearing. Others take many months, particularly where reports or a fact finding hearing are needed. Court workloads also differ between areas. A solicitor can give you a better idea once they know your situation. Urgent matters can be dealt with faster.
What happens if the other parent ignores a court order?
You can apply to the court to enforce a child arrangements order. The court can make a range of orders, including changing the arrangements, ordering unpaid work or requiring compensation for financial loss. The court will look at whether there was a reasonable excuse for not following the order. Keep a record of missed contact.
What is a prohibited steps order?
It is a court order that stops a parent, or someone else, from taking a particular step without the court's permission. Common examples include stopping a child being taken out of the country or moved to a different school. In urgent cases it can be made quickly. A solicitor can explain when it might be needed.
Can we write our own separation agreement?
You can, and templates are available. The risk is that a homemade agreement may leave out important points, use unclear wording or lack the safeguards a court looks for. Many couples who start with their own draft ask solicitors to check it and put it into proper form. That can be a cost effective middle ground.
Can civil partners make a separation agreement?
Yes. Civil partners can make a separation agreement in the same way as married couples. It records how the home, money, debts and children will be dealt with while you live apart, and the same safeguards apply. A solicitor can explain how it would work for you. It can later form the basis of a consent order on dissolution.
Who stays in the family home?
That is for the two of you to agree, and the agreement records it. Common arrangements include one person staying with the children, the home being sold, or one person buying the other out. A solicitor can explain how each option might work, but the decision rests on your own circumstances.
Can the agreement cover the mortgage?
Yes. It can say who will pay the mortgage and other costs of the home. It is important to know that your lender is not bound by your agreement. If the mortgage is in joint names, the lender can usually still ask either of you to pay. Speak to your lender if you are worried about keeping up payments.
Can it include maintenance for my spouse?
Yes. Many agreements include regular payments from one person to the other, often for a set period or until a review date. The agreement can say how much, how often and what happens if circumstances change. Your solicitor can explain the factors usually considered. Some agreements also say what will happen to maintenance if the paying person loses their job or the receiving person starts working more.
What about child maintenance?
The agreement can record how much child maintenance will be paid. Even with an agreement in place, either parent can still apply to the Child Maintenance Service. Many parents use the government's online child maintenance calculator as a starting point, and your solicitor can explain how this fits together. It is good to know this from the start.
Can we divide our pensions in a separation agreement?
You can record what you intend to happen, but a pension cannot actually be shared without a court order. That order is made as part of a divorce or dissolution. Your solicitor can explain how to protect your position until then. Some couples ask for pension valuations at this stage so that everyone has a realistic picture.
What happens to our joint debts?
The agreement can say who repays each debt. It does not change your contract with the lender, so if a debt is in joint names, either of you can usually still be pursued. Some couples choose to close or refinance joint debts as part of the arrangement. Your solicitor can explain the practical options.
Are there tax issues to think about?
There can be. Transferring property, shares or other assets between spouses can have tax consequences, and the rules for separating couples have changed over time. Your solicitor may suggest speaking to an accountant or tax adviser before you finalise the agreement. Timing can sometimes affect the tax position, so it is worth raising early.
My partner promised I would always have a home. Does that count?
It might. If a clear promise was made and you relied on it in a significant way, such as giving up your own home or working unpaid in a family business, the law may sometimes give you a remedy. These claims depend heavily on evidence, so try to keep any messages or records.
Can I stay in the home while we sort things out?
That depends on whether you own or rent it and in whose name. A joint owner usually has a right to live there. A partner with no legal ownership may have more limited rights. A solicitor can explain your position, and you should tell them if you have any safety concerns.
My partner moved into my home. Can they claim part of it?
A partner does not gain a share of your home simply by living there. A claim usually needs evidence of a shared intention that they would have an interest, and that they relied on it. Payments towards the mortgage or major works, or promises you made, could be relevant. A solicitor can explain the risk in your situation.
What is a TOLATA claim?
It is a claim under the Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996. It allows the court to decide who owns what share of a property and whether it should be sold. Most cohabitation property disputes are brought this way, in the civil courts rather than the family court.
Do we have to go to court?
No. Many disputes are settled through negotiation or mediation. Courts expect people to try to resolve things before starting proceedings, and the threat of paying the other side's costs encourages settlement. Court is usually a last resort. Settling also gives you more control over the outcome, because you agree terms rather than having them decided for you. Your solicitor can explain the options for resolving things without a hearing.
How long does a cohabitation dispute take?
It varies. Some settle within weeks after an exchange of letters. Contested court cases can take a year or more to reach a final hearing. Your solicitor can give you a better idea once they understand the facts and how the other side is responding. Keeping evidence organised and responding promptly can help avoid delay.
What evidence will I need?
Useful evidence often includes the title documents, any declaration of trust, mortgage statements, bank statements showing payments, and emails or messages about who would own what. Notes of important conversations can help too. Gather what you can, but do not take documents that belong only to your partner. Your solicitor will explain what is relevant.
Can the court order the house to be sold?
Yes, the court can order a sale and decide how the money is divided. It will consider factors such as the purpose for which the property was bought, the welfare of any children living there and the interests of anyone with a mortgage over it. It can also decide how sale costs are shared.
What if my partner refuses to sell?
A joint owner cannot usually force a sale without the other's agreement or a court order. If agreement is not possible, either owner can ask the court to decide. Your solicitor can explain the steps and how long they might take. In the meantime, both owners usually remain responsible for the mortgage. Some couples agree that one person will buy the other out instead of selling.
Can a prenup protect my business?
A prenup can set out how a business owned before the marriage, or a share in a family business, should be treated. This is one of the most common reasons people make one. A valuation of the business may be needed as part of disclosure. The agreement can also deal with any growth in the business during the marriage and whether that is shared.
What happens if we do not have a prenup?
If the marriage ends, the court decides how to divide the finances using a set of factors, including needs, contributions and the length of the marriage. Assets built up during the marriage are often shared. Assets brought in from outside may be treated differently, but there is no certainty. A prenup lets you set out your own view in advance.
How far before the wedding should we sign?
As early as possible. Many solicitors aim for it to be signed at least 28 days before the wedding, and starting several months ahead is common. Signing close to the wedding can suggest pressure and may weaken the agreement. If your wedding is very close, a solicitor can explain the options, which may include signing a postnuptial agreement after the wedding instead.
Do we both need our own solicitor?
It is strongly recommended. One solicitor cannot usually advise both of you because your interests are different. Each of you having independent advice helps show that you both understood what you were agreeing to. The cost of separate advice is usually seen as a worthwhile part of making the agreement robust. Your partner is free to choose their own firm.
Do we have to share full financial details?
Full and frank disclosure is generally recommended. It helps show each person knew what they were agreeing to. Some couples agree to limited disclosure, but that can make the agreement easier to challenge later. Your solicitor will explain the risks. Disclosure is usually exchanged between the two solicitors, and it is kept confidential.
What information will I need to provide?
Usually details of property, savings, investments, pensions, business interests, income and debts, with supporting documents. You may also be asked about expected inheritances or trust interests. Your solicitor will give you a list. Recent statements and valuations are best, and estimates are often fine at the start. If something is hard to value, such as a business or a trust interest, your solicitor will explain how it is usually dealt with.
How long does the process take?
It depends on how complex your finances are and how quickly information is exchanged. A straightforward agreement may take a few weeks of work. Complex agreements can take longer. Starting several months before the wedding gives everyone room. Delays are most often caused by waiting for documents or valuations, so gathering these early can help keep things moving.
Can my parents be involved?
Parents often encourage a prenup to protect family wealth, and they may provide information about trusts or gifts. However, the agreement is between the couple, and each person must decide freely. Too much family pressure can undermine an agreement. Your solicitor will usually speak to you on your own so they can be sure the decisions are yours.
What if we are marrying abroad?
You can still make a prenup in England and Wales. If either of you has links to another country, or you are marrying or living abroad, the agreement may need advice from a lawyer in that country too. Your solicitor will explain the issues. Some countries treat prenups very differently, so it is worth raising this at the start.
