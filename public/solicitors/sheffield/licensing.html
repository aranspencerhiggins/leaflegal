Licensing explained

General information for people and businesses in Sheffield. Pick a topic to read more.

Licensing law decides who can sell alcohol, put on entertainment, serve hot food late at night or carry passengers for hire. In England and Wales most of this work is handled by local councils acting as licensing authorities. For pubs, bars, restaurants, shops, clubs and venues the main law is the Licensing Act 2003. For taxis and private hire vehicles it is a separate set of older laws, along with each council's own policy.

These permissions matter because trading without the right licence, or breaking the conditions on one, can be a criminal offence. A licence can also be one of the most valuable things a hospitality business owns. A bar with a late licence in a busy town centre is worth far more than the same building without one, and a taxi driver without a badge cannot earn a living.

Licensing is a specialist field. The procedures are detailed, the deadlines are strict and a lot depends on local policy and on the relationship between the business and bodies such as the police and environmental health. Many people only ever deal with it once or twice, which is why they often look for a solicitor who handles licensing work every day.

The four licensing objectives

Every decision under the Licensing Act 2003 is made by reference to four licensing objectives. These are the prevention of crime and disorder, public safety, the prevention of public nuisance and the protection of children from harm. Councils, the police and residents have to tie any objection to one or more of these, and the council has to make its decision with them in mind.

Understanding the objectives is the key to most licensing work. A well prepared application shows how the business will promote each of them. A strong response to an objection shows why the concern raised either does not relate to the objectives or can be dealt with by sensible conditions.

Councils are expected to aim to permit licensable activities where they can, while promoting the objectives. In practice that means most applications are granted, often with conditions, and refusal or revocation is usually kept for cases where conditions would not be enough. That does not mean any particular application will succeed, and local policy can make a big difference.

Licensing work falls into a handful of main types. Each has its own page on this site with more detail, but here is a short guide to help you work out which one fits.

New premises licences

A premises licence lets a particular place carry on licensable activities such as selling alcohol, providing regulated entertainment or serving hot food and drink between 11pm and 5am. A new application is needed for a new venue, or for existing premises that have never been licensed or whose licence has lapsed. It involves an operating schedule, a plan of the premises, public notices and a consultation period.

Variations

Once a premises has a licence, changes to hours, layout, activities or conditions usually need a variation. Some small changes can go through a simpler minor variation process. Larger changes follow a process much like a new application.

Transfers and DPS changes

When a business is sold or the operator changes, the premises licence usually has to be transferred to the new holder. When the manager named as designated premises supervisor leaves, a new one has to be put in place. These are routine applications, but timing matters, especially on the sale of a business.

Reviews

A review is where the police, another responsible authority or a member of the public asks the council to look again at a licence because of problems linked to the licensing objectives. The council can do anything from taking no action to revoking the licence. Reviews are often the most serious and urgent kind of licensing matter.

Personal licences

A personal licence is held by an individual and allows them to authorise the sale of alcohol from licensed premises. Questions often arise where the applicant has a criminal conviction, or where a holder is convicted and the council considers suspending or revoking the licence.

Taxi and private hire licences

Drivers, vehicles and operators are licensed by the local council, or by Transport for London in London. Councils can refuse, suspend or revoke licences, sometimes with immediate effect, and drivers often need help quickly to protect their income.

Most general high street firms do not offer licensing work, so it helps to find a firm that does it regularly. Licensing law is closely tied to local practice. Every council has its own statement of licensing policy, and some areas have special policies for busy nightlife districts. A firm that knows how licensing committees work, and how the police licensing teams approach things, can make a real difference to how smoothly a matter goes.

Things worth asking a firm

  • How often they deal with the kind of matter you have
  • Whether they have appeared before your council's licensing committee
  • Who will actually do the work and who will attend any hearing
  • Whether they offer a fixed fee, and what it covers
  • How they handle talks with the police and other authorities
  • How quickly they can act if there is a hearing date or deadline

It is also worth thinking about how the firm communicates. Licensing work often involves short notice requests from the police or council, so you want a firm that replies quickly and explains things in plain terms. For multi site operators, ask whether the firm can handle licences across different councils and keep track of renewals, annual fees and DPS changes for you.

Some licensing solicitors also work with licensing consultants, acoustic experts, security advisers or architects who draw licensing plans. For bigger applications or reviews, the firm may suggest using one of these specialists. Ask what they think is needed and what it would cost before you agree.

Solicitors and other advisers

Licensing is not a reserved legal activity, so some help is offered by non-lawyer consultants. Leaflegal only introduces regulated law firms. A solicitor is regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, carries insurance and is bound by rules on conduct and confidentiality. They can also represent you in the magistrates' court if a matter goes to appeal.

Licensing often overlaps with other areas. A business sale may involve commercial property and company work. A planning issue may sit alongside a licensing application. A taxi driver facing a licensing hearing may also have a related criminal or motoring matter. The firm can tell you if any of these apply and whether they can help.

Do not worry if you are not sure what kind of help you need. Describe the problem in your own words on the form and the firm will work out which process applies.

Licensing costs come in two parts. There are the fees charged by the council, and there are the fees charged by the solicitor if you choose to use one.

Council fees

Premises licence fees under the Licensing Act 2003 are set nationally and usually depend on the rateable value of the premises. There is an application fee and, once granted, an annual fee. Variations, transfers, DPS changes and personal licence applications each have their own fee. Taxi and private hire fees are set locally by each council and can vary a great deal between areas. Fees change from time to time, so check the current figures with your council or on GOV.UK.

Solicitor fees

Many licensing firms offer fixed fees for routine applications such as transfers, DPS changes, minor variations and straightforward new premises licences. Work that depends on how others respond, such as contested applications, reviews and appeals, is often charged on an hourly basis or as a staged fee, with a separate fee for attending a hearing.

Ask the firm what is included. For example, does the fee cover drafting the operating schedule, arranging the newspaper notice, talking to the police and attending a hearing if one is needed? Are council fees, plan drawing and advertising costs included or extra? A good firm will confirm its fees in writing before work starts.

For businesses with several sites, some firms offer retainers or annual packages covering routine changes and compliance checks. These can be more predictable than paying for each piece of work separately, but compare what is included with what you are likely to need.

  • Newspaper advertising for new and full variation applications
  • Accredited training for personal licence applicants
  • Specialist reports such as noise or crowd safety assessments
  • Costs in the magistrates' court if a decision is appealed

Licensing has fixed periods set by law, and councils have to follow them. This means some parts of the process cannot be rushed, however urgent your opening date is.

Applications with a consultation period

New premises licence applications and full variations have a consultation period during which responsible authorities and members of the public can make representations. If none are made, the licence is usually granted shortly afterwards. If representations are made and cannot be resolved, a hearing is arranged, which adds several weeks.

Quicker applications

Temporary event notices have their own deadlines, measured in working days before the event. A late notice gives less time but is limited in number and can be stopped outright by a police or environmental health objection. If an event date is fixed, it is worth checking the deadlines well in advance.

Minor variations, transfers and DPS changes are usually quicker. Transfers and DPS changes can often be given immediate effect on application, so trading can continue while the council processes them.

Reviews and urgent matters

Reviews follow a set timetable that leads to a hearing. Summary reviews brought by the police after serious crime or disorder move much faster, with interim steps considered within days. Taxi licence suspensions can take effect immediately in some cases.

Appeals

Most licensing decisions can be appealed to the magistrates' court, but the time limit is short. In many cases it is 21 days from being told of the decision. If you think you may want to appeal, speak to a solicitor as soon as you receive the decision.

Councils differ in how quickly they process paperwork and list hearings. A local licensing firm will usually know what to expect in your area.

The biggest cause of delay is usually a problem with the paperwork, such as a plan that does not meet the requirements, a notice that was not displayed for the full period or a newspaper advert placed late. If any of these go wrong, the application may have to start again, which resets the clock. Building in some spare time before an opening date is sensible.

Getting organised before you speak to a firm will save time and can reduce costs. You do not need everything, but the more the solicitor can see at the start, the better they can explain where you stand.

It also helps to think about what really matters to you. For a new venue, is it the closing time, the right to have live music or permission for an outdoor area? For a review, is it keeping the licence at any cost or protecting particular hours? Knowing your priorities helps the solicitor focus on what counts.

Do I have to tell the council if I am arrested?
Many councils include a condition on driver licences requiring drivers to report arrests, charges, cautions and convictions within a short period. The exact requirement depends on your council's conditions. Failing to report when required can itself lead to action against your licence. A solicitor can check what your conditions say.
What happens at a licensing hearing?
A sub-committee of councillors, or sometimes an officer, considers the case. A licensing officer usually presents a report, and you or your representative can respond, give evidence and answer questions. The committee decides and must give reasons. Hearings are less formal than court but are taken seriously.
Can I bring someone to the hearing?
Yes. You can usually be represented by a solicitor or bring a friend, family member or interpreter, depending on the council's procedure. Character witnesses can sometimes give evidence or provide written references. Check the council's hearing procedure, which is often sent with the notice of hearing.
How do I appeal a revocation?
Most driver licence decisions can be appealed to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being notified. The court hears the case afresh and can consider new evidence. Some decisions can be appealed further to the Crown Court. The time limit is strict, so speak to a solicitor as soon as you receive the decision.
Can I keep working while I appeal?
If the council's decision was not given immediate effect, you can usually keep working until the appeal period ends or the appeal is decided. If it was given immediate effect on public safety grounds, you cannot work during the appeal. A solicitor can explain your position and whether the appeal can be heard quickly.
What are the risks of appealing?
If the appeal fails, the court can order you to pay the council's legal costs, which can be significant. The court also gives weight to the council's decision and policy. A solicitor will look at the strength of your case and the likely costs before you decide whether to appeal.
Can I apply to a different council instead?
You can apply elsewhere, but you must usually declare previous refusals and revocations, and councils share this information through a national register and safeguarding duties. Failing to disclose them can lead to refusal or revocation. A solicitor can explain how a previous decision may be viewed.
What does a private hire operator licence involve?
An operator licence allows a person or business to accept and arrange private hire bookings. The operator must be fit and proper, keep records of bookings, drivers and vehicles, and use only drivers and vehicles licensed by the same council. Conditions often cover insurance, premises, complaints handling and safeguarding.
Can operators face action because of their drivers?
Yes. If an operator uses unlicensed drivers or vehicles, fails to keep proper records or does not deal with safeguarding concerns, the council can take action against the operator licence. Problems with individual drivers can lead to questions about the operator's systems. A solicitor can help operators review their processes.
Will I have to go to a hearing?
Only if relevant representations are made and not withdrawn, unless everyone agrees a hearing is unnecessary. Many applications are resolved by agreeing conditions with the police or environmental health during the consultation. If a hearing is needed, your solicitor can prepare your case and speak for you.
What can I do to reduce objections?
Speaking to the authorities early, being clear about how the premises will be run and offering sensible conditions often helps. Some operators also meet neighbours to explain their plans. A solicitor can identify likely concerns from the council's policy and the area, and suggest ways to deal with them.
Can the council refuse my application?
Yes, if it decides that refusal is appropriate to promote the licensing objectives. In practice, councils often grant licences with extra conditions or shorter hours rather than refusing outright. The outcome depends on the evidence, local policy and how the application is presented. Nobody can guarantee a particular result.
Can I appeal if my application is refused?
Yes. An applicant can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. Anyone who made a relevant representation can also appeal if they are unhappy with a grant. Appeals take time and carry a costs risk, so your solicitor will explain the options before you decide.
How long does a new premises licence take?
There is a 28 day consultation period after a valid application is made. If there are no representations, the licence is usually issued soon after. If there is a hearing, it is normally held within 20 working days of the end of the consultation, with the decision following. Allow extra time for preparing the documents before you apply.
Can I open before the licence is granted?
You should not carry on licensable activities until the licence is in force. Some operators use temporary event notices for a launch, within the limits that apply, but these can be objected to. Your solicitor can explain what is possible if your opening date is close.
How much is the council fee?
The fee depends on the rateable value band of the premises, and there is an annual fee once the licence is granted. Very large venues may pay more. Fees change from time to time, so check the current figures with the council or on GOV.UK.
How much will a solicitor charge?
Many firms quote a fixed fee for preparing and submitting the application, with a separate fee for attending a hearing if one is needed. More complex applications, such as late venues in busy areas, may cost more. Ask for a written quote and check whether plans and advertising are included.
Does Leaflegal charge for the introduction?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The licensing firm we introduce you to pays us a fee, and that does not change what you pay them. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm.
Can I remove conditions that do not make sense?
Often yes. Unclear, out of date or unenforceable conditions can sometimes be removed by a minor variation. Conditions imposed after a hearing or review may need a full variation and a clear explanation. Your solicitor will look at the history of each condition before suggesting an approach.
Can I add deliveries or off sales?
If your licence only allows alcohol for consumption on the premises, adding sales for consumption off the premises, such as deliveries or takeaway drinks, usually needs a full variation. Conditions about age checks at the point of delivery are common. A solicitor can explain what the council will usually expect.
Do I need a variation to add live music?
Not always. Some live and recorded music in licensed premises has been deregulated within certain hours and audience sizes. Outside those limits, or for other types of entertainment, a variation may be needed. A solicitor can check whether your plans are already covered.
Can I change the times for late night refreshment only?
Yes. Late night refreshment is a separate licensable activity from alcohol, so the hours for hot food and drink can be varied on their own. Extending them still needs the right kind of variation and may attract objections about noise or people gathering. A solicitor can explain how your council usually treats these requests.
Can residents object to a variation?
Yes. Anyone can make a representation about a full variation, and people can also comment on a minor variation. For a full variation, representations must relate to the effect of the changes on the licensing objectives. Complaints about the existing licence as a whole are not normally relevant to a variation.
What happens if the police object?
Police representations are given significant weight because they are the main source of advice on crime and disorder. Many police objections are resolved by agreeing conditions or adjusting the hours requested. If not, the matter goes to a hearing. A solicitor can talk to the police licensing team on your behalf.
Can the council take away my existing hours at a variation hearing?
Generally the committee deals only with the changes applied for and cannot use a variation to take away existing rights. However, it can attach conditions to the varied part of the licence. Serious concerns raised at the hearing could lead someone to apply for a review separately.
Can I appeal a refused full variation?
Yes. An applicant can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. There is no right of appeal against a refused minor variation, but you can apply again as a full variation. Your solicitor will explain the merits and costs risks before you decide.
Can I reapply if my variation is refused?
You can apply again, but a council can treat a repeat application that is essentially the same as one recently refused as repetitive. Changing the proposal meaningfully, such as asking for shorter hours or offering stronger conditions, gives a better basis for a fresh application.
What should a buyer check about the licence?
A buyer will usually want to see the full licence with its plan and conditions, check the annual fee has been paid, confirm the licence matches how the business trades and find out about any reviews, warnings or complaints. Checking the DPS arrangements and any shadow licence is also common. A licensing solicitor can carry out these checks.
How is the transfer timed on a sale?
Often the seller signs the transfer consent before completion, and the buyer's solicitor holds it. On the day of completion, the transfer and DPS change are submitted with a request for immediate effect. This allows the buyer to trade from day one. The licensing solicitor coordinates with the business sale solicitors.
Does a share sale need a transfer?
Not usually. If you buy the company that holds the licence, the holder stays the same. You may still want to change the DPS and update any notified details. You will also inherit the company's licensing history, so it is worth checking the background before you buy.
What is a shadow licence?
A shadow licence is a second premises licence for the same building, usually held by the landlord or pub company. It protects their interest if the tenant's licence is lost. A buyer or tenant should know whether a shadow licence exists, as it can affect their position. A solicitor can explain how it works in your deal.
Should the licence match how the business trades?
Yes. If a seller has been trading later than the licence allows, using areas not on the plan or ignoring conditions, a buyer may be taking on a business that cannot lawfully trade in the same way. Finding this out before completion gives the buyer the chance to negotiate or ask for the problem to be fixed.
What happens if the licence holder dies?
The licence usually lapses on the death of the holder. In certain cases, someone connected to them or with an interest in the premises can give an interim authority notice within a short window, which keeps the licence alive for a limited period while a transfer is arranged. Time limits are strict, so act quickly.
What happens if the licence holder becomes insolvent?
A licence held by an individual who becomes bankrupt, or a company that goes into certain insolvency processes, can lapse. Insolvency practitioners and landlords often need to act quickly, using an interim authority notice or a transfer, to protect the licence. A solicitor can explain the options and deadlines.
Can a lapsed licence be saved?
In some cases, yes, if an interim authority notice or a reinstatement transfer is made within the strict time limits. After those limits pass, the licence is usually lost and a new application is needed. If you think a licence may have lapsed, speak to a licensing solicitor straight away.
Do I need to tell the council if my address changes?
Yes. A premises licence holder must notify the council of a change of name or address, and a DPS must notify changes to their own details. There is usually a small fee. Keeping these details up to date avoids problems with notices and correspondence going missing.
Can I challenge interim steps?
Yes. The licence holder can make representations against the interim steps, and the council must then hold a hearing within a short time to consider them. This can be an opportunity to propose alternative measures that address the police concerns while allowing some trading to continue.
What happens at the full summary review hearing?
The full hearing must take place within 28 days of the council receiving the police application. The committee can take any of the steps available on an ordinary review and must also decide what to do about the interim steps. Thorough preparation in a short period is essential.
Is a closure order the same as a summary review?
No. Closure notices and orders are powers under separate anti-social behaviour laws, used by the police or council to close premises quickly, often with a court hearing within days. A summary review is a licensing process before the council. They can happen together, and a closure order can itself trigger a review. Both need urgent help.
Can my licence be revoked?
Yes. Revocation is the most serious outcome and is available on any review. Committees must decide what is appropriate to promote the licensing objectives, and many reviews end with conditions or management changes rather than revocation. National guidance treats some criminal activity, such as drug dealing or knowingly employing illegal workers, particularly seriously.
How long can a licence be suspended?
A committee can suspend a licence for up to three months. During a suspension, the premises cannot carry on the licensable activities covered by the suspension. Suspension is sometimes used as a deterrent or to give the operator time to put changes in place.
Can the committee remove my DPS?
Yes. Removing the DPS is one of the options available on a review, often where the committee has concerns about how the premises has been managed. The licence holder would then need to apply to name a new DPS before alcohol sales can resume.
Can I appeal the decision?
Yes. The licence holder can appeal to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being told of the decision. The review applicant and those who made relevant representations can appeal too. On an ordinary review, the decision usually does not take effect while an appeal is pending. A solicitor will explain the merits and costs risks.
What does the court look at on appeal?
The magistrates' court rehears the case and can consider evidence that was not before the committee, including events since the hearing. It gives weight to the council's decision, and the appellant usually has to persuade the court that the decision was wrong. The court can confirm, change or overturn the decision.
Will a review affect the sale of my business?
It can. A buyer will want to know about any review, its outcome and any conditions added. A revoked licence would usually need a new application. Even a review that ends with no action may be raised in due diligence. A solicitor can explain how the review is likely to be seen by buyers or landlords.
