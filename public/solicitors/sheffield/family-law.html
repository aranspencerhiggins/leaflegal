Family law explained

General information for people and businesses in Sheffield. Pick a topic to read more.

Family law deals with the legal side of relationships, separation and children. It covers how a marriage or civil partnership is ended, how money and property are divided, where children live and how they spend time with each parent, and the agreements couples make before or during a relationship. In England and Wales most of this work is handled by the Family Court, although the large majority of families settle things without a judge ever making a decision.

People usually come to family law at a difficult time. A relationship may have just ended, or it may have been ending slowly for years. There may be children who need stability, a home that one or both people want to keep, and worries about money. It is normal to feel overwhelmed. A family solicitor deals with these situations every day and can help you understand where things stand and what the next steps usually look like.

Family law is also about planning ahead. Couples who are about to marry, move in together or buy a home sometimes take advice to record what they intend, so that there is less to argue about if things go wrong later.

If you are worried about your safety

If you or your children are at risk of harm from a partner or family member, your safety comes first. In an emergency call the police on 999. You can also contact a domestic abuse support service such as the National Domestic Abuse Helpline, which is free and confidential. A family solicitor can explain the protective orders the court can make, but they are not an emergency service.

Paid help and legal aid

Legal aid is only available for some family matters, usually where there is evidence of domestic abuse or a risk to a child, and it depends on your income and savings. GOV.UK has a tool to check whether you might qualify. Leaflegal introduces people who are looking for paid help from a private family law firm. We are not a law firm and we do not give legal advice.

There is more than one way to sort out a family matter. Going to court is usually the last resort, and the courts expect people to think about other options first. A solicitor will explain which routes might suit your situation, and many families use more than one.

Agreeing between yourselves

Some couples talk things through and reach an agreement on their own, then ask a solicitor to check it and put it into the right legal form. This can be the quickest and cheapest route where both people are calm, honest about their finances and broadly agree.

Solicitor negotiation

Each person has their own solicitor, who exchanges information and proposals by letter or email. The solicitors can help keep discussions focused and make sure nothing important is missed. Most financial settlements are reached this way or through mediation.

Mediation

A trained, neutral mediator helps both of you talk and work towards an agreement. The mediator does not take sides or make decisions. Before applying to court about children or finances, you will usually need to attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting, known as a MIAM, unless an exemption applies. Mediation may not be suitable where there has been domestic abuse.

Collaborative law

Each person has a specially trained collaborative lawyer, and everyone meets together to work things out. Both people usually sign an agreement not to go to court, so if the process breaks down they need to instruct new solicitors.

Arbitration and private hearings

An arbitrator, usually an experienced family lawyer, makes a decision that both people agree in advance to accept. Some couples also pay for a private early neutral evaluation, where an experienced lawyer gives a view on the likely outcome. These options can be quicker and more private than court, though both people pay the arbitrator or evaluator.

Court

Where agreement is not possible, or where someone needs urgent protection, either person can apply to the Family Court. Court proceedings follow set stages and can take many months. Even once a case is started, many families still reach agreement before the final hearing.

One of the first questions people ask is how long it will all take. The honest answer is that it depends on the type of matter, how busy the local court is, and above all how much the two people can agree. Here is a general picture of how timing usually works in England and Wales.

Divorce and dissolution

These have fixed minimum waiting periods set by law. There must be at least 20 weeks between the application being issued and the applicant asking for the conditional order. After the conditional order there is a further wait of six weeks and one day before the final order can be applied for. In practice many divorces take longer, often because people wait to sort out their finances first.

Financial matters

If both people agree early and give full information, a financial settlement can sometimes be reached and approved within a few months. Where negotiation takes longer, or the case goes to court, it can take a year or more. Court cases usually pass through a first appointment, a settlement meeting with a judge and, if needed, a final hearing.

Children matters

Many parents agree arrangements within weeks with help from mediation or solicitors. If a court application is made, the court will usually hold a first hearing and may ask for safeguarding checks and reports. Some cases settle at the first hearing. Others take many months, particularly where there are safety concerns that need to be looked into.

Urgent matters

Where someone needs protection from abuse, or a child is at risk of being taken abroad without consent, the court can act very quickly, sometimes on the same day. Tell the firm straight away if your matter is urgent.

What can slow things down

  • One person not replying or not providing financial information
  • Waiting for valuations of property, pensions or a business
  • Court backlogs in some areas
  • Disagreements that need a judge to decide
  • Changes in circumstances, such as a job loss or a house sale falling through

A solicitor will give you an idea of likely timescales once they understand your situation, and will keep you updated as things progress.

Family matters are personal, and you will be sharing a lot with the solicitor you choose. It helps to feel comfortable with them as well as confident in their experience. Here are some things people often consider.

Regulation

Solicitors in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Every firm we introduce is a regulated law firm. Regulation means the firm has to follow professional rules, hold insurance and have a complaints procedure.

Family law experience

Family law has its own procedures and its own court. A firm that does family work every day will know how the local courts run, what judges expect and how to keep costs proportionate. Some solicitors are members of Resolution, an organisation of family lawyers committed to a non confrontational approach. Some also hold accreditation as a specialist in particular areas such as complex finances or children.

Approach

Ask how the solicitor usually works. Some will try hard to settle things through negotiation or mediation, while being ready to go to court if needed. Others are trained in collaborative law. A good family solicitor will be honest about what is realistic and will keep the focus on reaching a workable outcome.

Clear pricing

Ask for an estimate in writing before you agree to anything. A good firm will explain how they charge, what is included and what might cost more.

Questions you might ask

  • Who will deal with my matter day to day?
  • How do you charge, and what is the likely total?
  • How often will you update me?
  • Do you offer mediation, collaborative law or arbitration?
  • What do you need from me to get started?

Family law costs vary a great deal. A straightforward, agreed divorce costs far less than a contested financial case that goes to a final hearing. The biggest factor is usually how much the two people can agree on.

How firms charge

Many family firms offer a fixed fee for well defined work, such as handling an uncontested divorce or drafting a consent order. Work that is harder to predict, such as negotiating a financial settlement or running court proceedings, is usually charged by the hour or in stages, with an estimate for each stage. Some firms offer a first meeting at a fixed price.

Court and other fees

The court charges fees for applications, such as starting a divorce or asking the court to approve a consent order. Other costs might include mediation, property or pension valuations, and barristers if a case goes to a hearing. Court fees change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask the firm. If you have a low income, you may be able to get help with court fees.

Keeping costs down

  • Gather your documents before the first meeting
  • Put your questions in one email rather than several
  • Be open about your finances from the start
  • Think about mediation or other ways of agreeing
  • Ask for regular updates on costs so there are no surprises

In some financial cases where one person has much less access to money than the other, the court can order the wealthier person to pay towards the other's legal fees. A solicitor can explain whether this might apply.

You do not need to have everything ready before you make an enquiry. A family solicitor will guide you through what they need. But a little preparation can make the first conversation more useful and help keep costs down.

Useful information to gather

  • Your marriage or civil partnership certificate, if you have one
  • The dates you married, separated and started living together
  • Your children's names and dates of birth
  • A rough list of property, savings, pensions, debts and income for both of you
  • Any letters, emails or court papers you have received
  • Any agreement you have already reached, even informally

Thinking about what matters to you

It can help to write down what matters most to you, such as staying in the home, keeping the children's routine, or having a clean financial break. Your solicitor will help you understand which of these are likely to be achievable.

Looking after yourself

Separation is stressful. Many people find it helps to talk to friends, family, a GP or a counsellor alongside the legal process. Family solicitors can often recommend local support services, including counselling and parenting programmes.

Keeping records safe

Keep copies of important documents somewhere private and secure. If there are safety concerns, think about who can see your emails, phone and post. Support services can help with safety planning.

Words you may hear

Family law has its own vocabulary, and some terms changed when no fault divorce arrived in 2022. You may still see older words in letters or online, so here is a short guide.

  • Applicant and respondent, the person who applies to court and the person who responds
  • Conditional order, the middle stage of a divorce, previously called the decree nisi
  • Final order, which legally ends the marriage, previously called the decree absolute
  • Financial remedy, the legal term for a financial settlement decided or approved by the court
  • Disclosure, sharing full details of your finances with the other person
  • Parental responsibility, the legal rights and duties a parent has for a child
  • Child arrangements order, a court order about where a child lives and who they spend time with
  • Clean break, an outcome where former partners have no ongoing financial claims against each other

Your solicitor will explain any term you are unsure about. It is always fine to ask them to put something more simply.

What happens to belongings like the car or furniture?
Generally, items belong to whoever bought them or was given them, though shared purchases can be harder to untangle. Most couples resolve this through discussion because the value rarely justifies court action. A solicitor can help you approach it sensibly. Cars on finance can be more complex, because the finance agreement is with the lender. Pets are usually treated as belongings in law, though many couples agree arrangements that suit everyone.
What is a letter of claim?
It is a letter sent before court proceedings that sets out the claim, the facts relied on and what is being asked for. The other side is given a reasonable time to reply. Courts expect this step, and failing to take it can affect who pays costs later. It is usually prepared by a solicitor.
Can I protect my interest while the dispute is going on?
In some cases a solicitor can register a restriction or notice at HM Land Registry, which can make it harder for the property to be sold or mortgaged without you knowing. Whether this is possible depends on the circumstances. Joint owners may also be able to change how they hold the property.
Can I claim money for our children?
Yes. Child maintenance is usually arranged through the Child Maintenance Service or by agreement. In addition, a parent can sometimes apply to the court for further orders for the child's benefit, such as housing or a lump sum, under Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989. These claims sit alongside any property dispute.
Can I stay in the home because of the children?
The court can sometimes order that a home is provided for a child until they finish their education, after which it may return to the other parent. This is for the child's benefit rather than the parent's. A solicitor can explain whether it is realistic in your case. Each case depends on the family's circumstances.
Does an unmarried father have parental responsibility?
An unmarried father usually has parental responsibility if he is named on the birth certificate of a child registered after 1 December 2003. Otherwise he can gain it by agreement with the mother or by court order. Parental responsibility is separate from questions about money. A solicitor can explain how it can be obtained if needed.
Can I claim maintenance for myself?
Generally no. Unlike married couples, cohabiting partners cannot usually claim maintenance for themselves when they separate. Any claim for money is normally made for the benefit of children. Your solicitor can explain what might be available in your situation. In some cases, orders for the children may indirectly help the parent who cares for them, for example by providing a home. A solicitor can explain how this might apply to you.
How are arrangements for the children decided?
Most parents agree arrangements themselves or with the help of mediation. If that is not possible, a parent can apply for a child arrangements order, usually after attending a mediation information meeting. The court's main concern is the child's welfare. Most parents agree without a court ever being involved. A solicitor can explain the process if agreement is not possible.
Does it matter if the children are not both of ours?
Claims under Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989 are usually made against a child's legal parent. If a child is not your partner's child, claims against them for that child are more limited. Every family is different, so a solicitor will want to understand who is in your household.
Can we use an online template?
Templates exist, but a prenup is only as good as the process behind it. Without independent advice and proper disclosure, a template agreement may carry little weight. Many couples use a template as a starting point for discussion and ask solicitors to prepare the final version. A solicitor can also check whether a template covers everything that matters to you.
What if my partner does not want a prenup?
Nobody can be forced to sign one, and pressure can undermine an agreement. Many couples find the conversation easier once they understand that a prenup can be balanced and protect both people. A solicitor can explain how the process usually works, which may help you both decide. In the end it is a decision for both of you.
Can a prenup deal with children?
A prenup cannot decide where children will live or limit the financial support a child is entitled to. A court will always put children's welfare first. An agreement can recognise that children may change things and include a review when they are born. Many couples find it reassuring to see the children's needs expressly protected in the agreement.
Can it cover maintenance?
Yes, a prenup can say whether either person would pay maintenance and on what terms. Courts look carefully at these provisions, especially after a long marriage or where one person has given up work to care for children. Meeting real needs remains important. Some agreements set out a fixed sum or period, while others leave maintenance to be decided based on needs at the time. Your solicitor can explain the approach that suits your circumstances.
Can it cover the family home?
Yes. A prenup can say who would keep the home, how it would be sold and how deposits or contributions would be treated. Because the home is often needed to house children, the court will still consider whether the terms meet everyone's needs. Some agreements treat the home as shared even if other assets are kept separate.
Can a prenup cover pensions?
Yes, it can say how pensions built up before and during the marriage would be treated. Pensions can be complex to value and divide. Your solicitor may suggest specialist input if pensions are a significant part of the finances. Some couples agree that pensions built up before the marriage stay separate while those built up during it are shared. A court order would still be needed to actually divide a pension on divorce.
Can it decide what happens if one of us dies?
A prenup deals mainly with what happens on divorce. What happens on death is dealt with through wills. Many couples review or make wills alongside their prenup, especially if they want to protect children from a previous relationship. Marriage usually cancels an existing will in England and Wales, unless it was made in expectation of that marriage. That is another reason many couples deal with wills at the same time as their prenup.
Can we include a sunset clause?
Some agreements say they will end or change after a certain number of years. Others include review dates instead. Your solicitor can explain the pros and cons of each approach. A sunset clause can give certainty but may also mean the protection ends when it is still wanted. A review clause is more flexible because it asks you to look at the agreement again rather than ending it automatically. The right choice depends on your aims.
Can a prenup deal with debts?
Yes. It can record who is responsible for debts each person brings into the marriage, such as student loans or business borrowing, and how debts taken on during the marriage would be shared. It does not change what you owe a lender, which depends on whose name the debt is in.
Do civil partners have the same financial rights as married couples?
Yes. Civil partners can make the same financial claims as spouses, including claims for property, lump sums, maintenance and pension sharing. The court considers the same factors as it would in a divorce. Your solicitor can explain how these apply to your own circumstances and what a fair outcome might look like.
Does dissolution decide who gets the house?
No. The dissolution only ends the legal relationship. What happens to the home, savings and pensions is decided separately, either by agreement turned into a consent order or by the court on a financial application. Without an order, claims can remain open. A solicitor can explain the options for the home.
What is a consent order?
A consent order is a court order that records a financial agreement between civil partners. It is drafted by a solicitor, signed by both of you and approved by a judge after the conditional order. Once approved, it is legally binding and can be enforced. It can also achieve a clean break so no further claims can be made.
Do we need a financial order if we have very little?
Many solicitors recommend one even where assets are modest. Without a clean break order, a former civil partner may be able to make a claim in future, for example if one of you later receives an inheritance or builds up savings. A consent order can be relatively straightforward where finances are simple.
Can I lose my right to a financial claim?
Yes, in some circumstances. If you form a new civil partnership or marry before making a financial application, you may lose the right to make certain claims. There are also practical risks in delaying, as evidence becomes harder to gather. Your solicitor can explain how to protect your position. Acting early usually helps.
What happens to pensions?
Pensions can be shared through a pension sharing order, which transfers part of one person's pension to the other. They can also be offset against other assets. Pension values can be hard to compare, and specialist reports are sometimes needed. Your solicitor can explain the options and what information to gather.
What if my civil partner hides money?
Each person has a duty to give full and frank financial disclosure. If one person hides assets, the court can draw conclusions, make orders for further information and, in some cases, set aside an agreement made on false information. If you have concerns, tell your solicitor early so they can explain the steps available.
Can I stay in our home while things are sorted out?
A civil partner who does not legally own the home usually has home rights, which means a right to occupy it while the civil partnership continues. These rights can be protected by registering them at HM Land Registry. If there are safety concerns, the court can make orders about who lives in the home. A solicitor can explain your position.
How are arrangements for children decided?
Most parents agree arrangements themselves or with the help of mediation. If agreement is not possible, a parent or someone with parental responsibility can apply to court for a child arrangements order, usually after a mediation information meeting. The court's main concern is the child's welfare, and it will look at the child's needs and wishes.
What is the difference between the conditional order and the final order?
The conditional order is the middle stage. It confirms the court sees no reason why you cannot divorce, and it allows a financial order to be approved. The final order is the document that legally ends the marriage. You remain married until the final order is made. Keep both documents safe.
Should I wait to apply for the final order?
Many people do, but it depends on your circumstances. Solicitors often discuss delaying the final order until a financial order is approved, to protect pension and other rights that depend on being married. Your solicitor will explain whether this matters in your case. You should not feel rushed, but very long delays can cause their own complications, so discuss the timing with your solicitor.
What happens if neither of us applies for the final order?
You stay married. If more than 12 months pass after the conditional order, the applicant will usually need to explain the delay when they apply. If the applicant does not apply, the respondent can usually apply after a further three months have passed. Some people discover years later that they were never finally divorced. A solicitor can help complete the process.
Can we stop the divorce if we get back together?
Yes. You can withdraw the application at any point before the final order is made. If you are unsure, your solicitor can explain how to pause or end the process. Some couples choose to pause the process while they try to reconcile, rather than withdrawing it straight away. Counselling services can help couples who want to try again.
When can I remarry?
Only once the final order has been made. Remarrying before resolving your finances can also limit your own ability to make financial claims later, so many people take advice before setting a date. If you are planning a wedding, allow enough time for the final order to be made, as court processing times can vary.
Can the 20 week period be shortened?
Only in very limited circumstances. The court can shorten the 20 week period if the applicant shows a good reason, but this is rare and is decided case by case. In most divorces the full 20 weeks applies. A solicitor can explain whether there is any realistic basis for asking.
Does the divorce decide who gets the house?
No. The divorce ends the marriage but does not divide property. That is dealt with through a financial agreement or financial court proceedings. Until then, ownership stays as it is on the title deeds, whatever happens with the divorce. If you do not own the home but live there, you may be able to protect your right to stay. A solicitor can explain how.
Can I be made to leave the family home?
Getting divorced does not by itself mean you have to leave. Both spouses usually have a right to live in the family home until the final order, or until the court decides otherwise. If there are concerns about safety, the court can make orders about who lives there. A solicitor can explain your position.
Do I need a financial order if we have no assets?
Even couples with little money sometimes choose a consent order with a clean break. This stops either person making a claim later if their circumstances change, for example after an inheritance or a pay rise. A solicitor can explain whether it is worth considering in your situation. The cost is often modest compared with the protection it gives.
Are pensions included?
Yes. Pensions are often one of the most valuable assets, and can be shared, offset against other assets or attached. Pensions built up before the marriage may be treated differently from those built up during it. Your solicitor will explain the options. Pensions are sometimes overlooked because their value is not obvious, so make sure you have up to date valuations before any agreement is reached.
Is an inheritance protected?
Not always. Inherited money and assets may be treated differently from assets built up together, but they can still be shared if they are needed to meet either person's needs or have been mixed with family money. How an inheritance is treated depends on the facts of each case. Expected future inheritances are usually treated differently.
What happens to my business?
A business may need to be valued, and the court will consider how much can be taken from it without harming it. Options include one person keeping the business and the other receiving other assets. Your solicitor will explain how business assets are usually dealt with. Valuations can take time, so it helps to start early. Shares in a family business, partnerships and farms can raise particular issues.
What happens to joint debts?
Joint debts, such as a mortgage or loan, usually remain the responsibility of both people as far as the lender is concerned. A settlement can decide who repays what between you, but it does not change the agreement with the lender unless the lender agrees. It can help to tell lenders about the separation and keep paying while things are resolved, to protect both credit records.
What about savings or property abroad?
Assets abroad are still part of the financial picture and must be disclosed. Enforcing an order over property in another country can be more complicated. A solicitor with international experience can explain how these assets are usually dealt with. The court can still take them into account, and can make orders against a person in England and Wales even if the property is overseas. Valuations and currency changes may need to be considered.
Can I keep my pension if my spouse keeps the house?
This is called offsetting, and it is a common way of dealing with pensions. It can work well, but comparing a pension with a share of a house is not straightforward, because they are very different kinds of asset. A solicitor, and sometimes a pension expert, can help you understand what you would be giving up.
What is spousal maintenance?
It is regular payments from one former spouse to the other to help meet their income needs. It may be ordered for a fixed period or, less commonly, for longer. The court will consider whether the receiving person can adjust to independence over time. How much is paid depends on needs, income and earning capacity. It is not paid automatically, and in many cases there is a clean break instead.
How is child maintenance dealt with?
Child maintenance is usually arranged between parents or through the Child Maintenance Service, which uses a set formula. In some cases child maintenance can be included in a court order by agreement. Your solicitor can explain how it fits alongside a financial settlement. Child maintenance is separate from time with the children, and one does not depend on the other.
Can maintenance be changed later?
Usually yes. Maintenance orders can generally be varied if either person's circumstances change significantly. Some orders include terms that prevent the length being extended. Maintenance for a former spouse normally ends if they remarry. Changes are usually made by agreement or by applying to the court, and the court looks at all the circumstances, including any change in income, health or living arrangements. A solicitor can explain whether a variation might be possible.
