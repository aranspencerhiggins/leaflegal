Choosing a solicitor in Norwich

The right firm for you depends on the kind of matter you have, not just on being close by. A conveyancing firm on the high street in Norwich may not handle a contested licence review, and a family law specialist will not usually deal with a speeding charge. That is why we ask what your matter is about before we introduce anyone.

It is worth asking any firm how it charges before work starts. Many matters are handled for a fixed fee, while others are charged by the hour. A good firm will explain the likely total, what could make it change and what is included, and confirm it in writing.

You can check that a solicitor is regulated on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and that a licensed conveyancer is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Every firm we introduce in Norwich and Norfolk is regulated, and you are free to decide whether to instruct them.

How location is used

When you make an enquiry, we ask where you are, or where the property, court or premises is. We use that to introduce a firm that covers your area. For some matters that means a firm close to you. For others it means a firm that works across England and Wales and is used to handling matters wherever the client lives.

Using the local pages

It is natural to want a solicitor who is close by. For some kinds of matter that is useful. For others, the location of the firm makes very little difference to the service you receive.

When local knowledge can help

Some matters involve a particular court, council or local authority. A motoring offence is usually heard at a magistrates' court in the area where the offence happened. A premises licence is applied for to the local council where the premises is, and the council's licensing policy and the views of local police and residents can matter. A family dispute may be heard at the local family court. In these cases a firm that knows the local procedures and people can be an advantage, and attending a hearing is easier if the firm is not far away.

When location matters less

Conveyancing, lease extensions and business debt recovery are now mostly handled by phone, email, post and online portals. Property searches and Land Registry applications are done electronically, county court claims for money are often issued online, and documents can be signed and returned without visiting an office. Many people use a firm in a different town and never meet in person, and are perfectly happy with the service.

Meeting in person

If meeting face to face matters to you, say so when you make your enquiry. Some people find it easier to discuss personal matters, such as a separation, in person. Others are more comfortable with video calls. Many firms offer both, and you can ask what the firm usually does on the first call.

Getting the balance right

In the end, experience in your type of matter, clear communication and fair fees usually matter more than the distance to the office. A nearby firm that rarely handles your kind of matter may not be the best choice, while a firm a few towns away that does it every day may suit you well. The firm we introduce handles your type of work and covers your area.

Area by area

Conveyancing firms routinely act for buyers and sellers all over England and Wales. What matters more is that the firm is on your lender's panel and responds quickly. Local knowledge can help with things like unusual local searches or properties affected by mining or flood risk, but a good conveyancer will know how to deal with those wherever the property is.

Lease extension work is mostly paperwork and negotiation with the freeholder's solicitor, supported by a valuation from a surveyor. The surveyor usually needs to know the local property market, but the solicitor can be based anywhere. If the matter goes to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), hearings are often held remotely or at a regional venue.

Family solicitors often deal with the family court that covers the area where you or your children live. Many hearings now take place by video, but some are in person. Some people prefer a firm they can visit, especially during a difficult separation.

Motoring and licensing cases are the most local. Magistrates' courts, council licensing committees and local police all have their own ways of working. A firm that regularly appears before them can be a real help, and it may be able to attend a hearing at short notice.

Business debt recovery is usually handled through letters, negotiation and, if needed, the county court. Many money claims are issued and managed online, so the firm's location matters less than its experience with commercial debts.

Wales

England and Wales share one legal system, so firms in England can usually act for clients in Wales and the other way round. Some differences do apply in Wales, such as Land Transaction Tax instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax on property purchases, and some housing and planning rules. If you would like to deal with a firm in Welsh, mention it in the notes and the firm will tell you whether it can help.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, so we do not cover matters there for conveyancing, family law, motoring offences, licensing, lease extensions or debt recovery. Immigration introductions are available across the whole of the UK.

Whether a firm is round the corner or at the other end of the country, it is worth checking it before you instruct it. This is easy to do and takes a few minutes.

Check the register

Solicitors and law firms in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can search for them on the SRA register. Licensed conveyancers are regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers, which has its own register. Regulated immigration advisers can be checked with their regulator, such as the SRA or the Immigration Advice Authority.

Look for the regulatory information

Regulated law firms must show certain information on their websites, including who regulates them and their registration number. Many firms display the SRA's digital badge, which links to the firm's entry on the register. If a firm's website does not say who regulates it, ask.

Check the people, not just the firm

Ask who will handle your matter day to day. That might be a solicitor, a chartered legal executive, a licensed conveyancer or a paralegal working under supervision. All of these can be appropriate. It is reasonable to ask about their experience and who supervises them.

Read the paperwork

Before any work starts, the firm should send a client care letter and terms of business. These set out the fees, who will do the work, how complaints are handled and, if you were introduced by Leaflegal, the fee the firm pays for the introduction. Read them carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

Every firm we introduce is regulated

Leaflegal only introduces firms that are regulated for the work they do. Checking for yourself is still a good habit, and a good firm will be happy for you to do so.

Warning signs

Be cautious if anyone offering legal help will not say who regulates them, asks you to pay large sums before explaining their fees in writing, promises a particular result or pressures you to decide on the spot. Be especially careful with emails asking you to send money for a property purchase to new bank details. Always check bank details by phone using a number you already know before sending money to a firm.

Does a local firm cost more or less

Legal fees vary between firms, and location is only one factor. Firms in some large cities may have higher overheads, but that does not always mean higher prices for a particular piece of work. Fixed fee work, such as many house purchases, can be priced similarly across the country. The best way to understand the cost is to ask the firm for a written estimate or quote.

Travel and attendance

Where a solicitor needs to attend a court or hearing, the cost of travel and waiting time may be included in a fixed fee or charged separately. A local firm may charge less for travel to a local court. Ask how attendance is charged so you can compare like for like.

Disbursements

On top of the firm's own fees there may be disbursements, such as court fees, search fees, Land Registry fees, licensing application fees set by the council, or a barrister's fee. Official fees change from time to time, and GOV.UK lists many of them. The firm will tell you which apply.

Comparing quotes

If you speak to more than one firm, check that each quote covers the same work and includes the same items. A lower headline fee may leave out things that another firm includes. Ask each firm to confirm what is and is not included.

Legal questions from people in Norwich

When will I receive the money from my sale?
On completion, your conveyancer receives the buyer's money, pays off your mortgage and any other charges, deducts their fees and any estate agent's fee you have authorised, and sends you the balance. This is usually sent the same day or the next working day, depending on timing and bank processes.
Who pays the estate agent?
You pay the estate agent under the agreement you signed with them. Many sellers ask their conveyancer to pay the agent from the sale money on completion, which is convenient. Your conveyancer will need the agent's invoice before completion to include it in the completion statement.
What happens to my buildings insurance?
Keep your buildings insurance in place until completion. Even though the buyer may arrange their own cover from exchange, the property is still yours until completion, and your lender will expect cover to continue until the mortgage is paid off. Cancel it only once completion has happened.
What happens if the buyer does not complete on time?
If the buyer fails to complete on the agreed date, the contract usually allows you to charge interest and, after serving a notice, to end the contract and keep the deposit. You may also be able to claim further losses. Your conveyancer will explain your position under the contract if it happens.
Will I owe tax when I sell?
Selling your only or main home is often free of Capital Gains Tax, but tax can apply to second homes, rented properties, homes with large grounds or homes used partly for business. There can be a short reporting deadline. Tax rules change, so speak to an accountant or check GOV.UK for your situation.
Why must I sign a transfer deed?
The transfer deed is the document that actually moves legal ownership from you to the buyer. Your conveyancer will send it for you to sign in front of an independent witness, or electronically if the firm uses that method. It is held until completion, then sent to the buyer's conveyancer so they can register the buyer as owner.
Can I sell a property I inherited?
Yes, but the personal representatives usually need a grant of probate or letters of administration before the sale can complete. Marketing and agreeing a sale can often start earlier. The executors sign the documents, and the buyer's conveyancer will check that they have authority to sell.
Should I talk to my child about the court case?
Many parents find it helps to reassure children that they are loved and that the adults are sorting things out, without sharing details of the dispute or asking them to choose. Cafcass and other organisations publish guidance for parents on talking to children about separation. Your solicitor can point you to it.
Can I take my child on holiday abroad?
If a child arrangements order says the child lives with you, you can usually take them abroad for up to one month without the other parent's consent, unless the order says otherwise. Otherwise you generally need the written consent of everyone with parental responsibility, or the court's permission. Taking a child abroad without the right consent can be a criminal offence.
What if I think my child will be taken abroad without my consent?
This can be urgent. A solicitor can explain the orders the court can make to prevent removal, which can be obtained very quickly. If you believe a child is about to be taken out of the country, contact the police immediately. Keep copies of your child's passport details, recent photographs and the other parent's contact details abroad, as these can help the police and the court act quickly.
Can I move to another part of the UK with my children?
Moving within the UK does not always need the other parent's consent, but a significant move that affects their time with the children may lead them to apply to court. The court will focus on the child's welfare. A solicitor can explain how this might apply to you. Discussing plans early often helps.
Can I move abroad permanently with my children?
A permanent move abroad usually needs the consent of everyone with parental responsibility or the court's permission. The court looks closely at the reasons for the move, the plans for the child and the effect on their relationship with the other parent. These are complex cases where advice is important.
Can the other parent take our child abroad without asking me?
Usually not, unless they have a child arrangements order saying the child lives with them, in which case trips of up to one month are generally allowed. If you are worried about a planned trip, a solicitor can explain the options, including asking the court to set conditions. Holiday dates are often agreed in advance.
How much does a children case cost?
It depends on whether agreement is reached and how far a court case goes. Many firms charge hourly or in stages, and some offer fixed fees for particular steps. There is also a court fee for most applications. Ask for a written estimate. Leaflegal is free to use, and the firm pays us for the introduction.
Will I definitely be banned?
A conviction for drug driving carries an obligatory ban of at least 12 months, or at least three years if you have a relevant previous conviction within ten years. The court can only decide not to ban if special reasons are found, which is rare. If the medical defence succeeds, there is no conviction and so no ban.
How long will the ban be?
The court uses the Sentencing Council guideline. It looks at factors such as whether more than one drug was found, whether alcohol was also involved, the standard of driving and your previous record. The minimum is 12 months, and more serious cases attract longer bans. A solicitor can give a realistic range once they see the evidence.
Can my ban be reduced with a course?
The drink drive rehabilitation course is not usually available for drug driving. That means people convicted of drug driving should generally expect to serve the full ban. The court can, however, take into account personal mitigation and an early guilty plea when deciding the length of the ban within the guideline range.
What happens at the first hearing?
You will usually be asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you that day after hearing the facts and your mitigation, or adjourn for a report. If you plead not guilty, the court sets a trial date and timetable for evidence. A solicitor can attend with you and explain what is happening.
Could I go to prison?
A custodial sentence is possible but is usually kept for the most serious cases, such as those involving very poor driving, an accident or repeat offences. Many people receive a ban and a fine, and some receive a community order. A solicitor will look at the guideline and explain where your case is likely to sit.
Can I drive while I wait for the result?
Usually, yes. Unless a court has banned you or your bail conditions say otherwise, you can normally continue to drive while the blood sample is analysed and until the court decides the case. You still have to stay within the law, and a further allegation while you wait would be treated seriously. A solicitor can explain anything specific to your situation.
Can I appeal a drug driving conviction?
There is usually a right of appeal from the magistrates' court to the Crown Court against conviction, sentence or both. The time limit is short, normally 21 days from sentence. In some cases you can ask for the ban to be suspended until the appeal is heard. A solicitor can look at what happened and explain whether an appeal is realistic.
How long does a full variation take?
There is a 28 day consultation period. If there are no unresolved representations, the variation is usually granted shortly after. If a hearing is needed, it is normally held within 20 working days of the end of the consultation. Allow time beforehand for preparing the application and any plans.
What notice do I need to display?
For a minor variation, a white notice is displayed at the premises for a set period. For a full variation, a blue notice is displayed for 28 consecutive days, and a notice must also be published in a local newspaper. Each has set requirements for size, wording and position.
Can I trade under the new terms while I wait?
No. Until the variation is granted, you must trade in line with your existing licence. For a one off event, a temporary event notice may sometimes help, within the limits that apply. A solicitor can explain the options if you have a fixed date in mind.
Who is consulted on a minor variation?
The council decides which responsible authorities to consult, depending on what is being changed. Other people can also make representations during the notice period. The council considers what is said and decides whether the change could adversely affect the licensing objectives.
Do I need a new plan?
Only if the variation changes the layout or the areas used for licensable activities. If it does, the plan must meet the same requirements as for a new licence, including showing exits, fire safety equipment and the licensed area. Many operators use a specialist to redraw the plan.
What if the council does not decide a minor variation in time?
If the council does not make a decision within the time allowed, the application is treated as refused and the fee is not usually returned. In that situation the applicant can apply again, either as a minor variation or as a full variation. A solicitor can explain why the deadline was missed and what to do next.
Can I get later hours just for weekends or special days?
Yes, a variation can ask for different hours on different days, or for later hours on particular occasions such as bank holidays or New Year's Eve. These can be easier to justify than later hours every night. Temporary event notices are another way to cover occasional late nights, within the limits that apply.
What if the property already has a mortgage?
A charging order can still be obtained, but it ranks behind existing mortgages and charges. If the property is sold, those lenders are usually paid first and the charging order is paid from what is left. Your solicitor will look at the likely equity before recommending this method.
How long is a judgment valid for?
Judgments do not simply expire, but if more than six years have passed since the judgment, you need the court's permission to issue a writ or warrant of control. Other methods may also be affected by delay. It is generally best to enforce promptly after judgment.
Does interest continue after judgment?
In many cases, interest continues to run on a judgment debt until it is paid, though the rules depend on the court, the amount and the type of debt. Your solicitor will calculate any interest that can be added when enforcing.
How long does enforcement take?
It varies with the method. Enforcement agents can act within weeks of a warrant or writ being issued. Third party debt orders can freeze money quickly once granted. Charging orders can take longer and may leave you waiting for a sale. Your solicitor will give you an idea for your case.
What if the debtor asks to pay by instalments?
A debtor can ask the creditor, or sometimes the court, for time to pay. Accepting a sensible instalment plan can be the best outcome where the debtor cannot pay at once. Your solicitor can help agree terms and keep enforcement options open if payments stop.
Should I enforce quickly if the debtor is struggling?
Speed can matter where a debtor is in financial trouble, because enforcement that is not completed before an insolvency process starts may be affected. However, rushing into the wrong method wastes money. Your solicitor will balance the need to act quickly with choosing a method that is likely to work.
What happens once the judgment is paid?
Once the full amount, including any interest and recoverable costs, has been paid, enforcement stops. If the judgment was registered on the public register, the debtor can ask for it to be marked as satisfied. Your solicitor will confirm when the matter is closed and deal with any paperwork.
What happens to my service charge?
An informal extension does not usually change how the service charge works unless the new lease says so. However, some offers update the service charge clauses or add new charges. Your solicitor will compare the old and new wording so that you know whether anything you pay towards the building would change as a result of the deal.
Is the informal route cheaper than the statutory route?
Sometimes, but not always. The premium may be lower, but other terms, such as a continuing ground rent or a shorter extension, may make the overall deal worse value. Comparing the two needs a valuation and a careful look at the terms. Your solicitor and surveyor can help you weigh them up.
Is the informal route quicker?
It can be, because there are no statutory waiting periods. In practice it depends on how quickly the freeholder responds and how long it takes to agree terms. A freeholder that is slow to reply can make an informal deal take longer than a statutory one.
Can I still use the statutory route if talks fail?
Usually, yes, as long as you qualify. Negotiating informally does not normally stop you serving a statutory notice later. Your solicitor will watch for anything in the correspondence that might affect this.
Can I serve a statutory notice and still negotiate?
Yes. Many leaseholders serve a notice and then agree terms by negotiation within the statutory framework. This keeps the protections of the statutory process in place while leaving room for agreement. Your solicitor will explain the pros and cons.
How do the 2024 reforms affect informal offers?
The reforms change the statutory route, including the length of the new lease and how the premium is worked out, and they are being brought in over time. Because an informal offer is usually compared with the statutory alternative, the reforms can change how an offer looks. A solicitor will explain the position at the time you are deciding.
Can I pass an informal offer to a buyer?
Usually not in the same way as a statutory notice, which can be assigned to a buyer on a sale. An informal offer depends on the freeholder's agreement. If you are selling, your solicitor will explain which route is more practical given your timescales.
Does selling genuine second hand goods infringe?
Generally no. Once genuine goods have been put on the market in the UK or the European Economic Area by or with the consent of the owner, reselling them is usually allowed. There are exceptions, for example where the goods have been changed or the resale damages the reputation of the mark.
What should I do first if someone copies my brand?
Gather evidence, including dated screenshots and samples, and get advice before contacting the other side. The firm will check your rights and suggest the best first step. Acting quickly can matter, because long delay can sometimes weaken your position.
Should I contact the other business myself?
It is usually better to take advice first. A friendly message may work, but a poorly worded one can trigger an unjustified threats claim or reveal weaknesses in your position. The firm can help you decide the right tone and content.
Can I get an urgent injunction?
In clear and urgent cases, a court can grant an interim injunction to stop infringing use quickly, before a full trial. Courts expect the claimant to act promptly and may require an undertaking to pay damages if the injunction turns out to be wrong. The firm can tell you whether this is realistic.
What can I recover if I win?
Remedies can include an injunction, damages or the infringer's profits, delivery up or destruction of infringing goods, and a contribution to your legal costs. The amount depends on the evidence of loss or profit. The firm will give you a realistic view.
How long do I have to bring a claim?
Claims for damages are generally subject to a six year limitation period from the infringing acts. However, waiting a long time before acting can make it harder to get an injunction and can weaken your case in other ways. It is best not to delay.
What if the infringer is overseas?
If they sell into the UK, you may be able to act here, and platform takedowns work regardless of where the seller is. Enforcing a judgment abroad is harder. The firm can explain the practical options.
Can I pay in instalments?
Some firms allow fees to be paid in stages, for example part at the start and part when the application is submitted. Others ask for payment upfront. Government fees are usually paid when the application is made. Ask the firm what payment arrangements it offers before you commit.
Are cheaper advisers just as good?
Price is only one part of the picture. It is worth looking at what is included, who will handle your matter, how experienced they are and whether they are properly regulated. A clear written quote helps you compare firms fairly and avoid surprises later.
My visa ends soon. What should I do?
Leaflegal cannot advise on your situation, but we can introduce you to a regulated adviser quickly. Tell us the date your visa ends when you enquire. The sooner you speak to an adviser, the more time there is to understand your options and prepare.
I have received a letter from the Home Office.
Read it carefully and note any deadline, as some time limits are short. Contact a regulated adviser as soon as you can and have the letter ready to share. If you do not have an adviser yet, mention the deadline when you enquire so the firm knows it is urgent.
My application was refused. Can you help?
We can introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at the refusal. There may be short time limits for some kinds of challenge, so tell us the date on the decision when you enquire. Have the refusal letter and a copy of your application ready for the adviser.
How quickly will an adviser contact me?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day, often sooner. If you have an urgent date, such as a visa ending soon or a job starting, say so in your enquiry. Make sure you check the phone number and email you give us.
How do I check an adviser is regulated?
You can search the Solicitors Regulation Authority register for solicitors and the Immigration Advice Authority register for advisers. Both are free online. A genuine adviser will give you their registration details if you ask. Every firm we introduce is regulated. Advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority are registered at different levels, which show the kind of work they can do.
