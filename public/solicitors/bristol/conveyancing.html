Conveyancing explained

General information for people and businesses in Bristol. Pick a topic to read more.

Conveyancing is the legal work involved in moving the ownership of property, or a mortgage over property, from one person to another. In England and Wales it covers buying and selling homes, remortgaging, adding or removing an owner, and a range of related jobs such as dealing with a lease or a new build contract. The person doing the work is called a conveyancer, and they may be a solicitor or a licensed conveyancer.

For most people, conveyancing is something they only deal with a handful of times in their life. It usually sits alongside some of the biggest financial decisions they will ever make, so it helps to understand the basic shape of the work before it starts. The legal side is not just paperwork. It is about making sure you know what you are buying, that the person selling actually has the right to sell, and that your money and your lender's money are protected.

A good conveyancer acts as a guide as well as a lawyer. They explain what is happening at each stage, tell you what they need from you and when, and warn you early if something could cause a problem. They also deal with the people you do not see, such as the other side's solicitor, the mortgage lender, the local authority, HM Land Registry and HMRC or the Welsh Revenue Authority.

What conveyancing protects

  • Your legal title, so you own what you think you own
  • Your money, which passes through a regulated client account
  • Your lender's security, which most mortgage offers depend on
  • Your rights over things like access, drains and boundaries
  • Your position if something turns out to be different from what you were told

England and Wales

The law of conveyancing is largely the same across England and Wales, but there are differences. The biggest is property tax. In England, buyers may pay Stamp Duty Land Tax to HMRC. In Wales, the equivalent is Land Transaction Tax, collected by the Welsh Revenue Authority. Some renting and leasehold rules also differ. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own separate systems, and the firms we introduce handle property in England and Wales.

Who is involved

A typical sale or purchase involves more people than most clients expect. There is usually an estate agent, a conveyancer for the buyer and one for the seller, at least one mortgage lender and broker, a surveyor, and often other buyers and sellers further along the chain. Your conveyancer only acts for you and, where relevant, your lender. They cannot speak for the other side or make other people in the chain move faster, but they can chase, explain what is holding things up and keep your part of the transaction moving.

Conveyancing is a broad heading. Different jobs need different checks, and some firms specialise in particular kinds of work. These are the matters we introduce people for.

Buying a home

The buyer's conveyancer checks the title, orders searches, reviews the contract and the seller's answers, reports on anything unusual, and handles the money and registration. You can read more on our page about buying a home.

Selling a home

The seller's conveyancer prepares the contract pack, deals with the buyer's questions, pays off the mortgage and sends the seller the balance. Preparing early is the single biggest thing a seller can do to avoid delay. See our page on selling a home.

Buying and selling together

Most home movers have a sale and a purchase that need to happen on the same day. One firm can run both and keep them in step.

Remortgaging

Switching to a new lender needs the new lender's legal work done, the old mortgage paid off and the new one registered. Some lenders offer this free, while others leave you to instruct a firm.

Transfer of equity

Adding or removing someone from the ownership of a home without selling it. This often follows a marriage, a separation or a gift within a family, and it usually needs the lender's consent.

Other property matters

We also introduce first time buyers, landlords buying to let, people buying new build homes from developers and people buying leasehold flats. Each of these has its own extra checks, and it is worth telling us about them when you fill in the form so we can match you to a firm that does this work regularly.

Most people choose a conveyancer on price alone, or take whoever the estate agent suggests. Price matters, but it is not the only thing worth weighing up. Conveyancing is a service you will be in contact with for weeks or months, and the way a firm works can make a real difference to how stressful the process feels.

Solicitor or licensed conveyancer

Both can do residential conveyancing. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and licensed conveyancers by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Both must carry professional indemnity insurance and hold client money under strict rules. A solicitor's firm may also be able to help with related matters such as wills or separation, while a licensed conveyancer focuses on property.

Lender panels

If you are using a mortgage, the firm usually has to be on your lender's panel, which is the list of firms the lender trusts to act for it. Most established firms are on the panels of the major lenders, but it is worth checking, particularly with smaller or specialist lenders.

Things worth asking

  • Who will handle my file day to day, and how do I reach them?
  • How do you keep clients updated, and how often?
  • Is the quote fixed, and what would make it go up?
  • Do you do much work with this kind of property, such as leasehold or new build?
  • Are you on my lender's panel?
  • Do you belong to any quality schemes, such as the Law Society's Conveyancing Quality Scheme?

Online and local firms

Some firms work mainly online and by phone, with case tracking portals and set processes. Others are local high street practices that know the area and its usual issues. Neither is automatically better. What matters is that the firm is responsive, explains things clearly and has the experience your transaction needs.

How long conveyancing takes is one of the questions people ask most, and one of the hardest to answer. A simple remortgage may take a few weeks. A purchase in a long chain with a leasehold flat at one end can take several months. The legal work itself is only part of the timetable.

What usually affects the time

  • The length of the chain and how ready everyone in it is
  • How quickly the seller's paperwork is prepared
  • How long the local authority takes to return searches
  • Whether the mortgage offer is issued promptly
  • Leasehold management packs from landlords and managing agents
  • Problems found on the title, such as missing rights or unclear boundaries
  • How quickly each party answers questions and signs documents

The stages in brief

Most transactions follow a similar order. The firm is instructed and carries out identity and money checks. The title and contract are reviewed and searches are ordered. Enquiries are raised and answered. The buyer's mortgage offer arrives and the buyer is reported to. Contracts are signed and exchanged, and a completion date is fixed. Completion follows, and the change of ownership is registered.

Exchange and completion

In England and Wales, nobody is legally bound to buy or sell until contracts are exchanged. Until then either side can pull out. Once contracts are exchanged, both sides are committed and the completion date is fixed. The gap between exchange and completion is agreed between the parties and can range from the same day to several weeks.

After completion

Completion is not quite the end of the legal work. The buyer's conveyancer still has to submit the tax return and pay any tax due, then apply to HM Land Registry to register the new owner and any new mortgage. For leasehold property, notices are usually sent to the landlord or managing agent. The seller's conveyancer makes sure the old mortgage is formally removed from the register. Most of this happens in the background and does not need anything from you, but registration can take some time to show on the public register.

Your conveyancer can usually give a rough idea of timescales once they have seen the paperwork, but they cannot control everyone in the chain. Starting early and answering questions quickly are the two things most within your control.

Conveyancing costs come in three main parts. Understanding the difference makes it much easier to compare quotes.

Legal fee

This is what the firm charges for its own work. Many firms quote a fixed fee for straightforward residential work, often linked to the price of the property and whether it is freehold or leasehold. Some add separate charges for extra tasks, such as dealing with a gifted deposit, a Help to Buy loan, a new build or a lease.

Disbursements

These are payments the firm makes to other organisations on your behalf. Examples include search fees, HM Land Registry fees, bank transfer fees, identity check fees and, for leasehold property, fees charged by the landlord or managing agent. A clear quote lists disbursements separately.

Taxes

Buyers may need to pay Stamp Duty Land Tax in England or Land Transaction Tax in Wales. The amount depends on the price, the type of buyer and whether reliefs or higher rates apply. Rates and thresholds change, so check GOV.UK or the Welsh Revenue Authority, or ask the firm to confirm the figure for you. VAT is also charged on the legal fee and on some disbursements.

Comparing quotes

A low headline fee can rise quickly if extras are added later. When comparing quotes, look at the total including VAT and disbursements, check what is included, and ask what would happen if the transaction fell through. Some firms offer reduced or no fees if a sale or purchase does not go ahead, so it is worth asking.

It also helps to know when money is needed. Most firms ask for a payment on account at the start to cover searches and early disbursements. Buyers usually need their deposit ready before exchange and the rest of their funds, along with any tax, shortly before completion. Your firm will send a statement showing what is due and when, and it should never ask you to send money to a new bank account by email without confirming it by phone first.

A lot of conveyancing delay comes from waiting for information that could have been gathered at the start. A little preparation helps your firm move faster and reduces the chance of last minute surprises.

For everyone

  • Photo ID, such as a passport or driving licence
  • Proof of address, such as a recent bank statement or council tax bill
  • Details of where your money is coming from, with bank statements going back several months
  • Contact details for your estate agent and mortgage broker, if you have them

If you are selling

  • Your title deeds, or the name of your mortgage lender
  • Guarantees and warranties for work done to the property
  • Building regulation certificates and planning documents for any alterations
  • Certificates for things like replacement windows, boilers and electrical work
  • For leasehold property, your lease and your landlord or managing agent's details

If you are buying

  • Your mortgage in principle or offer
  • Evidence of your deposit, and gift letters if family are helping
  • Details of any scheme you are using, such as a Lifetime ISA or shared ownership
  • Your answers to any questions the firm sends, returned promptly

Source of funds checks

All regulated firms must check where the money in a property transaction comes from. This is a legal requirement under money laundering rules, not a sign that anyone suspects you. Expect to be asked for bank statements and an explanation of any large payments in or out. If a relative is gifting money, they will usually need to prove their identity and where the money came from too.

Keeping things moving

Once the transaction is under way, the most useful thing you can do is respond quickly. Read what your conveyancer sends, ask if anything is unclear and return signed documents promptly. Tell the firm straight away about any change in your plans, such as a new mortgage product, a change in the amount you are borrowing, someone else joining the purchase or a change in your move date. Small changes can affect paperwork that has already been prepared.

Having these ready when the firm first contacts you can save days or even weeks later in the process.

Which areas do you cover?
Conveyancing enquiries for properties in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems and are not covered at the moment.
How much does conveyancing cost?
It depends on the property, its value, whether it is leasehold and the firm. The firm will give you a written quote before you instruct them.
Do I need a licence to let the property?
Some properties do. Larger houses in multiple occupation always need a licence, and many councils run schemes that require licences for other rented homes in certain areas. Your conveyancer can ask the seller about licensing, and you can check the local council's website for current schemes.
What if the tenant is behind with the rent?
Rent arrears on a tenanted purchase are something to raise before exchange. The contract can say how arrears are dealt with, for example whether the seller keeps the right to recover them or the price is adjusted. Taking on a tenant who is already behind is a commercial risk you will want to weigh up. Your conveyancer will ask for a rent statement and explain what the contract says.
What about flats with cladding or fire safety problems?
Some blocks have fire safety defects that affect lending, insurance and service charges. The Building Safety Act 2022 limits what some leaseholders can be charged for certain repairs, but the protection depends partly on how many properties the owner holds, so landlords with several properties may be treated differently. Your conveyancer will ask the managing agent about the building and report on what they find, and your lender may have its own requirements.
Will I pay a higher rate of Stamp Duty?
Usually yes, if you already own a home or are buying through a company. Higher rates apply to most additional residential properties in England. The rates and the rules about when they apply change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask your conveyancer. In Wales, Land Transaction Tax has its own higher rates.
Can I reclaim the higher rate later?
In some cases, if you buy a new main home before selling your old one and then sell the old one within a set period, you may be able to claim back the extra tax. This does not usually apply to buy to let purchases where the new property will not be your home. Your conveyancer or a tax adviser can explain whether a refund could apply.
Why does the firm need so much detail about my funds?
Conveyancers must carry out anti money laundering checks on every purchase. Landlords often use money from several sources, such as savings, remortgages, sale proceeds or company funds. Each source needs to be explained and evidenced. Providing a clear trail early is the best way to avoid delays.
Who should I speak to about tax?
Your conveyancer will deal with the Stamp Duty Land Tax return for the purchase. Wider questions, such as income tax on rent, mortgage interest relief, capital gains tax and whether to use a company, are for an accountant or tax adviser. Many landlords speak to one before they make an offer.
Do I need insurance in place before completion?
If the property is freehold, you usually become responsible for insuring it from exchange of contracts, and your lender will expect cover to be in place. Landlords need a policy that covers letting, as ordinary home insurance often does not. For a leasehold flat, the building is usually insured by the freeholder, but you may still want cover for contents and liability. Your conveyancer will confirm what the lender requires.
Do I need a conveyancer who specialises in buy to let?
Any regulated conveyancer can handle a buy to let purchase, but one who deals with landlords regularly will know what to look for. Ask whether the firm acts for company buyers, buys with tenants in place and is on your lender's panel. Some buy to let lenders have smaller panels than residential lenders.
What should I do about snags after I move in?
Report them to the developer in writing as soon as you find them and keep a copy. Most developers have a customer care team and a period during which they put defects right. If problems are not dealt with, the warranty provider's dispute service or, for registered developers, the New Homes Ombudsman may be able to help.
Can I order upgrades or extras?
Many developers offer upgrades such as different flooring, kitchen finishes or appliances, sometimes as an incentive and sometimes at extra cost. Make sure every extra is confirmed in writing with the price and what is included. Tell your conveyancer, because extras may need to be disclosed to your lender and recorded in the contract. Paid extras can also be relevant to the tax calculation.
What is an estate management charge?
Many new estates have shared areas such as open spaces, private roads, play areas or drainage features that are not adopted by the council. A management company looks after them, and homeowners pay an annual charge. Your conveyancer will explain who runs the company, how the charge is worked out and what happens if it is not paid.
What does road adoption mean?
It means the council takes over responsibility for maintaining the estate roads. Until then, the roads are private and the developer or a management company maintains them. Developers usually sign an agreement with the council, often backed by a bond, to finish the roads to an adoptable standard. Your conveyancer will check what is in place.
Are new build houses ever leasehold?
Some new houses have been sold as leasehold in the past, which drew a lot of criticism. The law on new leasehold houses and on ground rent has been changing. Most new long residential leases granted since mid 2022 can only have a nominal ground rent. Your conveyancer will explain the tenure of the home you are buying and how the current rules apply.
How long does a building warranty last?
A typical warranty lasts ten years from completion. During the early years, often the first two, the developer is usually responsible for fixing defects. Later on, cover is usually limited to certain structural defects. Each warranty provider has its own terms, so ask your conveyancer for the policy details and read them.
Does the warranty cover everything?
No. Warranties usually exclude wear and tear, normal shrinkage cracks, damage caused by the owner and some fittings that have their own guarantees. Claims often have to be made within set time limits. The policy document explains what is covered. Your conveyancer checks the warranty is in place, but questions about a claim go to the provider.
Is buying a new build flat any different?
Yes. A new flat is leasehold, so your conveyancer will review the new lease as well as the developer's contract. They will look at the length of the lease, the service charge estimate, who manages the building, what the reserve fund will be and the rules for residents. Most new long leases now carry only a nominal ground rent. First year service charges are often estimates and can rise once the building is fully occupied.
Are legal fees higher for a new build?
Often slightly, because the work is done to a tight deadline and involves checks that do not arise on a resale, such as the estate plan, adoption agreements and warranty. Ask for a written quote that shows any new build supplement and lists disbursements separately.
Can the seller extend the lease before I buy?
Sometimes. The seller can start a statutory claim by serving notice and then assign the benefit of that notice to you on completion, so the extension carries on in your name. Alternatively the seller can finish the extension before the sale. Your conveyancer will explain how this would affect the timetable and costs.
Do I have to wait before extending the lease myself?
In the past, a new owner usually had to own the flat for two years before making a statutory claim. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 has changed this and other eligibility rules. Your conveyancer will explain the current position and whether it makes a difference to your plans.
Does a short lease mean I should not buy?
Not necessarily. Some buyers choose a flat with a shorter lease because the price reflects it, and then extend the lease. Others prefer to avoid the extra cost and uncertainty. It depends on your lender, your budget and your plans. Your conveyancer can explain the legal position, and a specialist surveyor can estimate what an extension might cost, but the decision is yours.
Why will my conveyancer ask about cladding and fire safety?
Since the Grenfell Tower fire, lenders and buyers want to know about fire safety in blocks of flats. Your conveyancer will ask the seller and managing agent about the external walls, any remediation works and any certificates. The answers can affect whether a lender will lend and whether costs could fall on leaseholders.
What protection does the Building Safety Act give leaseholders?
The Building Safety Act 2022 limits or removes what some leaseholders can be charged for fixing certain historical building safety defects. Whether a lease qualifies depends on things like the height of the building and how many properties the leaseholder owns. Your conveyancer will check what information is available and explain how the rules may apply.
What is a leaseholder deed of certificate?
It is a document the seller completes to show whether their lease qualifies for protection under the Building Safety Act. The freeholder may also provide a landlord's certificate. These help show who is responsible for paying for certain safety works. Your conveyancer will ask for them where they apply.
What is an EWS1 form?
It is a form used to record an assessment of the external walls of a residential building, created so lenders could value flats in buildings with possible cladding concerns. Not every building needs one, and lender requirements have changed over time. Your conveyancer and lender will tell you whether one is needed for the flat you are buying.
Can I still get a mortgage on a flat with building safety issues?
Sometimes. Lender attitudes vary and have changed over time. Some lend once remediation is planned or funded, while others want works finished first or particular certificates in place. Your mortgage broker can tell you which lenders may help, and your conveyancer will pass on whatever information the lender asks for. Building safety questions can add time to a purchase, so it helps to raise them early.
Why do leasehold purchases take longer?
There is more information to gather and more parties involved. Managing agents can be slow to supply the pack, the lease may need careful review and building safety questions can take time to answer. If the lease needs extending or correcting, that adds further time. Your conveyancer will keep you updated and tell you where any delay is coming from.
What is a completion statement?
It is a document from your conveyancer showing the money coming in, such as your mortgage and your own funds, and the money going out, such as the purchase price, fees, disbursements and tax. It tells you exactly how much you need to send before completion.
When is Stamp Duty or Land Transaction Tax paid?
Your conveyancer usually collects the tax from you before completion and submits the return and payment after completion, within a strict deadline. If the return is late, penalties and interest can apply. The amount depends on the price and your circumstances, and the rules change, so your firm will confirm the figure.
Why does my conveyancer need so much detail about my savings?
Regulated firms must check the source of funds for every purchase under money laundering rules. That usually means bank statements covering several months and an explanation of where larger sums came from, such as savings, a bonus, an inheritance or the sale of shares. It is a legal requirement and applies to every client, not just you.
What does exchange of contracts mean?
Exchange is the moment the contract becomes binding. Your conveyancer and the seller's conveyancer agree by phone that the signed contracts are exchanged, and your deposit is sent. From then on, if you do not complete, you could lose your deposit and be liable for the seller's losses.
Can I choose my completion date?
The completion date is agreed between buyer and seller before exchange, and in a chain everyone must agree the same date. You can suggest a date, but it needs to work for others too. Avoid booking removals until contracts have exchanged and the date is fixed.
What happens if completion is delayed?
If one side does not complete on the agreed date, the contract usually allows the other side to charge interest and, after a notice period, to cancel the contract and keep the deposit or claim losses. Delays are often short, but your conveyancer will explain what your contract says if it happens.
When do I get the keys?
You can usually collect the keys once the seller's conveyancer confirms they have received the money. The estate agent then releases the keys. In a chain, money often has to pass along several transactions, so the time you get the keys can vary on the day.
Can I visit the property between exchange and completion?
You have no automatic right to enter before completion, but sellers often agree to a visit to measure up or check something. Arrange it through the estate agent. Some buyers like to visit before completion to make sure fixtures and fittings agreed in the contract are still there.
Can I pull out after exchange?
Once contracts are exchanged, you are legally bound to complete. Withdrawing at that stage usually means losing your deposit and possibly paying the seller's extra losses, such as the cost of a delayed sale. That is why your conveyancer will check that your mortgage and funds are in place, and that you are ready, before exchanging.
Do I need to be available on completion day?
You do not usually need to be at your conveyancer's office, but you need to have moved out and left the keys with the estate agent or as agreed. Keep your phone to hand in case your conveyancer needs to contact you. You should also take meter readings before you leave.
Who sets the completion date?
The completion date is agreed between you and the buyer before exchange, usually through the conveyancers and the estate agent. In a chain, everyone has to agree the same date, so there is often some give and take. Once contracts have exchanged, the date is fixed in the contract and can only be changed if everyone agrees.
When will I receive the money from my sale?
On completion, your conveyancer receives the buyer's money, pays off your mortgage and any other charges, deducts their fees and any estate agent's fee you have authorised, and sends you the balance. This is usually sent the same day or the next working day, depending on timing and bank processes.
Who pays the estate agent?
You pay the estate agent under the agreement you signed with them. Many sellers ask their conveyancer to pay the agent from the sale money on completion, which is convenient. Your conveyancer will need the agent's invoice before completion to include it in the completion statement.
What happens to my buildings insurance?
Keep your buildings insurance in place until completion. Even though the buyer may arrange their own cover from exchange, the property is still yours until completion, and your lender will expect cover to continue until the mortgage is paid off. Cancel it only once completion has happened.
What happens if the buyer does not complete on time?
If the buyer fails to complete on the agreed date, the contract usually allows you to charge interest and, after serving a notice, to end the contract and keep the deposit. You may also be able to claim further losses. Your conveyancer will explain your position under the contract if it happens.
Will I owe tax when I sell?
Selling your only or main home is often free of Capital Gains Tax, but tax can apply to second homes, rented properties, homes with large grounds or homes used partly for business. There can be a short reporting deadline. Tax rules change, so speak to an accountant or check GOV.UK for your situation.
Why must I sign a transfer deed?
The transfer deed is the document that actually moves legal ownership from you to the buyer. Your conveyancer will send it for you to sign in front of an independent witness, or electronically if the firm uses that method. It is held until completion, then sent to the buyer's conveyancer so they can register the buyer as owner.
Can I sell a property I inherited?
Yes, but the personal representatives usually need a grant of probate or letters of administration before the sale can complete. Marketing and agreeing a sale can often start earlier. The executors sign the documents, and the buyer's conveyancer will check that they have authority to sell.
