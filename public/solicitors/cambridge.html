Choosing a solicitor in Cambridge

The right firm for you depends on the kind of matter you have, not just on being close by. A conveyancing firm on the high street in Cambridge may not handle a contested licence review, and a family law specialist will not usually chase a business debt. That is why we ask what your matter is about before we introduce anyone.

It is worth asking any firm how it charges before work starts. Many matters are handled for a fixed fee, while others are charged by the hour. A good firm will explain the likely total, what could make it change and what is included, and confirm it in writing.

You can check that a solicitor is regulated on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and that a licensed conveyancer is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. It is natural to want a solicitor who is close by. For some kinds of matter that is useful. For others, the location of the firm makes very little difference to the service you receive.

When local knowledge can help

Some matters involve a particular court, council or local authority. A premises licence is applied for to the local council where the premises is, and the council's licensing policy and the views of local police and residents can matter. A family dispute may be heard at the local family court. In these cases a firm that knows the local procedures and people can be an advantage, and attending a hearing is easier if the firm is not far away.

When location matters less

Conveyancing, lease extensions and business debt recovery are now mostly handled by phone, email, post and online portals. Property searches and Land Registry applications are done electronically, county court claims for money are often issued online, and documents can be signed and returned without visiting an office. Many people use a firm in a different town and never meet in person, and are perfectly happy with the service.

Meeting in person

If meeting face to face matters to you, say so when you make your enquiry. Some people find it easier to discuss personal matters, such as a separation, in person. Others are more comfortable with video calls. Many firms offer both, and you can ask what the firm usually does on the first call.

Getting the balance right

In the end, experience in your type of matter, clear communication and fair fees usually matter more than the distance to the office. A nearby firm that rarely handles your kind of matter may not be the best choice, while a firm a few towns away that does it every day may suit you well. The firm we introduce handles your type of work and covers your area.

Area by area

Conveyancing firms routinely act for buyers and sellers all over England and Wales. What matters more is that the firm is on your lender's panel and responds quickly. Local knowledge can help with things like unusual local searches or properties affected by mining or flood risk, but a good conveyancer will know how to deal with those wherever the property is.

Lease extension work is mostly paperwork and negotiation with the freeholder's solicitor, supported by a valuation from a surveyor. The surveyor usually needs to know the local property market, but the solicitor can be based anywhere. If the matter goes to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), hearings are often held remotely or at a regional venue.

Family solicitors often deal with the family court that covers the area where you or your children live. Many hearings now take place by video, but some are in person. Some people prefer a firm they can visit, especially during a difficult separation.

Licensing cases are the most local. Council licensing committees, local police and magistrates' courts all have their own ways of working. A firm that regularly appears before them can be a real help, and it may be able to attend a hearing at short notice.

Business debt recovery is usually handled through letters, negotiation and, if needed, the county court. Many money claims are issued and managed online, so the firm's location matters less than its experience with commercial debts.

Wales

England and Wales share one legal system, so firms in England can usually act for clients in Wales and the other way round. Some differences do apply in Wales, such as Land Transaction Tax instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax on property purchases, and some housing and planning rules. If you would like to deal with a firm in Welsh, mention it in the notes and the firm will tell you whether it can help.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, so we do not cover matters there for conveyancing, family law, licensing, lease extensions or debt recovery. Immigration introductions are available across the whole of the UK.

Check the register

Solicitors and law firms in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can search for them on the SRA register. Licensed conveyancers are regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers, which has its own register. Regulated immigration advisers can be checked with their regulator, such as the SRA or the Immigration Advice Authority.

Look for the regulatory information

Regulated law firms must show certain information on their websites, including who regulates them and their registration number. Many firms display the SRA's digital badge, which links to the firm's entry on the register. If a firm's website does not say who regulates it, ask.

Check the people, not just the firm

Ask who will handle your matter day to day. That might be a solicitor, a chartered legal executive, a licensed conveyancer or a paralegal working under supervision. All of these can be appropriate. It is reasonable to ask about their experience and who supervises them.

Read the paperwork

Before any work starts, the firm should send a client care letter and terms of business. These set out the fees, who will do the work, how complaints are handled and, if you were introduced by Leaflegal, the fee the firm pays for the introduction. Read them carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

Warning signs

Be cautious if anyone offering legal help will not say who regulates them, asks you to pay large sums before explaining their fees in writing, promises a particular result or pressures you to decide on the spot. Be especially careful with emails asking you to send money for a property purchase to new bank details. Always check bank details by phone using a number you already know before sending money to a firm.

Does a local firm cost more or less

Legal fees vary between firms, and location is only one factor. Firms in some large cities may have higher overheads, but that does not always mean higher prices for a particular piece of work. Fixed fee work, such as many house purchases, can be priced similarly across the country. The best way to understand the cost is to ask the firm for a written estimate or quote.

Travel and attendance

Where a solicitor needs to attend a court or hearing, the cost of travel and waiting time may be included in a fixed fee or charged separately. A local firm may charge less for travel to a local court. Ask how attendance is charged so you can compare like for like.

Disbursements

On top of the firm's own fees there may be disbursements, such as court fees, search fees, Land Registry fees, licensing application fees set by the council, or a barrister's fee. Official fees change from time to time, and GOV.UK lists many of them. The firm will tell you which apply.

Comparing quotes

If you speak to more than one firm, check that each quote covers the same work and includes the same items. A lower headline fee may leave out things that another firm includes. Ask each firm to confirm what is and is not included.

Why is 80 years important?
Under the current valuation rules, extending a lease with fewer than 80 years left usually costs more, because marriage value is added to the premium. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 provides for changes to this, but not all of the Act is in force yet. Your conveyancer will explain what applies when you buy.
How does a statutory lease extension work?
Under the long standing rules, a qualifying flat owner can serve a formal notice on the freeholder asking for a new lease. The extension usually adds 90 years to the remaining term and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn, in return for a premium. A surveyor values the premium and solicitors deal with the paperwork. The 2024 Act is set to change some of these rules.
Can the seller extend the lease before I buy?
Sometimes. The seller can start a statutory claim by serving notice and then assign the benefit of that notice to you on completion, so the extension carries on in your name. Alternatively the seller can finish the extension before the sale. Your conveyancer will explain how this would affect the timetable and costs.
Do I have to wait before extending the lease myself?
In the past, a new owner usually had to own the flat for two years before making a statutory claim. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 has changed this and other eligibility rules. Your conveyancer will explain the current position and whether it makes a difference to your plans.
Does a short lease mean I should not buy?
Not necessarily. Some buyers choose a flat with a shorter lease because the price reflects it, and then extend the lease. Others prefer to avoid the extra cost and uncertainty. It depends on your lender, your budget and your plans. Your conveyancer can explain the legal position, and a specialist surveyor can estimate what an extension might cost, but the decision is yours.
Why will my conveyancer ask about cladding and fire safety?
Since the Grenfell Tower fire, lenders and buyers want to know about fire safety in blocks of flats. Your conveyancer will ask the seller and managing agent about the external walls, any remediation works and any certificates. The answers can affect whether a lender will lend and whether costs could fall on leaseholders.
What protection does the Building Safety Act give leaseholders?
The Building Safety Act 2022 limits or removes what some leaseholders can be charged for fixing certain historical building safety defects. Whether a lease qualifies depends on things like the height of the building and how many properties the leaseholder owns. Your conveyancer will check what information is available and explain how the rules may apply.
What if my ex will not give full disclosure?
The court can order disclosure, and can draw conclusions about someone's finances if they fail to provide it. Costs orders can also be made against people who do not cooperate. Your solicitor can explain the options for obtaining the information. In serious cases, a settlement reached without full disclosure can later be set aside. Keep a note of anything you think is missing and share it with your solicitor.
Can I look at my partner's private documents?
Be careful. Taking or copying documents that belong to your partner, or accessing their emails or accounts without permission, can be unlawful and can harm your case. Tell your solicitor what you know or suspect, and they will explain the proper ways to obtain information. Documents you already have lawful access to, such as joint statements, are different.
What is a pension report?
In some cases a specialist actuary is asked to prepare a report comparing the value of pensions and suggesting how they might be shared fairly. These reports are often useful where pensions are large or of different types. Your solicitor will explain whether one is likely to be needed. The cost is often shared between both people.
Do I have to disclose money I have received since we separated?
Yes. Disclosure covers your current finances, not just those at the date of separation. New income, an inheritance or a change of job all need to be disclosed, and the duty continues until the case ends. Your solicitor will explain how assets acquired after separation are usually treated. Failing to disclose can be costly later.
Can I stay in the family home?
It depends on the assets available, both people's housing needs, and the needs of any children. Options include one person buying the other out, or staying in the home for a period before it is sold. A solicitor can explain what might be realistic in your circumstances. Mortgage capacity and the cost of rehousing are often key.
Are pensions included?
Yes. Pensions are often one of the most valuable assets, and can be shared, offset against other assets or attached. Pensions built up before the marriage may be treated differently from those built up during it. Your solicitor will explain the options. Pensions are sometimes overlooked because their value is not obvious, so make sure you have up to date valuations before any agreement is reached.
Is an inheritance protected?
Not always. Inherited money and assets may be treated differently from assets built up together, but they can still be shared if they are needed to meet either person's needs or have been mixed with family money. How an inheritance is treated depends on the facts of each case. Expected future inheritances are usually treated differently.
What happens at a licensing hearing?
A sub-committee of councillors, or sometimes an officer, considers the case. A licensing officer usually presents a report, and you or your representative can respond, give evidence and answer questions. The committee decides and must give reasons. Hearings are less formal than court but are taken seriously.
Can I bring someone to the hearing?
Yes. You can usually be represented by a solicitor or bring a friend, family member or interpreter, depending on the council's procedure. Character witnesses can sometimes give evidence or provide written references. Check the council's hearing procedure, which is often sent with the notice of hearing.
How do I appeal a revocation?
Most driver licence decisions can be appealed to the magistrates' court within 21 days of being notified. The court hears the case afresh and can consider new evidence. Some decisions can be appealed further to the Crown Court. The time limit is strict, so speak to a solicitor as soon as you receive the decision.
Can I keep working while I appeal?
If the council's decision was not given immediate effect, you can usually keep working until the appeal period ends or the appeal is decided. If it was given immediate effect on public safety grounds, you cannot work during the appeal. A solicitor can explain your position and whether the appeal can be heard quickly.
What are the risks of appealing?
If the appeal fails, the court can order you to pay the council's legal costs, which can be significant. The court also gives weight to the council's decision and policy. A solicitor will look at the strength of your case and the likely costs before you decide whether to appeal.
Can I apply to a different council instead?
You can apply elsewhere, but you must usually declare previous refusals and revocations, and councils share this information through a national register and safeguarding duties. Failing to disclose them can lead to refusal or revocation. A solicitor can explain how a previous decision may be viewed.
What does a private hire operator licence involve?
An operator licence allows a person or business to accept and arrange private hire bookings. The operator must be fit and proper, keep records of bookings, drivers and vehicles, and use only drivers and vehicles licensed by the same council. Conditions often cover insurance, premises, complaints handling and safeguarding.
How long is a statutory demand valid for?
There is no simple expiry date, but a demand that is relied on long after it was served may be looked at critically, especially if circumstances have changed. If you intend to petition, it is usually sensible to do so within a reasonable time after the 21 days end. Your solicitor will advise on timing.
What if the company goes into administration?
If a company enters administration or another insolvency process, there are often restrictions on creditors taking action, including presenting a winding up petition. You would usually need to deal with the insolvency practitioner instead. Your solicitor will explain how this affects your debt.
Will I get paid first if I petition?
Not necessarily. In a liquidation, unsecured creditors usually share what is left after costs and certain preferred debts, regardless of who brought the petition. The petitioning creditor can usually recover its petition costs from the company's assets. The real leverage often comes before the hearing.
What if the debtor pays part of the debt?
A part payment reduces the debt but may not deal with the demand. If the balance owed by a company is still more than £750, a petition may still be possible. If it falls below the threshold, it may not. Your solicitor will look at what has been paid and explain the options for the remaining balance.
What if the debtor disputes the debt?
If the dispute is genuine and substantial, a statutory demand is usually not appropriate. A company can ask the court to stop a petition, and the creditor may be ordered to pay its costs. An individual can apply to set the demand aside. A court claim is usually the better way to resolve a real dispute.
Can a company set aside a statutory demand?
Not in the way an individual can. Instead, a company that disputes the debt can apply to the court for an order preventing the creditor from presenting a winding up petition. If the court finds a substantial dispute, the creditor is likely to face a costs order.
How does a sole trader challenge a demand?
An individual can apply to the court to set aside the demand, usually within 18 days of service. Grounds include a genuine dispute, a counterclaim or set off that equals or exceeds the debt, or the creditor holding enough security. The time for compliance is paused while the application is decided.
Should I extend my lease before selling?
That depends on how long is left, your timescale and the market. Some sellers extend first, some start the statutory process and pass the notice to the buyer, and some sell without extending. A solicitor can explain the pros and cons of each for your flat, and the decision is yours.
Can I pass a lease extension to my buyer?
Under the statutory route, once a notice has been served, its benefit can usually be assigned to the buyer at the same time as the sale completes. The buyer then takes over the claim. This needs to be done in the right order and form, so both solicitors will coordinate it.
My lease has a doubling ground rent. Will that affect my sale?
It can. Some lenders and buyers are cautious about ground rents that double at fixed intervals. Some freeholders have agreed to vary these terms. Your solicitor can explain whether a variation might be needed and how it could be arranged alongside the sale.
I made alterations without the freeholder's consent. What happens?
Tell your solicitor early. Depending on the lease and the work done, it may be possible to get retrospective consent from the freeholder, or indemnity insurance may be offered. Building regulations approval may also be needed. Leaving it until the buyer's solicitor asks can cause delays.
What if my flat has a share of the freehold?
The buyer will usually take over your share or membership of the freehold company. The buyer's solicitor will ask for company documents, such as share certificates, accounts and details of the directors. Having these ready can speed up the sale.
Does the length of my lease affect who can buy?
Yes. Buyers who need a mortgage are limited by their lender's minimum lease requirements, which vary between lenders. Cash buyers are not limited in the same way, but they usually take the lease length into account in their offer.
My lease has a restriction on selling. What does that mean?
Some leases require the freeholder's consent before the flat is sold, or require the buyer to sign a deed of covenant or join a management company. These are usually routine steps with a fee rather than real obstacles. Your solicitor will check the lease and tell the buyer's solicitor what will be needed.
Can I rely on a search done by my designer or branding agency?
Agencies sometimes run basic checks, which can be helpful. They are not usually a legal clearance search with an opinion from a regulated professional. If you are investing heavily, it is worth asking a trademark firm to review the position.
What if I am already trading and the search finds a problem?
You still have options. The firm may look at who used their name first, whether the earlier mark is used, whether the businesses really overlap and whether an agreement is possible. A search now is better than a letter from the other side later.
Should I keep a copy of the search report?
Yes. Keep it with your brand records. It shows that you took care before choosing the name, which can help if a dispute arises, and it is useful background if you later expand or sell the business.
Can the same firm handle my application and any dispute?
Usually yes. Many firms handle searching, filing and oppositions. If a dispute needs to go to court, a solicitor can act, and attorney firms often work with solicitors for that stage.

Yes, most trademark firms can arrange searches abroad, often through agents in other countries. Tell the firm where you plan to trade.
What should I bring to the first consultation?
In general, it helps to have your passport, details of your current visa, any Home Office letters and a rough timeline of your time in the UK. A note of trips abroad and any worries you have is also useful. The adviser will tell you if they need anything else.
I cannot find some old documents. Is that a problem?
Many people have gaps in their paperwork, especially from years ago. Advisers are used to this and can explain what other records might help. Sometimes old records can be requested from employers, banks or government departments. Mention any missing documents at the start so there is time to deal with them.
Should I tell the adviser about something I am worried about?
Advisers can only work with what they know. What you tell a regulated adviser is confidential, and they deal with difficult histories regularly. Raising a concern early gives them the chance to explain how it might be relevant and avoids surprises later.
How early should I start?
There is no fixed answer, and it depends on your situation. Many people find it helps to make contact well before their current permission ends, so there is time to gather documents and ask questions. If your visa is due to end soon, say so when you enquire so the firm knows it is urgent.
Can I prepare my own application and ask an adviser to check it?
Some advisers offer a checking service for applications people have prepared themselves, usually for a lower fee than full representation. Others prefer to handle the whole matter. Ask the adviser what they offer and what a check would cover.
Do I need to keep copies of everything?
It is sensible to keep copies of every document you send and every letter you receive. Your adviser will also keep a file. Having your own copies can help in future, for example if you later apply for citizenship or need to prove your history.
Do I need to have everything organised before I call?
No. Many people make contact with documents in a box or spread across emails. The adviser can help you work out what is needed and how to organise it. Having your passport and current visa details to hand for the first call is usually enough to get started.
