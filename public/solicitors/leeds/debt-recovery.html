Debt recovery explained

General information for people and businesses in Leeds. Pick a topic to read more.

Debt recovery is the work of getting money back from someone who owes it to you and has not paid. On Leaflegal, debt recovery means one thing only. It is about a business that is owed money by another business, in England and Wales. That might be a limited company, a limited liability partnership, a partnership or a sole trader who bought goods or services from you in the course of their own business.

We do not introduce anyone collecting debts from consumers. If the person who owes you money bought something for their own personal use, such as a householder paying for a new kitchen, that is consumer debt and it is outside what we do. Consumer debt collection has its own rules and regulators, and it is not part of this service.

Late payment is one of the most common problems a business faces. A single unpaid invoice can tie up cash that is needed for wages, suppliers and tax. A few unpaid invoices at once can put a healthy business under real strain. Most business owners would rather keep a good relationship with a customer, but there comes a point where a firmer approach is needed.

A debt recovery solicitor helps a business move from polite reminders to formal steps. Those steps usually build on each other, from a formal letter, to a court claim, to enforcing a judgment, or in some cases to insolvency routes such as a statutory demand or a winding up petition. Each step has its own rules, costs and risks.

Debts between businesses tend to fall into a few familiar patterns. Some customers are simply slow and pay eventually once pressed. Some are short of cash and want time. Some raise a complaint about the goods or work, sometimes genuine and sometimes as a reason to delay. A smaller number have no intention of paying at all. The right approach depends a great deal on which of these you are dealing with, and an experienced solicitor will usually spot the pattern quickly.

What a solicitor usually helps with

  • Reviewing contracts, terms and conditions and invoices to confirm what is owed
  • Sending a formal letter before action
  • Adding interest and compensation for late payment where it applies
  • Issuing a claim in the County Court or High Court
  • Handling defended claims and settlement talks
  • Enforcing a judgment that has not been paid
  • Serving statutory demands on companies and sole traders
  • Presenting winding up petitions against companies
  • Advising on payment plans and settlement agreements

Debt recovery covers several related matters. Some businesses need help with only one of them. Others move through several in turn as a debt progresses. These are the matters we introduce for.

Unpaid invoices

This is where most debts start. A customer has not paid an invoice, or has paid part of it, and reminders have not worked. A solicitor will check what is owed under the contract and suggest the most sensible route to payment.

Letters before action

A formal letter from a solicitor sets out what is owed and warns that court proceedings will follow if the debt is not paid. It is often the first formal step, and courts expect to see that the debtor has been given a fair chance to pay before a claim is issued.

Court claims

If the letter does not produce payment, a solicitor can issue a claim. Many debt claims are not defended, and the creditor can ask for judgment without a hearing. Defended claims go through the full court process.

Enforcement

A judgment is an order to pay, but it does not guarantee that the money arrives. Enforcement covers the tools available to turn a judgment into cash, such as enforcement agents, charging orders over property and orders that freeze bank accounts.

Statutory demands and winding up petitions

These are insolvency tools. A statutory demand is a formal demand for an undisputed debt. If a company does not pay, the creditor may be able to petition the court to wind it up. These are powerful steps that should only be used in the right cases.

Late payment interest and compensation

Businesses owed money by other businesses can often add interest and a fixed sum of compensation to late invoices, either under their contract or under late payment law. A solicitor will work out what can be claimed and add it to the demand.

How the matters fit together

Not every debt goes through every stage. Many are settled after a letter. Some go straight from a letter to a claim and are paid once judgment is entered. Others need enforcement, and a few are better suited to an insolvency route from the outset. A solicitor will explain which path makes most sense for the amount owed, the strength of your evidence and what is known about the debtor's finances.

If you are not sure which of these matters fits your situation, that is fine. Choose the one that seems closest when you fill in the form, and the firm will help you work out the right starting point.

Debt recovery is a practical area of law. What most businesses want is a firm that acts quickly, explains costs clearly and keeps them updated without fuss. Here are some things to think about when you speak to a firm.

Commercial experience

Business debts are different from personal debts. A firm that regularly acts for businesses will understand contract terms, retention of title clauses, personal guarantees and the insolvency tools that only apply to companies. Every firm we introduce handles commercial debt recovery.

Clear pricing

Many firms offer fixed fees for early steps such as a letter before action or an undefended claim. Ask what is included, what would cost more, and whether court fees are included. Ask how the firm charges if the debt is disputed, because defended work usually costs more.

Speed and communication

Time matters in debt recovery. The longer a debt sits, the harder it can be to recover, especially if the debtor is in financial difficulty. Ask how quickly the firm can send a letter, and how often it will update you.

Volume and repeat work

If your business often has slow payers, ask whether the firm offers a service for ongoing debts. Some firms have online portals for sending new debts and tracking progress. Others offer credit control support or will review your terms and conditions.

Honest advice about value

A good solicitor will tell you when a debt is not worth chasing, for example because the debtor has no assets or is already insolvent. That advice can save you money. Be wary of anyone who promises recovery, because no firm can guarantee that a debtor will pay.

Checking the debtor first

Ask whether the firm can carry out basic checks on the debtor before you spend money on a claim. Public records at Companies House, the Register of Judgments and the Gazette can show whether a company is already in trouble, has other unpaid judgments against it or has been struck off. A few minutes of checking can change the whole plan.

Remember that you are choosing someone to act for your business, possibly against a customer you have dealt with for years. It helps to feel comfortable with how the firm will speak to that customer on your behalf, and to agree at the start how firm or how conciliatory you want the tone to be.

The cost of recovering a business debt depends on how far the matter goes. A debt paid after a solicitor's letter costs far less than one that goes to a defended trial. There are usually three kinds of cost to think about.

It is sensible to agree a budget with the firm at the outset and to review it at each stage. Before moving from a letter to a claim, or from a claim to enforcement, a good solicitor will explain the likely extra cost against the realistic chance of getting paid, so that you can make an informed decision about whether to carry on.

The firm's fees

Many firms charge fixed fees for straightforward steps such as a letter before action, issuing a claim and requesting judgment. Defended claims, insolvency work and complex enforcement are more often charged by the hour or in fixed stages. Some firms offer other arrangements for certain debts. Ask the firm to explain its pricing in writing before any work starts.

Court and other fees

Issuing a claim, applying for judgment, enforcing a judgment and presenting a winding up petition all carry court fees. Winding up petitions also need a deposit for the Official Receiver. These fees are set by the government and change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask the firm for the current figures.

What you might get back

In many debt claims, the winning side can recover some of its costs from the other side. For lower value claims, the costs that can be recovered are usually limited to fixed amounts, so you may not get all of your legal spend back. Between businesses, late payment law often allows a creditor to add interest, a fixed sum of compensation and in some cases reasonable recovery costs. A solicitor will explain what may be recoverable in your case.

How long it takes to recover a business debt varies a great deal. Some debts are paid within days of a solicitor's letter. Others take many months, especially if the debtor defends the claim or has no obvious assets. These are typical stages, but your solicitor will explain what to expect in your case.

Letter before action

A letter usually gives the debtor a set period to pay or respond. For a company or other business debtor this is often around 14 days. Where the debtor is an individual, including a sole trader, a specific pre-action protocol applies and usually gives them 30 days.

Court claim

Once a claim is served, the debtor has a short period to respond. If they do nothing, the creditor can usually ask for judgment in default without a hearing. If they defend, the case is allocated to a track and timetabled, and a defended claim can take many months to reach a final hearing.

Enforcement

After judgment, the debtor is usually given time to pay. If they do not, enforcement can begin. Some methods work quickly, while others, such as charging orders, can take longer but give security over property.

Limitation

In England and Wales, a claim for an unpaid contract debt must usually be started within six years of the date the money became due. The time can restart if the debtor acknowledges the debt in writing or makes a payment. Do not leave a debt too long, as evidence and the debtor's assets can disappear well before the deadline.

Insolvency routes

A statutory demand gives a company 21 days to pay or secure the debt before a winding up petition can usually be presented. The petition is then served, advertised and heard by the court. The whole process takes several weeks at the least, though many debtors pay well before the hearing date.

Having the right papers to hand makes the first conversation with a solicitor much quicker and helps them give you a clear view. You do not need everything, but the more you have, the better.

  • The full legal name and registered number of the debtor, if it is a company
  • The debtor's trading address and registered office
  • Copies of the unpaid invoices
  • Your contract, order confirmation or signed terms and conditions
  • Evidence that the goods were delivered or the work was done
  • Emails, letters and notes of calls about the debt
  • Any complaints the debtor has raised about the goods or work
  • A statement of account showing what has been paid and what is outstanding
  • Any personal guarantee signed by a director
  • Details of any assets you know the debtor owns

Check who your customer really is

A common problem is invoicing the wrong entity. A business might trade under one name but contract through a company with another name. Checking the details on Companies House early helps make sure any claim is brought against the right party.

Be ready to talk about disputes

If the debtor has complained about the quality of the goods or work, tell the solicitor straight away. A genuine dispute changes which routes are open and how much a recovery might cost. It is far better for the solicitor to know at the start.

Keep a simple timeline

A short note of key dates is very useful. Include when the order was placed, when the goods were delivered or the work finished, when each invoice was sent and when it fell due, and when you chased. This helps the solicitor work out interest, check the limitation period and draft the letter accurately.

Debt recovery in Leeds, questions and answers

Do you only introduce firms based in Leeds?
We introduce you to a firm that covers Leeds. Some have offices nearby, and others work remotely across England and Wales. The firm will tell you how they work when they contact you.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal in Leeds?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm explains its own fees before you agree to anything.
Do you cover Bradford and Wakefield?
Yes. We cover Leeds and the surrounding area, including Bradford, Wakefield, Harrogate, Huddersfield and Halifax.
Can you help with personal debts?
No. We only introduce businesses recovering debts owed by other businesses.
How much does debt recovery cost?
Many firms charge a fixed fee for a letter before action and agreed fees for court stages. They will explain their charges before you instruct them.
Do I need to be a limited company?
No. Sole traders, partnerships and companies can all recover business debts.
Can I email a statutory demand?
Email alone is not usually enough. The rules on service are strict, and a petition based on a demand that was not properly served can fail. Your solicitor will use a method of service that the court accepts and will keep a clear record of it.
What must the demand say?
It must follow a prescribed form and include details of the creditor and debtor, the amount owed and how it arose, any interest or charges and how they were calculated, and information about how the debtor can respond. Your solicitor will prepare the demand so that it meets these requirements.
Can I include interest in the demand?
Yes, if the interest is properly due, for example under your contract or under late payment law for debts between businesses. It must be shown separately with an explanation of how it was calculated. Including sums that are not due can give the debtor grounds to challenge the demand.
What if the registered office is out of date?
A company is responsible for keeping its registered office up to date, and service there is usually valid even if the company no longer uses that address. However, a creditor should not ignore information it knows about. Your solicitor may also send a copy to the trading address to make sure the demand comes to the directors' attention.
What happens if the company does not pay?
If a company owing more than £750 does not pay or deal with the demand within 21 days, it can be treated as unable to pay its debts. The creditor may then be able to present a winding up petition. Many creditors use this point to negotiate before deciding whether to petition.
Do I have to petition after 21 days?
No. The demand gives you the option, not an obligation. Some creditors use the demand to prompt payment or a payment plan and never petition. Others decide that a petition is the right step. Your solicitor will discuss the costs and likely outcome of each option with you.
Can the debtor pay in instalments instead?
The demand can be dealt with by securing or compounding the debt to the creditor's reasonable satisfaction, which can include an agreed payment plan. Whether to accept a plan is the creditor's decision. Your solicitor can help set out the terms in writing.
How long is a statutory demand valid for?
There is no simple expiry date, but a demand that is relied on long after it was served may be looked at critically, especially if circumstances have changed. If you intend to petition, it is usually sensible to do so within a reasonable time after the 21 days end. Your solicitor will advise on timing.
What if the company goes into administration?
If a company enters administration or another insolvency process, there are often restrictions on creditors taking action, including presenting a winding up petition. You would usually need to deal with the insolvency practitioner instead. Your solicitor will explain how this affects your debt.
Can a petition be used just to get paid?
Many creditors find that a petition prompts payment, and that is a legitimate result where the company genuinely owes the money. What the court will not allow is using a petition to pressure a company over a debt that is genuinely disputed. In that situation, the creditor risks paying the company's costs.
How long does it take to get to a hearing?
It depends on the court's list and the time needed for service and advertisement. Generally, a petition takes at least several weeks from presentation to hearing, and sometimes longer. Your solicitor will give you an idea of current timescales in the relevant court.
What information will the solicitor need?
Expect to provide the company's details, your invoices and contract, the statutory demand or judgment if there is one, evidence of service of the demand and a clear account of any payments or complaints. The solicitor will also need to know whether you have heard of other creditors or any insolvency process.
Is a petition better than a court claim?
They do different jobs. A court claim leads to a judgment and then enforcement against the company's assets. A petition asks the court to end the company. A petition can produce quick payment from a company that is solvent but unwilling, while a claim is usually better where there is any dispute. Your solicitor will explain which suits your debt.
What happens to the company's bank account?
Once a petition is advertised, banks often freeze or restrict the company's accounts. This is because payments made after the petition is presented can be void if the company is wound up. The company may apply to the court for a validation order allowing certain payments to continue.
What does advertising the petition mean?
The petition is published in the Gazette, the official public record, within a set window before the hearing. This alerts other creditors and lenders. Advertising is a required step in most cases, and the timing rules must be followed carefully.
Can the company stop the petition?
The company can pay the debt and costs, reach a settlement with the creditor or apply to the court to restrain the petition on the basis that the debt is genuinely disputed. If the dispute is substantial, the court is likely to stop the petition and order the creditor to pay costs.
What if the company pays after the petition is presented?
The creditor can usually ask the court to dismiss the petition or allow withdrawal. However, payments made after presentation can be challenged if the company is later wound up on another petition. Your solicitor will explain how to handle any payment and reduce the risk of having to repay it.
Can other creditors join in?
Yes. Other creditors can give notice that they intend to appear at the hearing to support or oppose the petition. If the petitioner is paid and withdraws, a supporting creditor may be substituted to continue the case.
What is an order to obtain information?
It is a court order requiring the debtor, or for a company one of its officers, to attend court and answer questions on oath about its finances, and to bring documents. The answers can show which assets are available and which enforcement method is most likely to work.
Can enforcement agents take anything they find?
No. They can only take goods that belong to the debtor, and some items are protected. Goods that are leased, on finance or owned by others cannot usually be taken. Agents must follow detailed rules on how and when they take control of goods.
Can I use more than one method?
Yes. Methods can often be used together or one after another. For example, information gained through an order to obtain information might lead to a third party debt order or a charging order. Your solicitor will plan the sequence that makes the most sense.
Can enforcement agents enter business premises?
Enforcement agents have powers to enter premises in certain circumstances, but those powers are limited by detailed rules. They usually visit during set hours and must follow the procedure laid down for taking control of goods. Your solicitor can explain how this works in practice.
Can the debtor stop enforcement agents?
A debtor can pay, agree a payment arrangement with the agents or apply to the court to suspend the warrant or writ, often by offering instalments. The court will look at the debtor's finances and decide what is fair. Your solicitor can respond to any application on your behalf.
Is an order to obtain information worth the cost?
It can be, especially where little is known about the debtor. The answers given on oath, and the documents produced, often show which assets exist and where. That helps avoid wasting money on methods that will not work. For a very small debt, though, the cost may outweigh the benefit. Your solicitor will give you a view.
What is a charging order?
A charging order secures the judgment debt against land or property owned by the debtor. It is made in two stages, interim and final, and is registered against the property. The debt is usually paid when the property is sold or refinanced. It can give long term security rather than immediate payment.
Can I force the sale of the property?
Once a final charging order is in place, a creditor can apply for an order for sale. Courts look carefully at these applications, especially where the property is a home. For business property owned by a company, the position may be different. Your solicitor will explain whether this is realistic.
What is a third party debt order?
It is an order directed at someone who owes money to the debtor, most often its bank. An interim order freezes funds in the account up to the judgment amount. If the order is made final, the money is paid to the creditor. It only catches money that is in the account at the time the interim order is served.
When does interest start to run?
If your contract sets a payment date, interest usually starts the day after that date. If no date was agreed, the law generally allows 30 days after the later of delivery and the customer receiving the invoice. Your solicitor will check the start date for each invoice.
Is it simple or compound interest?
Statutory interest is simple interest. It is calculated on the original unpaid amount and is not added to the debt and then charged interest itself. A contract can provide for a different arrangement, which your solicitor can explain.
Can I claim interest after the invoice has been paid?
Usually, yes. The right to statutory interest and compensation arises when the payment becomes late, so it can often be claimed even after the invoice itself has been paid late. Whether it is worth pursuing is a commercial decision for your business.
Can I claim both contractual and statutory interest?
No. If your contract provides a substantial remedy for late payment, that remedy usually applies instead of statutory interest. If your contractual term is not a substantial remedy, it may be void and statutory interest may apply instead. Your solicitor will check your terms.
What if the customer paid part of the invoice?
Interest is usually calculated on whatever remains unpaid from time to time. Interest on the part paid stops on the date it was paid, while interest on the balance continues. A clear account history showing each payment makes the calculation straightforward. Your solicitor can prepare it for you.
What fixed compensation can I claim?
Late payment law sets a fixed sum for each late debt, depending on its size. The bands have been £40 for debts under £1,000, £70 for debts of £1,000 up to £10,000 and £100 for debts of £10,000 or more. Check GOV.UK or with your solicitor in case they change.
Is compensation per invoice or per customer?
It is usually per debt, which normally means each separate invoice. If a customer has paid several invoices late, a fixed sum can often be claimed for each one. Your solicitor will look at how the invoices were raised and what the contract says.
What are reasonable recovery costs?
If the actual cost of recovering a debt is more than the fixed sum, the creditor may be able to claim reasonable costs of recovery as well, less the fixed sum already claimed. This can include some external costs of chasing. Whether costs are reasonable depends on the circumstances.
Do I need to prove what chasing cost me?
Not for the fixed sum. It is set by law and does not depend on the actual cost of chasing. If you want to claim reasonable recovery costs above the fixed sum, you will need to show what those costs were and why they were reasonable.
Can I still recover the part that is not disputed?
Often, yes. If the customer only disputes part of an invoice, the undisputed amount may be recoverable separately and more quickly. A solicitor can help identify what is genuinely in dispute and pursue the rest.
The customer says it is owed money by us. Can it deduct that?
This is called set off. Whether the customer can deduct money depends on your contract and the nature of its own claim. Some business terms exclude set off. A solicitor will look at your terms and the customer's claim and explain the position.
The customer raised a complaint only after I chased. Does that matter?
It can be relevant. A complaint that appears for the first time months after delivery, and only when payment is requested, may carry less weight than one raised promptly. However, it still needs to be looked at properly. A solicitor will consider the timing along with the other evidence.
Should I offer a discount to settle?
That is a commercial decision for your business. Some creditors accept a reduced sum to avoid the cost and time of a claim. A solicitor can help you weigh up the offer, but they will not decide for you, and any settlement should be recorded clearly in writing.
Will a dispute go to court?
Not always. Many disputes settle through negotiation once both sides have set out their positions. If agreement cannot be reached, a claim may be issued and the court may expect the parties to consider mediation or other ways of settling. Your solicitor will guide you through each stage.
What evidence helps if the customer disputes the invoice?
Useful evidence includes the signed order or accepted quote, your terms and conditions, delivery notes or proof of completion, photos, timesheets and any messages where the customer approved the work or asked for changes. Emails in which the customer accepts the debt or promises to pay can be especially helpful. Your solicitor will tell you what matters most.
How do I know who I contracted with?
Look at the order, the email that placed it, any account application and the name on your invoices. If the customer is a company, check its registered name and number on Companies House. A solicitor will confirm the correct party before sending any formal letter or claim.
The customer has changed its name. Can I still claim?
A company that changes its name is still the same legal entity, so the debt remains. If the business has moved to a different company, the position is more complicated. A solicitor can check the company records and explain who is liable.
What if the customer is a sole trader?
A sole trader is personally responsible for the debts of the business. Different pre-action rules apply where the debtor is an individual, including a longer response period. Insolvency routes are also different. A solicitor will explain how this affects your options.
