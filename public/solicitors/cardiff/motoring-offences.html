Motoring offences explained

General information for people and businesses in Cardiff. Pick a topic to read more.

Motoring law covers the offences that can be committed by anyone who drives, rides or keeps a vehicle on a road or in a public place. Some are minor and are dealt with by a fixed penalty in the post. Others lead to a court hearing, penalty points, a driving ban and in the most serious cases a prison sentence. Almost all motoring cases in England and Wales start in the magistrates' court, and most finish there too.

For many people the real worry is not the fine. It is the licence. Losing the right to drive can affect a job, a business, caring for a relative or getting children to school. That is why people often look for a solicitor who deals with motoring cases every day, rather than a general high street firm or no help at all.

A specialist motoring solicitor will usually start by reading the paperwork, checking the dates and looking at the evidence the police or prosecutor relies on. They will then explain in plain terms what the likely outcomes are, whether there is anything to challenge, and what the court will be looking at. If you are pleading guilty, they can put forward mitigation, which is information about you and the offence that may help the court reach a fair sentence.

Motoring cases also move quickly and to fixed dates. A notice may give you a short time to reply, and a court date is rarely moved without a good reason. People who get in touch with a solicitor early tend to have more time to gather documents, find witnesses or arrange evidence about how a ban would affect them. Leaving it until the morning of the hearing narrows what anyone can do.

Most motoring offences are endorsable. That means a conviction is recorded on your driving record with an offence code and, for many offences, a number of penalty points. The number of points depends on the offence and sometimes on how serious the court thinks it was. Some offences have a fixed number of points and others have a range.

How points add up

Points are counted under what is known as the totting up system. If you reach 12 or more points from offences committed within three years of each other, the court will usually disqualify you for at least six months. The minimum is longer if you have been banned before in the recent past. Points stay on your record for four years for most offences, and for eleven years for drink and drug driving related offences, even though only some of them count towards totting up.

Different kinds of ban

Before passing sentence, the court will look at your driving record. Points that are still live for totting up purposes are taken into account, so an offence that would normally mean three points can have a much bigger effect if you already have nine. This is one reason people often check their record before deciding how to respond to a notice.

A ban can happen in more than one way. Some offences, such as drink driving, carry an obligatory ban, so the court must disqualify unless there are special reasons not to. For other offences the court has a discretion to ban instead of adding points, often where the offence was serious, such as a very high speed. And then there is the totting up ban, which comes from the build up of points over time rather than from one offence on its own.

New drivers

Drivers in their first two years after passing their test have a lower limit. If they reach six points in that period, the DVLA will usually revoke their licence and they will need to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again. This happens automatically and is separate from the court's own powers.

Extended retests

For some of the most serious offences, such as dangerous driving, the court must order an extended retest. The driver cannot simply get their licence back at the end of the ban. They need to pass a longer, harder test first.

Motoring cases follow the criminal court process in England and Wales. The details vary with the offence, but most cases go through the same broad stages. A solicitor will tell you where your own case is and what comes next.

Notices and paperwork

For many offences caught on camera, the first thing you receive is a notice of intended prosecution and a request to name the driver. For offences where you were stopped, you may have been told at the roadside or charged at a police station. Cases then usually reach court by a postal requisition, a written charge with a Single Justice Procedure notice, or a charge sheet with a date to attend.

Single Justice Procedure

Many lower level motoring cases are dealt with by a single magistrate on the papers, without a hearing in open court. You are sent a notice and asked to respond within a set time, pleading guilty or not guilty and giving details of your finances. If you plead not guilty, or if a ban is being considered and you want to be heard, the case can be moved to a magistrates' court hearing.

First hearing

At the first hearing in the magistrates' court you are asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you there and then, or adjourn for reports or for a further hearing. If you plead not guilty, the court sets a trial date and gives directions about evidence.

Most hearings take place at the magistrates' court for the area where the offence happened, which may be some way from where you live. Hearings are usually listed for the morning or afternoon, and you may have to wait several hours to be called. Your solicitor can tell you what to expect on the day, where to go and what to bring.

Trial and sentence

At a trial, the prosecution has to prove the offence. Witnesses may give evidence and be cross examined. If you are found guilty, or have pleaded guilty, the court passes sentence using guidelines published by the Sentencing Council. A few serious offences, such as dangerous driving, can be sent to the Crown Court.

Appeals

After a magistrates' court decision there is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court, within a strict time limit. A solicitor can explain whether an appeal is possible and what it involves.

Motoring law is a specialist area. The rules on notices, time limits, evidence and sentencing are technical, and local courts each have their own way of doing things. Many people prefer a firm that handles motoring work as a main part of its practice.

Solicitors and barristers

It also helps to think about what you want from the firm. Some people want a quick review and a view on the likely outcome. Your solicitor may attend court themselves or instruct a barrister to represent you. Both are regulated. If a barrister is used, ask whether their fee is included in the price you have been given.

Regulation

Every firm we introduce is regulated, usually by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Regulated firms must be clear about costs, have a complaints procedure and hold insurance. You can check a firm on the regulator's online register.

Legal aid is rarely available for motoring cases, because it is usually only granted where there is a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately for help with a motoring matter.

How firms usually charge

Many motoring firms charge fixed fees for each stage, such as an initial review, a guilty plea hearing, an exceptional hardship hearing or a trial. Others charge by the hour for complex cases. Trials cost more than guilty pleas because of the preparation and court time involved. Some cases also need expert evidence, such as a report on a speed device or a blood analysis, which is usually an extra cost.

Court costs and fines

If you are convicted, the court will usually order a fine, a victim surcharge and a contribution to the prosecution's costs. Fines for many offences are linked to your income. These amounts are set by the court and are separate from your solicitor's fees.

When you compare quotes, look at what is included rather than just the headline number. A low fee for a guilty plea may not include travel to a distant court, a barrister's fee or work on a separate exceptional hardship argument. A good firm will confirm the fee and what it covers in writing before any work starts.

If you are acquitted

If you are found not guilty, you may be able to recover some of your legal costs from public funds. The amount that can be recovered is usually limited and is often less than what you paid, so ask the firm how this works before you start.

Try to be open with the solicitor about what happened, even the parts you are unsure of. What you tell them is confidential, and they can only help if they have the full picture.

If you think a ban would cause problems for other people, such as an employer, a relative you care for or your own staff, it can help to note down who they are and how they depend on your driving. Your solicitor can then explain whether that information is relevant and what kind of evidence the court usually expects to see.

How long do drugs stay in the blood?
It varies a great deal between drugs and between people, depending on things like the dose, how often the drug is used, body weight and metabolism. Some substances can remain in the blood for longer than people expect, even after they no longer feel any effect. There is no reliable rule of thumb, which is why many cases need careful scientific review.
What is the roadside drug test?
It is a preliminary test, usually a saliva swab on a handheld device, that screens for some common drugs such as cannabis and cocaine. A positive result usually leads to arrest and a blood test at the police station. The roadside result on its own is not normally the evidence of the level used to prosecute.
Can I refuse a blood test?
Refusing to give a blood sample without a reasonable excuse is an offence in itself, with penalties similar to drug driving. A reasonable excuse is usually medical, such as a genuine phobia of needles supported by evidence. Whether an excuse is reasonable is decided by the court. A solicitor can explain how this is assessed.
Why is my case taking so long?
Blood samples must be analysed by an accredited laboratory, and this can take weeks or months. You are usually released while you wait and are not charged until the results are back. The delay can be stressful, but it also gives time to gather documents, such as prescriptions, and to speak to a solicitor before any court date.
Can I get my own sample tested?
When blood is taken, you should be offered part of the sample. You can ask an independent laboratory to analyse it, usually through your solicitor, who can arrange secure collection and storage. Independent analysis has a cost and is not useful in every case. Your solicitor can explain whether it is likely to help in yours.
What if the level is only just over the limit?
Laboratory results come with a margin of uncertainty, and reports usually take this into account. Where a level is close to the limit, a solicitor may want to look closely at how the analysis was done and what the uncertainty is. Whether this makes a difference depends on the evidence in the particular case.
Could passive exposure explain a positive result?
People sometimes say they were near others using drugs rather than taking them themselves. The limits for illegal drugs were set with accidental exposure in mind, so this explanation is not straightforward. Whether it has any relevance would usually need expert evidence. A solicitor can talk it through after seeing the analyst's report.
What is a field impairment test?
It is a set of simple physical tasks an officer may ask you to carry out, such as walking heel to toe, standing on one leg or following a moving object with your eyes. The officer records how you perform. It is mainly used to decide whether to arrest and can be relevant evidence in the separate offence of driving while unfit through drugs.
Can I be prosecuted for taking my prescribed medicine?
It is possible if your medicine is on the specified list and your blood level is over its limit, or if your driving was impaired. However, there is a medical defence to the specified limit offence where the medicine was lawfully prescribed and taken in line with the advice given. A solicitor will look at the prescription and how you took it.
What if the car is a company car?
Where a company is the registered keeper, the company must name the driver. The notice to the driver then follows. Companies that do not keep proper records of who uses their vehicles can find it hard to respond, and the company itself can be prosecuted for failing to name the driver. Many fleet managers ask a solicitor to review their process.
How many points will I get for speeding?
Speeding carries between three and six points. A fixed penalty is normally three points. If the case goes to court, the number of points depends on how far over the limit you were, along with other factors, using the Sentencing Council guideline. For higher speeds the court can impose a short ban instead of points.
Can I be banned for speeding?
Yes, in two main ways. The court can impose a discretionary ban for the speeding offence itself, usually where the speed was well above the limit. Or the points for the offence can take you to 12 or more within three years, which normally means a totting up ban of at least six months.
Who decides whether I get a speed awareness course?
The police decide, following national guidance. A course is usually offered where the speed was not much above the limit and you have not done a similar course recently. You cannot insist on one, and it is not available once a case has gone to court. If you are offered a course, you pay the provider directly.
Does a speed awareness course go on my record?
Completing a course means no points and no conviction. The police keep a record that you attended, which is used to decide whether you can be offered another course. Some insurers ask whether you have attended a course, so it is worth answering their questions accurately.
How is the fine worked out?
If the case goes to court, the fine is based on the seriousness of the offence and your weekly income, following the Sentencing Council guideline. You will be asked for details of your finances. If you do not provide them, the court can make assumptions about your income. The court also adds a victim surcharge and usually prosecution costs.
What happens if I am a new driver?
If you passed your test within the last two years and reach six or more points, the DVLA will usually revoke your licence. Two speeding offences can be enough. You would then need to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again. The court cannot prevent this if the points are imposed.
Will speeding affect my insurance?
A speeding conviction or points usually needs to be declared to insurers when they ask, and it may increase premiums, particularly if there is more than one. A speed awareness course is not a conviction, but some insurers ask about courses too. Answering insurers' questions accurately protects your cover if you need to make a claim.
How long do speeding points last?
Speeding points stay on your driving record for four years from the date of the offence. For totting up purposes, the court looks at offences committed within three years of each other. So points can still show on your record for a while after they stop counting towards a totting up ban. You can check your record on GOV.UK.
Does a speed awareness course add points?
No. A speed awareness course is offered instead of prosecution, so completing it means no conviction and no points. It does not count towards totting up. Whether a course is offered is a decision for the police, and it is not usually available once a case has gone to court or if you have done a similar course recently.
Do points from a fixed penalty count?
Yes. Points accepted through a fixed penalty are endorsed on your licence in the same way as points imposed by a court, and they count towards totting up. That is why a fixed penalty is not usually offered if it would take you to 12 points. In that situation, the case goes to court so that a ban can be considered.
How long is a totting up ban?
The minimum is six months. It rises to at least one year if you have had one ban of 56 days or more in the three years before the new offence, and at least two years if you have had two or more. The court can impose a longer ban, but usually imposes the minimum unless there are aggravating features.
Is a ban automatic once I reach 12 points?
Not automatic, but close to it. The law says the court must disqualify unless there are mitigating circumstances, which in practice means exceptional hardship. The court cannot take into account ordinary hardship or the fact that the new offence was minor. A solicitor can explain whether an exceptional hardship argument might be available.
Do I have to retake my test after a totting up ban?
No, not normally. At the end of the ban you apply to the DVLA to get your licence back. Retests are ordered for some serious offences, and new drivers who reach six points within two years face revocation and must pass again, but a standard totting up ban does not require a new test.
Can I drive while I wait for court?
In most cases, yes. A ban starts on the day the court imposes it. If the case is adjourned, the court may sometimes impose an interim ban before sentence. Until then, you can usually drive as long as your licence is valid and you are insured.
What happens if I drive while banned?
Driving while disqualified is a serious criminal offence. It can lead to a further ban, a community order or a custodial sentence, and your insurance will be invalid. It is treated far more seriously than the offence that led to the ban. Whatever the pressures, the ban must be respected for its whole length.
Will a totting up ban affect my job?
It can, especially if you drive for work or your contract requires a valid licence. Some employers can adjust duties during a ban, others cannot. Your contract or employer's policies will set out what happens. The effect on your job may also be relevant if you are considering an exceptional hardship argument.
Is a totting up ban the same as a ban for drink driving?
No. A drink driving ban is an obligatory ban for that offence and is not about points. A totting up ban comes from reaching 12 points across several offences. The rules for each are different. For example, exceptional hardship is an argument against a totting up ban, while drink driving bans can only be avoided in rare cases through special reasons.
Who has to prove exceptional hardship?
The driver does. The court must be satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that a ban would cause exceptional hardship. That means it is more likely than not. The court will usually expect evidence from the driver and supporting documents, and sometimes evidence from other people who would be affected.
Does it always work?
No. Some arguments succeed and some do not. The outcome depends on the circumstances, the quality of the evidence and how the court sees the case. A solicitor can give a realistic view of the strengths and weaknesses of your circumstances, but no one can guarantee a result.
Can a business owner argue exceptional hardship?
Yes. Business owners often argue that a ban would affect the business and the people who work for it. The court will want evidence, such as accounts, contracts and details of staff, and will look at whether someone else could drive or whether the business could adapt for the period of the ban. The effect on employees can carry particular weight.
Can I argue it if I only have a short time before court?
It may still be possible, but less time means less opportunity to gather documents and arrange witnesses. If the evidence is not ready, a solicitor may ask the court to adjourn, which the court can agree to or refuse. Mentioning your hearing date as soon as you get in touch helps the firm judge what can realistically be done.
What evidence will I need?
It depends on your circumstances. Common examples include a letter from your employer, your employment contract, medical letters about a relative you care for, evidence of public transport options and costs, business records and evidence of your finances. Your solicitor will tell you which documents matter most in your case and how they should be presented.
Will I have to give evidence myself?
Usually yes. The court normally expects to hear from the driver in person, under oath or affirmation. You will explain your circumstances and be asked questions by your own representative, the prosecutor and sometimes the court. Your solicitor will help you prepare so that you know what to expect.
Can other people give evidence for me?
Yes. Witnesses who would be affected by a ban, such as an employer, a business partner or a family member, can give evidence. They usually need to attend court in person and can be asked questions by the prosecutor. Letters alone may carry less weight than a witness the court can hear from directly.
Is losing my job enough?
It can be part of an argument, but courts do not always treat job loss as exceptional on its own, because it is a common consequence of a ban. It may carry more weight with clear evidence the job would be lost, and where the loss would have serious effects on others, such as dependants. A solicitor can explain how courts tend to view this.
What if my employer could change my duties?
The court is likely to ask whether your employer could adjust your role during the ban, or whether a colleague could drive. If that is possible, it may weaken the argument. If it is not, evidence from your employer explaining why can help. Being open about alternatives usually helps the court trust the rest of your evidence.
Do I have to go to court?
For careless driving you will usually be asked to attend the first hearing. If you are pleading not guilty, you will need to attend the trial. Your solicitor can tell you whether you need to be there for other hearings. If the court decides to disqualify you, it may want you present. Not attending when you are required to can lead to a warrant.
What happens at the first hearing?
You confirm your name and details and are asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court will hear the facts from the prosecution and any mitigation, then decide the sentence. If you plead not guilty, the court will fix a trial date and decide what evidence and witnesses are needed. A solicitor can attend with you or on your behalf for parts of the process.
Who decides the case?
Careless driving is decided in the magistrates' court. The case will be heard either by a bench of usually three lay magistrates, who are trained volunteers, or by a district judge, who is a legally qualified judge sitting alone. They are advised on the law by a legal adviser. There is no jury.
What evidence will the prosecution use?
It depends on the case. Common evidence includes statements from the other driver and witnesses, police officer accounts, photographs, dashcam or CCTV footage, collision investigation reports and anything you said in interview or in writing. The prosecution should provide initial details of its case before the first hearing, and your solicitor will review it.
Can I give my side of the story?
Yes. If you plead not guilty, you can give evidence at your trial and be questioned by the prosecution. You can also call your own witnesses. Your solicitor will advise you on whether giving evidence is likely to help and will prepare you for what to expect.
Can I appeal if I am convicted?
You can appeal to the Crown Court against your conviction, your sentence or both. The time limit for lodging an appeal is short, usually 21 days from sentence. In some cases you can also ask the magistrates to reopen the case. A solicitor can advise on whether an appeal has reasonable prospects.
What is mitigation?
Mitigation is information put before the court to explain the circumstances of the offence and your personal situation, with the aim of reducing the sentence. It might cover your previous driving record, why the incident happened, any remorse you have shown, and how a ban or high fine would affect you and others. A solicitor knows what courts tend to find relevant and how to present it clearly.
How many points will I get?
If you are not disqualified, the court must give between 3 and 9 points. The number depends on how serious the court considers the driving to be, following sentencing guidelines. Minor lapses tend to be at the lower end, while more prolonged or risky driving attracts more points. A solicitor can explain where your case might fall.
Could I be banned from driving?
Yes. Careless driving does not carry a compulsory ban, but the court can disqualify you if it thinks the driving was serious enough. You can also face a ban if the points you receive take you to 12 or more within three years. A solicitor can tell you how likely a ban is and what can be argued.
