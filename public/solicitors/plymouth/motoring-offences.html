Motoring offences explained

General information for people and businesses in Plymouth. Pick a topic to read more.

Motoring law covers the offences that can be committed by anyone who drives, rides or keeps a vehicle on a road or in a public place. Some are minor and are dealt with by a fixed penalty in the post. Others lead to a court hearing, penalty points, a driving ban and in the most serious cases a prison sentence. Almost all motoring cases in England and Wales start in the magistrates' court, and most finish there too.

For many people the real worry is not the fine. It is the licence. Losing the right to drive can affect a job, a business, caring for a relative or getting children to school. That is why people often look for a solicitor who deals with motoring cases every day, rather than a general high street firm or no help at all.

A specialist motoring solicitor will usually start by reading the paperwork, checking the dates and looking at the evidence the police or prosecutor relies on. They will then explain in plain terms what the likely outcomes are, whether there is anything to challenge, and what the court will be looking at. If you are pleading guilty, they can put forward mitigation, which is information about you and the offence that may help the court reach a fair sentence.

Motoring cases also move quickly and to fixed dates. A notice may give you a short time to reply, and a court date is rarely moved without a good reason. People who get in touch with a solicitor early tend to have more time to gather documents, find witnesses or arrange evidence about how a ban would affect them. Leaving it until the morning of the hearing narrows what anyone can do.

Most motoring offences are endorsable. That means a conviction is recorded on your driving record with an offence code and, for many offences, a number of penalty points. The number of points depends on the offence and sometimes on how serious the court thinks it was. Some offences have a fixed number of points and others have a range.

How points add up

Points are counted under what is known as the totting up system. If you reach 12 or more points from offences committed within three years of each other, the court will usually disqualify you for at least six months. The minimum is longer if you have been banned before in the recent past. Points stay on your record for four years for most offences, and for eleven years for drink and drug driving related offences, even though only some of them count towards totting up.

Different kinds of ban

Before passing sentence, the court will look at your driving record. Points that are still live for totting up purposes are taken into account, so an offence that would normally mean three points can have a much bigger effect if you already have nine. This is one reason people often check their record before deciding how to respond to a notice.

A ban can happen in more than one way. Some offences, such as drink driving, carry an obligatory ban, so the court must disqualify unless there are special reasons not to. For other offences the court has a discretion to ban instead of adding points, often where the offence was serious, such as a very high speed. And then there is the totting up ban, which comes from the build up of points over time rather than from one offence on its own.

New drivers

Drivers in their first two years after passing their test have a lower limit. If they reach six points in that period, the DVLA will usually revoke their licence and they will need to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again. This happens automatically and is separate from the court's own powers.

Extended retests

For some of the most serious offences, such as dangerous driving, the court must order an extended retest. The driver cannot simply get their licence back at the end of the ban. They need to pass a longer, harder test first.

Motoring cases follow the criminal court process in England and Wales. The details vary with the offence, but most cases go through the same broad stages. A solicitor will tell you where your own case is and what comes next.

Notices and paperwork

For many offences caught on camera, the first thing you receive is a notice of intended prosecution and a request to name the driver. For offences where you were stopped, you may have been told at the roadside or charged at a police station. Cases then usually reach court by a postal requisition, a written charge with a Single Justice Procedure notice, or a charge sheet with a date to attend.

Single Justice Procedure

Many lower level motoring cases are dealt with by a single magistrate on the papers, without a hearing in open court. You are sent a notice and asked to respond within a set time, pleading guilty or not guilty and giving details of your finances. If you plead not guilty, or if a ban is being considered and you want to be heard, the case can be moved to a magistrates' court hearing.

First hearing

At the first hearing in the magistrates' court you are asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you there and then, or adjourn for reports or for a further hearing. If you plead not guilty, the court sets a trial date and gives directions about evidence.

Most hearings take place at the magistrates' court for the area where the offence happened, which may be some way from where you live. Hearings are usually listed for the morning or afternoon, and you may have to wait several hours to be called. Your solicitor can tell you what to expect on the day, where to go and what to bring.

Trial and sentence

At a trial, the prosecution has to prove the offence. Witnesses may give evidence and be cross examined. If you are found guilty, or have pleaded guilty, the court passes sentence using guidelines published by the Sentencing Council. A few serious offences, such as dangerous driving, can be sent to the Crown Court.

Appeals

After a magistrates' court decision there is usually a right of appeal to the Crown Court, within a strict time limit. A solicitor can explain whether an appeal is possible and what it involves.

Motoring law is a specialist area. The rules on notices, time limits, evidence and sentencing are technical, and local courts each have their own way of doing things. Many people prefer a firm that handles motoring work as a main part of its practice.

Solicitors and barristers

It also helps to think about what you want from the firm. Some people want a quick review and a view on the likely outcome. Others want a solicitor to handle everything, from writing to the prosecutor to standing up in court. Being clear about this from the start makes it easier for the firm to give you an accurate fee.

Your solicitor may attend court themselves or instruct a barrister to represent you. Both are regulated. If a barrister is used, ask whether their fee is included in the price you have been given.

Regulation

Every firm we introduce is regulated, usually by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Regulated firms must be clear about costs, have a complaints procedure and hold insurance. You can check a firm on the regulator's online register.

Leaflegal introduces you to one firm, so you are not passed between lots of different companies. You are free to decide whether to instruct them.

Legal aid is rarely available for motoring cases, because it is usually only granted where there is a real risk of prison or other special factors. Most people pay privately for help with a motoring matter.

How firms usually charge

Many motoring firms charge fixed fees for each stage, such as an initial review, a guilty plea hearing, an exceptional hardship hearing or a trial. Others charge by the hour for complex cases. Trials cost more than guilty pleas because of the preparation and court time involved. Some cases also need expert evidence, such as a report on a speed device or a blood analysis, which is usually an extra cost.

Court costs and fines

If you are convicted, the court will usually order a fine, a victim surcharge and a contribution to the prosecution's costs. Fines for many offences are linked to your income. These amounts are set by the court and are separate from your solicitor's fees.

When you compare quotes, look at what is included rather than just the headline number. A low fee for a guilty plea may not include travel to a distant court, a barrister's fee or work on a separate exceptional hardship argument. A good firm will confirm the fee and what it covers in writing before any work starts.

If you are acquitted

If you are found not guilty, you may be able to recover some of your legal costs from public funds. The amount that can be recovered is usually limited and is often less than what you paid, so ask the firm how this works before you start.

  • The date of the offence and the date of any court hearing

Try to be open with the solicitor about what happened, even the parts you are unsure of. What you tell them is confidential, and they can only help if they have the full picture.

If you think a ban would cause problems for other people, such as an employer, a relative you care for or your own staff, it can help to note down who they are and how they depend on your driving. Your solicitor can then explain whether that information is relevant and what kind of evidence the court usually expects to see.

Motoring offences in Plymouth, questions and answers

Do you only introduce firms based in Plymouth?
We introduce you to a firm that covers Plymouth. Some have offices nearby, and others work remotely across England and Wales. The firm will tell you how they work when they contact you.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal in Plymouth?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm explains its own fees before you agree to anything.
Do you cover Plymstock and Saltash?
Yes. We cover Plymouth and the surrounding area, including Plymstock, Saltash, Ivybridge and Tavistock.
Which areas do you cover?
Motoring offences dealt with in the courts of England and Wales.
Is there legal aid for motoring offences?
Usually not, unless you are at risk of prison. We only introduce people looking for paid legal help.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm aims to contact you quickly, especially if you have a court date coming up. Please give a mobile number you can answer.
The other driver was also at fault. Does that help me?
It may be relevant. The question for the court is whether your driving fell below the standard, not whether someone else was more to blame. However, the other driver's actions can explain why an incident happened and may show that your driving was reasonable in the circumstances. A solicitor will look at the evidence of both drivers' actions.
What if I was charged with dangerous driving instead?
Dangerous driving is a more serious offence with a compulsory ban. If you have been charged with it, the court can convict you of careless driving instead if it decides the driving was poor but not far below the standard. A solicitor dealing with a dangerous driving charge will usually consider whether this is a realistic outcome.
How long do the police have to bring a case?
There are time limits for starting a prosecution for careless driving, and there are rules about warning the driver within 14 days unless an accident happened that the driver knew about. Whether these rules were followed depends on the facts. If you think the case has been brought late or you were never warned, ask a solicitor to check.
What is a notice of intended prosecution?
It is a formal warning that the police are considering prosecuting a driver for certain offences, including careless driving. It is usually sent to the registered keeper within 14 days of the incident, or given verbally at the scene. Where there was an accident that the driver was aware of, no notice is usually needed. The notice is not a charge, and receiving one does not mean a case will definitely follow.
Can the police offer a course instead of court?
Sometimes. For less serious careless driving, the police may offer a driver awareness course or a fixed penalty with points. These are offered at the discretion of the police and depend on the circumstances, your history and local policy. There is no right to be offered one. If you are offered a course, the paperwork will explain the conditions and the deadline for accepting it.
Do I have to go to court?
For careless driving you will usually be asked to attend the first hearing. If you are pleading not guilty, you will need to attend the trial. Your solicitor can tell you whether you need to be there for other hearings. If the court decides to disqualify you, it may want you present. Not attending when you are required to can lead to a warrant.
What happens at the first hearing?
You confirm your name and details and are asked to enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court will hear the facts from the prosecution and any mitigation, then decide the sentence. If you plead not guilty, the court will fix a trial date and decide what evidence and witnesses are needed. A solicitor can attend with you or on your behalf for parts of the process.
Who decides the case?
Careless driving is decided in the magistrates' court. The case will be heard either by a bench of usually three lay magistrates, who are trained volunteers, or by a district judge, who is a legally qualified judge sitting alone. They are advised on the law by a legal adviser. There is no jury.
What evidence will the prosecution use?
It depends on the case. Common evidence includes statements from the other driver and witnesses, police officer accounts, photographs, dashcam or CCTV footage, collision investigation reports and anything you said in interview or in writing. The prosecution should provide initial details of its case before the first hearing, and your solicitor will review it.
Is a police chase always dangerous driving?
Not automatically. The prosecution must still prove that the driving itself fell far below the expected standard. Failing to stop for the police is a separate offence. In practice, driving during a pursuit is often closely examined using police camera footage, and the court looks at the speed, the roads and the risk to other people.
Can the charge be reduced to careless driving?
Sometimes. The court can convict of careless driving as an alternative if it is not sure the driving was dangerous. The prosecution may also accept a plea to careless driving in some cases. Whether this is realistic depends on the evidence, and a solicitor can tell you once they have reviewed it.
What if someone was injured?
If dangerous driving causes serious injury or death, different and more serious offences apply. These are always heard in the Crown Court and carry much longer sentences. Tell the firm straight away if anyone was hurt, even if the police have not yet said what charge they are considering.
Do I have to receive a warning before being prosecuted?
For dangerous driving, the police usually have to warn the driver that a prosecution is being considered, either at the scene or by a notice of intended prosecution sent within 14 days. There is an exception where an accident happened that the driver was aware of. Whether the rules were followed depends on the facts, and a solicitor can check this when reviewing the case.
Should I have a solicitor at my police interview?
Leaflegal cannot advise you on that. What we can say is that everyone interviewed under caution has a right to free legal advice, whatever their income. A solicitor can find out what the police allege before the interview and talk through how to approach it. The firm we introduce you to can explain this in more detail.
What does released under investigation mean?
It means the police have released you without charge and without bail conditions while they continue their enquiries. There is no fixed date for a decision. You may later be charged, interviewed again or told no further action will be taken. Your solicitor can contact the officer in the case for updates.
Will my case go to the Crown Court?
Not necessarily. Dangerous driving can be heard in either court. The magistrates decide whether the case is suitable for them, and you can choose a jury trial in the Crown Court if you plead not guilty. Magistrates can also send a case to the Crown Court for sentence. A solicitor will explain the pros and cons of each court for your case.
What happens at the first hearing?
The first hearing takes place at the magistrates' court. You confirm your details and indicate whether you intend to plead guilty or not guilty. The court then decides where the case should be heard. If the case stays in the magistrates' court and you plead guilty, sentence may happen that day or later after a report.
Will I need a barrister?
Crown Court cases usually involve a barrister, who represents you at hearings and trial, working with your solicitor. Some solicitors have higher rights of audience and can appear in the Crown Court themselves. In the magistrates' court, a solicitor normally represents you. Your firm will explain who will be doing what.
What if the car is shared by several people?
Sharing a car does not remove the duty to name the driver. The keeper is expected to find out who was driving, for example by checking diaries, fuel receipts and asking everyone who used the car. If, after real effort, you cannot find out, a solicitor can explain whether the reasonable diligence defence might apply.
I sold the car before the date on the notice. What now?
If you were no longer the keeper on the date of the alleged offence, you can usually say so in your response and give details of the buyer. Evidence such as the sale receipt and confirmation that you told the DVLA will help. If the DVLA record was not updated, a solicitor can explain how this affects your position.
What if the car was stolen or cloned?
If you believe the car was stolen at the time, or that your registration has been copied onto another vehicle, explain this in your response and provide whatever evidence you have, such as a crime reference number or photographs. A solicitor can help you present this clearly if the police do not accept it.
Do I have to sign the form?
The notice will tell you how to respond, which may include signing a declaration or completing an online submission. Follow those instructions exactly. If you are unsure whether a response you sent was valid, or the police say it was incomplete, a solicitor can look at what you sent.
I lent my car to a friend. What do I need to do?
As the keeper, you are expected to give the name and address of the person who was driving. If you lent the car to a friend, that usually means naming them. The police may then send them their own notice. If you are unsure who had the car at the time, think about what records or messages might help you find out, and speak to a solicitor if you are still unsure.
Is there a defence if I genuinely do not know who was driving?
There may be. The keeper has a defence if they can show they did not know and could not have found out with reasonable diligence. Courts look closely at what steps were actually taken. Simply saying you do not know is unlikely to be enough. A solicitor can tell you whether your efforts might meet the test.
I never received the notice. Is that a defence?
It may be. The law usually presumes a notice posted to the right address was received, but that can sometimes be challenged with evidence. If the notice went to an old address because you had not updated your details, the position is more difficult. A solicitor can look at how and where the notice was sent.
What if I was ill or abroad when it arrived?
If it was not reasonably practicable to respond within 28 days, and you responded as soon as it was reasonably practicable, that can be a defence. You will usually need evidence, such as medical records or travel documents. A solicitor can explain how to present this to the police or the court.
Can I name a few people who might have been driving?
Listing several possible drivers does not usually meet the requirement to identify the driver. However, a genuine explanation of who had access and what you did to find out may be relevant to the reasonable diligence defence. A solicitor can look at what you know and explain how the law treats it.
Does it matter that I was only driving a short distance?
The offence can be committed over any distance, even moving a car a few metres on a public road. However, in some cases the distance driven and the reason for driving, such as a genuine emergency, may be relevant to whether special reasons exist or to the penalty. A solicitor can explain how courts tend to view the circumstances in your case.
Does my policy let me drive other cars?
Some policies include driving other cars cover, but many do not, and where it is included it is often limited to third party cover for certain drivers. It may not apply to cars you own or that are registered to you. Check your certificate and policy wording. A solicitor can look at the exact terms if you have been charged.
My insurer cancelled my policy without telling me. What then?
Insurers usually have to follow the cancellation terms in the policy, including giving notice. Whether you were insured on the day depends on the dates and what the insurer did. Ask the insurer for copies of any cancellation letters and when they were sent. A solicitor can review these and explain how they affect your case.
Is food delivery covered by my normal insurance?
Usually not. Delivering food or parcels for payment normally needs specific cover, often described as hire and reward or delivery cover. Standard social, domestic and commuting policies will not usually cover it. If you were stopped while working for a delivery platform, a solicitor will check your policy and any cover the platform provides.
I had just bought the car. Was I covered?
Insurance does not usually transfer automatically from one car to another. Some policies give short temporary cover for a newly bought vehicle, but many do not. Whether you were covered depends on your policy and what you told your insurer. A solicitor can look at the documents to see whether a defence or special reasons might apply.
What is the Motor Insurance Database?
It is the central record of insured vehicles in the UK. The police use it, often through number plate recognition cameras, to check whether a vehicle is insured. The database can occasionally be out of date or wrong. If you were insured but the database said otherwise, keep your certificate and contact your insurer to correct it.
What is continuous insurance enforcement?
It is the rule that the registered keeper must keep a vehicle insured unless it has been declared off the road with a SORN. The authorities compare vehicle records with insurance records and send letters to keepers of vehicles that appear uninsured. It is a separate matter from driving without insurance, and usually dealt with through penalties rather than points.
Is a learner driver covered by the family car insurance?
Not automatically. A learner usually needs to be named on the policy or have separate learner driver insurance, and must meet the conditions for supervised driving. If a learner was driving without the right cover, both the learner and the person who allowed them to drive could face charges. A solicitor can look at the policy and the circumstances.
How many points will I get?
If you are convicted and not disqualified, the court must give between 6 and 8 points. The number depends on the circumstances, such as how long the vehicle was uninsured and why. The court can disqualify you instead of giving points. If special reasons are found, the court can decide not to give any points at all.
What is the drink driving limit in England and Wales?
The limits are 35 microgrammes of alcohol in 100 millilitres of breath, 80 milligrammes in 100 millilitres of blood and 107 milligrammes in 100 millilitres of urine. The limits are lower in Scotland. There is no safe number of drinks that guarantees you will be under the limit, because the level depends on many things, including your weight, what you have eaten and how quickly your body processes alcohol. A solicitor can explain how the limits apply to your reading.
Can I be charged if I was not actually driving?
You can be charged with being in charge of a vehicle while over the limit, even if you were not driving. This can apply to someone sitting in a parked car with the keys. There is a defence if you can show there was no likelihood of you driving while you were still over the limit. A solicitor can explain how this works on your facts.
What does failing to provide a specimen mean?
It is an offence to fail, without a reasonable excuse, to provide a specimen of breath, blood or urine when properly asked. If you were driving or attempting to drive, it carries an obligatory ban like drink driving itself, and the DVLA treats it seriously. A reasonable excuse is usually medical. A solicitor will look at the circumstances and any medical evidence.
Is drink driving a criminal offence?
Yes. Drink driving is a criminal offence, and a conviction is a criminal conviction as well as an endorsement on your licence. It stays on your driving record for eleven years. It may need to be disclosed to insurers and some employers, and it can be relevant to travel to some countries. How long it must be disclosed in other contexts depends on the rehabilitation rules.
What if I was caught the morning after?
Morning after cases are treated in the same way as any other drink driving case. If the evidential reading is over the limit, the offence is made out, even if you felt sober. The circumstances, such as the time since your last drink, can be relevant to mitigation. A solicitor will explain how the court is likely to see it.
Is it different if I drove a very short distance?
Driving a short distance does not stop it being an offence. In rare cases, a very short distance in circumstances where there was little chance of danger can be raised as a special reason not to impose a ban. Courts look at this strictly and consider things like how far you drove, why, and the road and traffic conditions. A solicitor can say whether it is realistic.
Which reading is used against me?
If you gave breath at the police station, the lower of the two evidential readings is the one used. The roadside screening reading is not usually the one relied on in court. If blood or urine was taken instead, the analysed level from that sample is used. Your solicitor will see the printout or the analyst's certificate as part of the evidence.
Why did the police take blood instead of breath?
The police can ask for blood or urine if the breath machine is unreliable or unavailable, or if there is a medical reason you cannot give breath. In some situations, such as a hospital case, the procedure is different again. The reasons and the steps followed are recorded, and a solicitor will look at them as part of reviewing the case.
Can I get my blood sample tested independently?
When blood is taken, you should be offered part of the sample in a sealed container. You can arrange for it to be analysed by an independent laboratory. This is usually done through a solicitor, because the sample needs to be stored and handled correctly. Whether an independent analysis would be useful is something the solicitor can discuss with you.
