Trademarks explained

General information for people and businesses in Newcastle. Pick a topic to read more.

A trademark is the sign customers use to recognise your business. It is usually a name or a logo, but it can also be a slogan, a product name, a shape, a colour combination or even a sound. In the UK, trademarks are registered at the UK Intellectual Property Office, often called the UK IPO, and one registration covers England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Registering a trademark gives you the exclusive right to use it for the goods and services listed in the registration. That right makes it much easier to stop copycats, to take down lookalike listings online and to license or sell your brand later. Without a registration you may still have some protection through the law of passing off, but it is harder and usually more expensive to rely on.

Trademarks sit alongside other kinds of intellectual property. Copyright protects original creative work such as writing, photographs, artwork, music and software. Design rights protect the look of a product. Many businesses need help with more than one of these, which is why the firms we introduce handle copyright and designs as well as trademarks.

Who does trademark work

Trademark work is not a reserved legal activity, so in principle anyone can file an application. In practice most businesses use one of two kinds of regulated professional. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and can handle filings, disputes and court claims. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg, the regulator for patent and trade mark attorneys, and specialise in searching, filing and UK IPO proceedings such as oppositions.

Leaflegal only introduces trademark enquiries to firms regulated by one of those two bodies. Both sorts of firm work by phone, email and video, so the firm does not need to be near you. Because there is one UK register, we can introduce you wherever you are in the UK.

There is rarely only one way to deal with a brand question. The right route depends on what you sell, where you trade, how long you have used the name and how much risk you are prepared to accept. The firm will look at your own situation, but it helps to know the usual options before the first call.

Protecting a brand you already use

If you have been trading under a name for a while without registering it, the usual first step is a clearance search to see whether anyone has earlier rights, followed by an application for the classes that match what you sell. Using a name for years does not stop someone else registering something similar, so many owners file as soon as they know the brand is staying.

Launching something new

Before you commit to packaging, signage or a website, a search can tell you whether the name is likely to be free to use and register. If the search shows a problem, it is far cheaper to change the name at that stage. Some businesses file an application before launch so that the filing date is secured early.

When someone else is using a similar brand

Options range from a polite letter to a formal cease and desist letter, a takedown request to an online marketplace, an opposition at the UK IPO or a court claim. Many disputes settle without a hearing, sometimes through a coexistence agreement in which both sides agree how they will use their marks.

When you are on the receiving end

If you receive a letter, an opposition or a takedown notice, the firm can tell you how strong the other side's case looks and what your options are. Those may include changing the name, narrowing what you sell under it, negotiating a timetable to rebrand or defending your position.

Most trademark firms are small specialist practices or IP teams within larger law firms. Some focus on filings and portfolios, while others spend more time on disputes. When the firm contacts you, it is worth asking a few questions to check they are a good fit.

  • Are you a solicitor or a chartered trade mark attorney, and who regulates you?
  • How much of your work is UK trademark filing and how much is disputes?
  • Do you work with businesses of my size and in my sector?
  • Will you search before filing, and what will the search cover?
  • How do you charge, and what is included in a fixed fee?
  • Who will actually do the work and how will you keep me updated?

You can check a solicitor on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register and a chartered trade mark attorney on the IPReg register. Both registers are free to search online.

Solicitor or attorney

For a straightforward filing, a chartered trade mark attorney or a trademark solicitor can both do the job well. If a dispute looks likely to go to court, a solicitor can take the claim forward, and many attorney firms work closely with solicitors for that stage. Tell us in the form if you already know there is a dispute, so the introduction fits.

Firms outside the UK

If you also need protection abroad, ask whether the firm files international applications through the Madrid system and works with local agents in the countries you need. Most UK trademark firms do this as a matter of routine.

Trademark costs come in two parts. The first is the official fee charged by the UK IPO, which depends on how many classes your application covers. The second is the professional fee charged by the firm for its advice and work. Official fees change from time to time, so check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask the firm.

Many firms quote a fixed fee for a clearance search, a fixed fee for preparing and filing a UK application, and a further fixed fee if the examiner raises an objection that needs a reply. Renewals and recording a change of owner are also often fixed fee work.

Disputes are harder to price at the outset because they depend on how the other side responds. Firms usually charge in stages, for example a fee for reviewing the evidence and sending a letter, and a separate estimate for an opposition or court claim. Ask for an estimate for each stage and for an idea of the costs you could have to pay the other side if you lose.

  • Choose only the classes you really need now
  • Keep dated evidence of when and where you use your brand
  • Reply quickly to the firm's questions and to any official deadlines
  • Consider whether a negotiated settlement could save the cost of a hearing

Most trademark problems are avoidable with a little planning. These are the issues firms see most often, and why they matter.

Choosing a descriptive name

Names that describe the product, its quality or where it comes from are tempting because customers understand them straight away. They are also the names most likely to be refused, and the hardest to enforce against competitors who use similar words. A name with a twist, or an invented word, is usually easier to protect.

Filing too narrowly or too widely

An application that only covers part of what you sell can leave gaps that a competitor can use. An application that claims far more than you will ever sell can attract objections and become vulnerable to a non use challenge after five years. The wording of the goods and services is one of the most important parts of the application.

The wrong owner

Marks are sometimes filed in the name of a director or owner personally, or a company that later stops trading, when the business intends to own the brand. Investors and buyers often check this during due diligence. Putting it right usually means recording an assignment, which is simpler before there is a dispute or a sale.

Ignoring letters and deadlines

Examiner reports, oppositions and renewal reminders all have fixed deadlines. Letters from other brand owners usually have a deadline too. Leaving them until the last minute reduces your options, and missing them can mean losing rights altogether.

Scam invoices

After you file, your details appear on the public register. Businesses often receive official looking letters offering to publish or monitor their mark for a large fee. These are not from the UK IPO. If you are unsure about a letter, ask the firm before you pay anything.

Trademark matters run to official timetables set by the UK IPO, and many of the deadlines are fixed. Knowing roughly how long each stage takes helps you plan launches, investment rounds and marketing.

  • A clearance search usually takes a few days to a couple of weeks, depending on how wide it is
  • The UK IPO usually examines a new application within a few weeks of filing
  • Accepted applications are published for two months, which can be extended to three, so that others can oppose
  • If nobody opposes, registration usually follows around four months after filing
  • An opposition commonly adds many months, and longer if it goes all the way to a decision
  • A registration lasts ten years from the filing date and can be renewed again and again

Disputes outside the UK IPO move at their own pace. A cease and desist letter usually gives the other side a short deadline to reply, often around two weeks. Court claims take much longer, which is one reason many disputes settle early.

Deadlines that matter

The deadlines to watch most closely are the date to reply to an examiner's report, the end of the opposition period for an application you want to challenge, the deadline in any opposition you are defending and the renewal date for each registration. Missing some of these can mean losing rights altogether, so tell the firm about any date on your paperwork.

  • The name, logo or slogan, and a picture of how you use it
  • A short description of what you sell or plan to sell
  • When you started using the brand and where
  • Any trademark application or registration numbers
  • Any letters, notices or emails you have received, with their dates
  • Links or screenshots showing a third party's use, if there is a dispute
  • Where you trade now and where you might trade in future

If you are dealing with copying, keep a record of what you find with the date you found it. Screenshots that show the web address and the date are especially helpful. Avoid contacting the other side in anger before you have spoken to the firm, because what you say can matter later.

Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.

Can I challenge a registered design?
Yes. A registered design can be declared invalid, for example if it was not new or lacked individual character when filed. A successful challenge removes the registration. Evidence of earlier designs is key.
What is a must fit or must match exclusion?
UK unregistered design right doesn't protect features that must fit another product to work, or that must match the appearance of another article of which the product is part, such as some spare parts. The details are technical, and the firm will advise.
Can I stop copies being imported?
Customs can be asked to watch for goods that infringe registered designs and other IP rights entering the UK. An application to customs needs details of your rights and how to identify copies. The firm can help prepare it.
What if the copy is slightly different?
For a registered design, the question is whether the other product gives the informed user a different overall impression. Small changes may not be enough to avoid infringement. For unregistered rights, the question is whether the design was copied exactly or substantially. The firm will compare the products.
How long do I have to bring a claim?
Claims for damages are generally subject to a six year limitation period, but delay can weaken your case and make an injunction harder to obtain. Unregistered rights may also expire if you wait too long, so it is best to act promptly.
Can I license my design?
Yes. A licence lets a manufacturer or another brand use your design on agreed terms, often in return for a royalty. Licences of registered designs should be in writing. The firm can prepare or review the agreement.
Can I sell my design rights?
Yes, by assignment. An assignment of a registered design should be in writing and recorded at the UK IPO so the register shows the new owner.
Should I use the word registered on my product?
You can mark products with the words registered design and the registration number. Marking can make it harder for an infringer to claim they didn't know about your rights, which can affect damages. Never mark a product as registered if it isn't.
How do I protect a product design and its brand together?
Many product businesses register both the design of the product and the brand name as a trademark. The firm can plan both together and file them at the same time.
Can I use evidence that my brand is already well known?
Yes. If the examiner objects that a mark is not distinctive, evidence that customers have come to recognise it through your use can sometimes overcome the objection. The evidence needs to be detailed, such as sales figures, advertising spend and examples of use over time. The firm will explain what is needed.
How long does a trademark last?
A UK registration lasts ten years from the filing date and can be renewed every ten years indefinitely, as long as the renewal fees are paid. There is a six month grace period after expiry with a late fee.
Do I have to use my registered trademark?
Yes, to keep it secure. If a registered mark is not genuinely used in the UK for the registered goods or services for a continuous five year period, anyone can apply to revoke it. Keep dated evidence of use.
Can I add new products to my registration?
No. To cover new goods or services you need a new application. Many businesses review their portfolio when they launch a new line and file again for the new classes.
Can I sell or license my registered mark?
Yes. A registered mark can be assigned, licensed or used as security, like other property. Assignments and licences should be in writing and recorded at the UK IPO so the register reflects who owns and uses the mark.
Does registration stop all similar names?
Not automatically. Registration gives you the right to act, but you need to watch for conflicts and take action. Others may also have earlier rights or be able to use similar signs in ways that don't cause confusion.
What should I do with my registration certificate?
Keep it safely with your business records, along with the filing receipt and any correspondence. Note the renewal date in your diary. Investors, buyers and online marketplaces may ask for your registration number, which you can also find on the public register.
Can I register a trademark for a brand I sell online only?
Yes. Online sellers can and often do register their marks, and many marketplaces offer extra brand protection tools to owners of registered trademarks. Make sure the specification covers both the goods you sell and, where relevant, your online retail services.
How does Leaflegal help with trademark registration?
You answer a few questions about your brand and what you sell. We pass your enquiry to one regulated firm, either a solicitor or a chartered trade mark attorney, that handles UK filings. They contact you to discuss the mark, the classes and their fees.
Should I search before or after choosing a designer?
Ideally before the final design is approved, and certainly before printing. A name can be checked early, and the logo can be checked once it is close to final. It is cheaper to adjust a design at concept stage.
Do I need to search again later?
If you expand into new goods or new countries, a fresh search for those areas is sensible. A watch service can also alert you to new marks that could conflict with yours over time.
Is a search included when I file a trademark?
Some firms include a basic search in their filing fee and others charge for it separately. Ask what is included. A search included in a filing package may be narrower than a full clearance search with a written opinion.
Can I get a search done urgently?
Many firms can turn around a screening search quickly if there is a real deadline, such as a printer's date or an investor meeting. Full clearance searches take longer because of the analysis involved. Mention any deadline in the form.
What should I do if the search is clear?
Many businesses file a trademark application straight away, so the filing date is secured. The firm can prepare the application using what they learned from the search. Searches go out of date as new applications are filed, so it is best not to wait too long.
What is a trademark watch?
A watch service monitors new trademark applications and alerts you when someone applies for a mark similar to yours. That gives you time to oppose before the mark is registered. Firms usually charge a yearly fee for watching.
Can I rely on a search done by my designer or branding agency?
Agencies sometimes run basic checks, which can be helpful. They are not usually a legal clearance search with an opinion from a regulated professional. If you are investing heavily, it is worth asking a trademark firm to review the position.
What if I am already trading and the search finds a problem?
You still have options. The firm may look at who used their name first, whether the earlier mark is used, whether the businesses really overlap and whether an agreement is possible. A search now is better than a letter from the other side later.
Should I keep a copy of the search report?
Yes. Keep it with your brand records. It shows that you took care before choosing the name, which can help if a dispute arises, and it is useful background if you later expand or sell the business.
How long does an opposition take?
A case that settles early may be over within a few months. A contested case with evidence rounds and a hearing can take a year or more. Cooling off periods add time but can save costs if they lead to a settlement.
Can I get a quick decision?
The UK IPO has a fast track opposition procedure for straightforward cases based on a small number of earlier registered marks, where proof of use is provided at the start. It is quicker and cheaper but has limits, and it isn't suitable for every case.
Is an opposition the same as a court case?
No. An opposition is handled by the UK IPO tribunal, which is less formal and usually much cheaper than court. It decides whether a mark can be registered. Questions about infringement and damages are decided by the courts.
Should I oppose or wait and cancel later?
Opposing during the publication period is usually quicker and cheaper than cancelling a registered mark later. The burden of proof and the procedures are similar, but stopping a mark before it is registered also prevents the applicant from relying on the registration in the meantime.
How do I find out about new applications?
The Trade Marks Journal is published online and can be searched. Most businesses use a watch service through a trademark firm, which alerts them to applications for similar marks so they have time to act before the deadline.
Can I oppose if my brand isn't registered?
Possibly. You may be able to oppose based on unregistered rights if you can show goodwill in your brand and that use of the new mark would amount to passing off. The evidence needed is more demanding than for a registered mark.
What if the applicant is acting in bad faith?
Bad faith is a ground for opposition. It can apply, for example, where someone files a mark they know belongs to another business, such as a former distributor or partner. The firm will consider whether the facts support this ground, which needs careful evidence.
Is there a risk in opposing?
Yes. If the opposition fails, you may have to contribute to the applicant's costs. Opposing also tells the applicant you see them as a threat, which can lead them to look closely at your own registrations. The firm will help you weigh this before you file.
Can I oppose an international registration that covers the UK?
Yes. International registrations that designate the UK are published and can be opposed in much the same way as UK applications. The timetable is set by the publication date in the UK.
What if I was using my name first?
Earlier use can give you rights of your own and sometimes a defence, particularly if you have built up local or national goodwill before their mark was filed. The details matter, so evidence of your early use is very important.
Will I have to pay damages?
If you are found to infringe, you may have to pay damages or account for profits, as well as costs. A negotiated settlement often avoids this, particularly if you agree to stop or change your branding. The firm will explain your exposure.
Can I use my own surname as a brand?
There is a defence for honest use of your own name in some cases, but it is narrow and applies to individuals rather than company names in most situations. It doesn't permit use that suggests a connection with another business. The firm will look at the facts.
Can a marketplace remove my listings because of a complaint?
Yes. Platforms often remove listings when they receive a complaint from a trademark owner, sometimes without looking closely at the merits. You can usually file a counter notice if the complaint is wrong. The firm can help you respond and get listings restored where appropriate.
What is an undertaking?
An undertaking is a formal promise, for example to stop using a name or to destroy infringing stock. Letters often ask for signed undertakings. Breaking an undertaking given to a court can be serious, so get advice before signing anything.
How do I remove copycat listings from a marketplace?
Use the platform's reporting process, usually supported by your registration number. A registered trademark makes this much easier. The firm can file reports, respond to counter notices and help you decide whether further action is needed.
Someone has registered a domain with my brand. What can I do?
For .uk domains, Nominet's dispute resolution service can transfer or cancel abusive registrations. For many other endings, an international procedure applies. Both are usually quicker and cheaper than court. The firm will explain what you need to show.
Can I stop someone using my brand on social media?
Social media platforms have trademark reporting tools, and impersonation accounts can often be removed. A registered mark makes reports more effective. Persistent misuse may need a formal letter or further action.
What about counterfeit goods?
Counterfeits can involve criminal offences as well as civil claims. Owners can involve Trading Standards and ask customs to watch for counterfeits at the border. The firm can explain these options alongside civil action.

More questions people ask

What is a licensable activity?
Under the Licensing Act 2003 the main licensable activities are selling alcohol by retail, supplying alcohol in a members club, providing regulated entertainment and providing late night refreshment, which is hot food or hot drink between 11pm and 5am. Some entertainment has been deregulated in certain circumstances, so not every event needs a licence. A licensing solicitor can explain how the rules apply to what you plan to do.
Who is the licensing authority?
For premises and personal licences, the licensing authority is usually the local district, borough, city or unitary council where the premises is, or where the applicant lives for a personal licence. For taxis and private hire, it is the council that issues the licence, or Transport for London for London. Each authority publishes its own licensing policy, which it must take into account when making decisions.
What are responsible authorities?
These are the public bodies that receive copies of premises licence applications and can make representations. They usually include the police, the fire and rescue authority, environmental health, planning, trading standards, the body responsible for child protection, the public health team, the licensing authority itself and Home Office Immigration Enforcement. Any of them can also apply for a review.
What is a statement of licensing policy?
Every licensing authority must publish a statement of its licensing policy and review it regularly. The policy explains how the council approaches applications, what it expects from operators and any local issues, such as areas with special rules on cumulative impact. Your solicitor will look at the relevant policy when preparing an application or responding to an objection.
Is planning permission the same as a licence?
No. Planning permission and licensing are separate regimes. A premises can hold a licence that allows later hours than the planning permission, or the other way round. Both usually need to be in place, and breaching either can cause problems. A solicitor can explain how the two interact for your premises and whether planning advice is also needed.
Does licensing cover gambling?
Gambling premises are licensed under a separate law, the Gambling Act 2005, with councils and the Gambling Commission both involved. Some licensing firms also handle gambling work. If your matter involves gaming machines in a pub or a betting or bingo premises, mention it on the form so the firm knows what you need.
Do I need a licence to play background music?
Not always. Recorded music and some live music in licensed premises have been deregulated within certain hours and audience sizes, and background music is generally not regulated entertainment. Separate copyright licences for playing music are a different matter and are not covered by the Licensing Act. A licensing solicitor can check whether your plans need to be on your premises licence.
