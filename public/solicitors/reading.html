Choosing a solicitor in Reading

The right firm for you depends on the kind of matter you have, not just on being close by. A conveyancing firm on the high street in Reading may not handle a contested licence review, and a family law specialist will not usually deal with a speeding charge. That is why we ask what your matter is about before we introduce anyone.

It is worth asking any firm how it charges before work starts. Many matters are handled for a fixed fee, while others are charged by the hour. A good firm will explain the likely total, what could make it change and what is included, and confirm it in writing.

You can check that a solicitor is regulated on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and that a licensed conveyancer is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Every firm we introduce in Reading and Berkshire is regulated, and you are free to decide whether to instruct them.

For others it means a firm that works across England and Wales and is used to handling matters wherever the client lives.

It is natural to want a solicitor who is close by. For some kinds of matter that is useful. For others, the location of the firm makes very little difference to the service you receive.

When local knowledge can help

Some matters involve a particular court, council or local authority. A motoring offence is usually heard at a magistrates' court in the area where the offence happened. A premises licence is applied for to the local council where the premises is, and the council's licensing policy and the views of local police and residents can matter. A family dispute may be heard at the local family court. In these cases a firm that knows the local procedures and people can be an advantage, and attending a hearing is easier if the firm is not far away.

When location matters less

Conveyancing, lease extensions and business debt recovery are now mostly handled by phone, email, post and online portals. Property searches and Land Registry applications are done electronically, county court claims for money are often issued online, and documents can be signed and returned without visiting an office. Many people use a firm in a different town and never meet in person, and are perfectly happy with the service.

Meeting in person

If meeting face to face matters to you, say so when you make your enquiry. Some people find it easier to discuss personal matters, such as a separation, in person. Others are more comfortable with video calls. Many firms offer both, and you can ask what the firm usually does on the first call.

Getting the balance right

In the end, experience in your type of matter, clear communication and fair fees usually matter more than the distance to the office. A nearby firm that rarely handles your kind of matter may not be the best choice, while a firm a few towns away that does it every day may suit you well. The firm we introduce handles your type of work and covers your area.

Area by area

Conveyancing firms routinely act for buyers and sellers all over England and Wales. What matters more is that the firm is on your lender's panel and responds quickly. Local knowledge can help with things like unusual local searches or properties affected by mining or flood risk, but a good conveyancer will know how to deal with those wherever the property is.

Lease extension work is mostly paperwork and negotiation with the freeholder's solicitor, supported by a valuation from a surveyor. The surveyor usually needs to know the local property market, but the solicitor can be based anywhere. If the matter goes to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), hearings are often held remotely or at a regional venue.

Family solicitors often deal with the family court that covers the area where you or your children live. Many hearings now take place by video, but some are in person. Some people prefer a firm they can visit, especially during a difficult separation.

Motoring and licensing cases are the most local. Magistrates' courts, council licensing committees and local police all have their own ways of working. A firm that regularly appears before them can be a real help, and it may be able to attend a hearing at short notice.

Business debt recovery is usually handled through letters, negotiation and, if needed, the county court. Many money claims are issued and managed online, so the firm's location matters less than its experience with commercial debts.

Wales

England and Wales share one legal system, so firms in England can usually act for clients in Wales and the other way round. Some differences do apply in Wales, such as Land Transaction Tax instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax on property purchases, and some housing and planning rules. If you would like to deal with a firm in Welsh, mention it in the notes and the firm will tell you whether it can help.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, so we do not cover matters there for conveyancing, family law, motoring offences, licensing, lease extensions or debt recovery. Immigration introductions are available across the whole of the UK.

Whether a firm is round the corner or at the other end of the country, it is worth checking it before you instruct it. This is easy to do and takes a few minutes.

Check the register

Solicitors and law firms in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can search for them on the SRA register. Licensed conveyancers are regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers, which has its own register. Regulated immigration advisers can be checked with their regulator, such as the SRA or the Immigration Advice Authority.

Look for the regulatory information

Regulated law firms must show certain information on their websites, including who regulates them and their registration number. Many firms display the SRA's digital badge, which links to the firm's entry on the register. If a firm's website does not say who regulates it, ask.

Check the people, not just the firm

Ask who will handle your matter day to day. That might be a solicitor, a chartered legal executive, a licensed conveyancer or a paralegal working under supervision. All of these can be appropriate. It is reasonable to ask about their experience and who supervises them.

Read the paperwork

Before any work starts, the firm should send a client care letter and terms of business. These set out the fees, who will do the work, how complaints are handled and, if you were introduced by Leaflegal, the fee the firm pays for the introduction. Read them carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

Every firm we introduce is regulated

Leaflegal only introduces firms that are regulated for the work they do. Checking for yourself is still a good habit, and a good firm will be happy for you to do so.

Warning signs

Be cautious if anyone offering legal help will not say who regulates them, asks you to pay large sums before explaining their fees in writing, promises a particular result or pressures you to decide on the spot. Be especially careful with emails asking you to send money for a property purchase to new bank details. Always check bank details by phone using a number you already know before sending money to a firm.

Does a local firm cost more or less

Legal fees vary between firms, and location is only one factor. Firms in some large cities may have higher overheads, but that does not always mean higher prices for a particular piece of work. Fixed fee work, such as many house purchases, can be priced similarly across the country. The best way to understand the cost is to ask the firm for a written estimate or quote.

Travel and attendance

Where a solicitor needs to attend a court or hearing, the cost of travel and waiting time may be included in a fixed fee or charged separately. A local firm may charge less for travel to a local court. Ask how attendance is charged so you can compare like for like.

Disbursements

On top of the firm's own fees there may be disbursements, such as court fees, search fees, Land Registry fees, licensing application fees set by the council, or a barrister's fee. Official fees change from time to time, and GOV.UK lists many of them. The firm will tell you which apply.

Comparing quotes

If you speak to more than one firm, check that each quote covers the same work and includes the same items. A lower headline fee may leave out things that another firm includes. Ask each firm to confirm what is and is not included.

Can I buy a property at auction to let?
Yes, but auction purchases move quickly. When the hammer falls, contracts are usually exchanged and completion often follows within a short fixed period. That means the legal checks need to happen before the auction. A conveyancer can review the legal pack for you in advance, and you will need your finance ready in time.
Can I buy a house in multiple occupation?
Yes, but these properties carry extra rules. A large house in multiple occupation needs a mandatory licence, and smaller ones may need one under local schemes. Planning permission can also be needed. Many lenders have specific products for these properties. Your conveyancer will raise enquiries about licensing and planning, and it is wise to check with the council too.
Does the conveyancer check whether the property is a good investment?
No. Your conveyancer checks the legal title and raises questions about anything that might affect your ownership or your plans. Whether the rent, price and location make sense as an investment is a commercial decision for you. Many landlords speak to letting agents, mortgage brokers and accountants alongside their conveyancer.
How long does a buy to let purchase take?
There is no fixed timescale. A simple purchase of a vacant freehold house with no chain can move quickly, while a leasehold flat with a tenant in place, a company buyer and a lender with strict conditions will usually take longer. Searches, the speed of the seller's side and the managing agent's response all play a part. Your conveyancer can give a better idea once they have seen the paperwork.
Can I buy through a limited company?
Yes. Many landlords now buy through a company set up to hold property. The company is the legal owner. Your conveyancer will need details of the company, its directors and its owners, and evidence that it can buy and borrow. Whether this route suits you is mainly a tax question for an accountant.
Will the lender want a personal guarantee?
Most lenders who lend to property companies want the directors to give personal guarantees. This means they can be personally liable for the company's mortgage debt. Lenders often require guarantors to have independent legal advice before signing, and this may have to come from a separate solicitor. Your conveyancer will explain the lender's requirements.
Does a company pay Stamp Duty?
Yes. A company buying residential property usually pays the higher rates of Stamp Duty Land Tax that apply to additional properties, and different rules can apply to higher value homes. Rates and reliefs change, so check GOV.UK or ask your conveyancer what applies to your purchase.
What is a consent order?
A consent order is a court order that records a financial agreement between civil partners. It is drafted by a solicitor, signed by both of you and approved by a judge after the conditional order. Once approved, it is legally binding and can be enforced. It can also achieve a clean break so no further claims can be made.
Do we need a financial order if we have very little?
Many solicitors recommend one even where assets are modest. Without a clean break order, a former civil partner may be able to make a claim in future, for example if one of you later receives an inheritance or builds up savings. A consent order can be relatively straightforward where finances are simple.
Can I lose my right to a financial claim?
Yes, in some circumstances. If you form a new civil partnership or marry before making a financial application, you may lose the right to make certain claims. There are also practical risks in delaying, as evidence becomes harder to gather. Your solicitor can explain how to protect your position. Acting early usually helps.
What happens to pensions?
Pensions can be shared through a pension sharing order, which transfers part of one person's pension to the other. They can also be offset against other assets. Pension values can be hard to compare, and specialist reports are sometimes needed. Your solicitor can explain the options and what information to gather.
What if my civil partner hides money?
Each person has a duty to give full and frank financial disclosure. If one person hides assets, the court can draw conclusions, make orders for further information and, in some cases, set aside an agreement made on false information. If you have concerns, tell your solicitor early so they can explain the steps available.
Can I stay in our home while things are sorted out?
A civil partner who does not legally own the home usually has home rights, which means a right to occupy it while the civil partnership continues. These rights can be protected by registering them at HM Land Registry. If there are safety concerns, the court can make orders about who lives in the home. A solicitor can explain your position.
How are arrangements for children decided?
Most parents agree arrangements themselves or with the help of mediation. If agreement is not possible, a parent or someone with parental responsibility can apply to court for a child arrangements order, usually after a mediation information meeting. The court's main concern is the child's welfare, and it will look at the child's needs and wishes.
Do I have to receive a warning before being prosecuted?
For dangerous driving, the police usually have to warn the driver that a prosecution is being considered, either at the scene or by a notice of intended prosecution sent within 14 days. There is an exception where an accident happened that the driver was aware of. Whether the rules were followed depends on the facts, and a solicitor can check this when reviewing the case.
Should I have a solicitor at my police interview?
Leaflegal cannot advise you on that. What we can say is that everyone interviewed under caution has a right to free legal advice, whatever their income. A solicitor can find out what the police allege before the interview and talk through how to approach it. The firm we introduce you to can explain this in more detail.
What does released under investigation mean?
It means the police have released you without charge and without bail conditions while they continue their enquiries. There is no fixed date for a decision. You may later be charged, interviewed again or told no further action will be taken. Your solicitor can contact the officer in the case for updates.
Will my case go to the Crown Court?
Not necessarily. Dangerous driving can be heard in either court. The magistrates decide whether the case is suitable for them, and you can choose a jury trial in the Crown Court if you plead not guilty. Magistrates can also send a case to the Crown Court for sentence. A solicitor will explain the pros and cons of each court for your case.
What happens at the first hearing?
The first hearing takes place at the magistrates' court. You confirm your details and indicate whether you intend to plead guilty or not guilty. The court then decides where the case should be heard. If the case stays in the magistrates' court and you plead guilty, sentence may happen that day or later after a report.
Will I need a barrister?
Crown Court cases usually involve a barrister, who represents you at hearings and trial, working with your solicitor. Some solicitors have higher rights of audience and can appear in the Crown Court themselves. In the magistrates' court, a solicitor normally represents you. Your firm will explain who will be doing what.
How long will the case take?
It depends on the length of the police investigation, whether you plead guilty, which court hears the case and how busy that court is. A guilty plea in the magistrates' court may be dealt with fairly quickly. Crown Court trials can take many months to come to court. Your solicitor can give you an idea once the case is underway.
How long does a review take?
There is a 28 day period for representations after the application is advertised. The hearing is then normally held within 20 working days of the end of that period. Summary reviews brought by the police are much quicker, with interim steps considered within 48 hours and a full hearing within 28 days.
Can I keep trading during a review?
Usually yes. On an ordinary review, the licence continues as normal until the decision and, in most cases, until any appeal period has passed. In a summary review, the council can impose interim steps, such as suspension, that take effect immediately. A solicitor can explain the position in your case.
What documents will I receive?
You should receive a copy of the review application from the applicant. Before the hearing, the council usually sends a report with the application, the representations and any supporting evidence. If you think documents are missing, your solicitor can ask the council or the applicant for them.
Who sits on the licensing sub-committee?
A sub-committee is usually made up of three councillors from the council's licensing committee, supported by a licensing officer and a legal adviser. The councillors make the decision. They are expected to keep an open mind and decide on the evidence and the licensing objectives.
Can new evidence be produced at the hearing?
Documentary evidence usually needs to be provided before the hearing day. New material on the day can be allowed only if all parties agree. This is one reason why careful preparation in advance is so important. Your solicitor will plan when and how to send in your evidence.
Do I have to attend the hearing myself?
You do not have to, but it often helps if the licence holder, the DPS or the person running the premises attends. The committee may want to ask them how the premises will be run in future. If you have a solicitor, they will usually speak for you and prepare you for any questions.
Can I agree an outcome with the police before the hearing?
Often yes. Many reviews are narrowed or settled by agreeing conditions, management changes or a reduction in hours with the police and other authorities. The agreed proposals are then put to the committee, which still makes the final decision but usually gives weight to agreement. Your solicitor can lead these discussions.
What is a letter before action?
It is a formal letter sent to a debtor before a court claim is issued. It sets out what is owed and why, gives a deadline for payment or a response and explains that court proceedings will follow if the matter is not resolved. It is often the first formal step in recovering a business debt and many debts are paid at this stage.
Is a letter before action the same as a letter of claim?
In debt recovery the two terms are often used to mean the same thing. Both describe the formal letter sent before court proceedings. Some people also call it a letter before claim or a pre-action letter. What matters is that the letter contains the right information and gives the debtor a fair chance to respond.
Do I have to send one before going to court?
Courts expect parties to exchange information and try to resolve matters before issuing a claim. There is no absolute bar on issuing without a letter, but doing so can lead to costs penalties or the claim being paused. In almost all debt cases, a solicitor will send a letter before action first.
Can I write the letter myself?
Businesses can send their own letters, and many send a final demand before involving a solicitor. A solicitor's letter often carries more weight and is more likely to meet the court's expectations, which matters if the case goes further. If the debtor is a sole trader, the letter must follow a specific protocol with set enclosures.
Is a letter from a solicitor more likely to get paid?
Often it is, though nothing is guaranteed. A letter on a law firm's letterhead shows that the creditor is prepared to take legal action and has taken advice. Customers who have ignored ordinary reminders frequently respond to a solicitor's letter by paying, proposing a plan or explaining a dispute that can then be dealt with.
Does sending a letter commit me to going to court?
No. A letter sets out what you intend to do if the debt is not paid, but you can still decide not to issue a claim. It is sensible not to threaten steps you have no intention of taking, so your solicitor will discuss your plans with you before the letter is sent.
Will a letter damage my relationship with the customer?
It can feel like a big step, especially with a customer you value. Many solicitors write in a firm but professional tone that leaves room to keep trading once the debt is paid. You can talk to your solicitor about the tone you want, and about whether a phone call from you first might help.
What happens when a lease runs out completely?
In some circumstances the law allows the occupier of a flat to stay on as a tenant when a long lease ends, but usually paying a market rent and without owning a lease they can sell. The flat's capital value effectively goes back to the freeholder. This is rare, because most owners extend well before it happens.
I inherited a flat with a short lease. What are my options?
Personal representatives can often start a statutory lease extension on behalf of the estate within a set period after the grant of probate or letters of administration. They can also sell the flat as it is, or sell with the benefit of a notice. A solicitor can explain the options and the time limits for the estate.
Does a short lease affect my service charge?
No, the service charge is set by the terms of the lease and the costs of running the building, not by how many years are left. However, if you extend informally, the new lease could change some terms. Under the statutory route, only limited changes to the existing terms are allowed.
Does the ground rent make a short lease worse?
It can. Under the long standing valuation rules, part of the premium compensates the freeholder for losing its ground rent, so a higher or rising ground rent usually means a higher premium. Some short leases also have ground rent terms that worry lenders. A surveyor can explain how your ground rent affects the cost of extending.
Can I extend a lease with very few years left?
Usually, yes. The statutory right to extend does not depend on how many years remain, as long as the lease was originally granted for more than 21 years and you qualify. However, the shorter the lease, the higher the premium tends to be under the long standing rules. A surveyor can give you an idea of the likely cost.
Should I extend now or wait for the reforms?
That depends on your circumstances and is a decision for you. Waiting may mean the lease falls further and becomes more expensive under the current rules, and it may not fit plans to sell or remortgage. The timing and detail of the reforms can also change. A solicitor can explain the factors involved so you can make an informed choice.
My freeholder has offered an extension. Should I accept?
Leaflegal cannot tell you that. A solicitor can review the offer, compare it with the statutory route and point out any terms that might cause problems later, such as a ground rent that rises sharply. A surveyor can also tell you how the price compares. You can then decide whether to accept, negotiate or take another route.
A competitor has copied my product. What can I do?
Gather evidence of your design and the copy, check which rights you have and get advice. Options include platform takedowns, a carefully worded letter and a court claim. A registered design makes action much easier, because copying doesn't need to be proved.
Do I have to prove copying?
For a registered design, no. If the other design gives the same overall impression, it can infringe even if it was created independently. For unregistered rights, you do need to show copying, which can be harder.
Is copying a registered design a crime?
Intentionally copying a registered design in the course of business, so as to make a product exactly like it or with features that differ only immaterially, can be a criminal offence in the UK. Civil remedies are also available.
I've been accused of copying a design. What should I do?
Don't ignore the letter and don't reply in anger. Gather evidence of how and when you created your design. The firm will compare the designs, check the other side's rights and help you respond.
Can I challenge a registered design?
Yes. A registered design can be declared invalid, for example if it was not new or lacked individual character when filed. A successful challenge removes the registration. Evidence of earlier designs is key.
What is a must fit or must match exclusion?
UK unregistered design right doesn't protect features that must fit another product to work, or that must match the appearance of another article of which the product is part, such as some spare parts. The details are technical, and the firm will advise.
Can I stop copies being imported?
Customs can be asked to watch for goods that infringe registered designs and other IP rights entering the UK. An application to customs needs details of your rights and how to identify copies. The firm can help prepare it.
What is the EU Settlement Scheme?
It is the scheme set up after Brexit to protect the rights of many EU, EEA and Swiss citizens and their family members who had made their lives in the UK. People granted status under it receive either settled status or pre-settled status, which is held digitally. A regulated adviser can explain how it relates to you.
What is the difference between settled and pre-settled status?
Settled status is permanent permission to stay. Pre-settled status is permission for a limited period. What your status means for you, and what may happen next, depends on your situation. A regulated adviser can look at your status and explain it in plain terms.
Can Leaflegal tell me whether I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We cannot tell you whether you can apply or what your status means. We introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at your situation and explain your options.
Has the scheme changed since it opened?
Yes. The Home Office has changed how the scheme works several times, including how it handles some types of status and applications. Information from a few years ago, or from friends, may be out of date. A regulated adviser works with the current position and can explain it.
Do I get a card or certificate?
Status under the scheme is usually digital. You can view it online and prove it using a share code. Some people still have older physical documents, but these do not usually replace the digital record. Your adviser can explain how this works for you.
Is the scheme the same as a visa?
The scheme is separate from the visa routes in the main Immigration Rules and has its own rules. People sometimes confuse the two. A regulated adviser can explain how the scheme relates to other parts of the immigration system in your situation.
Do Irish citizens need to think about the scheme?
Irish citizens have a separate position in the UK because of long standing arrangements between the UK and Ireland. Their family members may have different questions. A regulated adviser can explain how the scheme does or does not relate to you and your family.
