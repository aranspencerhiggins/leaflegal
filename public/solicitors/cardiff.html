Choosing a solicitor in Cardiff

The right firm for you depends on the kind of matter you have, not just on being close by. A conveyancing firm on the high street in Cardiff may not handle a contested licence review, and a family law specialist will not usually deal with a speeding charge. That is why we ask what your matter is about before we introduce anyone.

It is worth asking any firm how it charges before work starts. Many matters are handled for a fixed fee, while others are charged by the hour. A good firm will explain the likely total, what could make it change and what is included, and confirm it in writing.

You can check that a solicitor is regulated on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and that a licensed conveyancer is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. Every firm we introduce in Cardiff and South Wales is regulated, and you are free to decide whether to instruct them.

It is natural to want a solicitor who is close by. For some kinds of matter that is useful. For others, the location of the firm makes very little difference to the service you receive.

When local knowledge can help

Some matters involve a particular court, council or local authority. A motoring offence is usually heard at a magistrates' court in the area where the offence happened. A premises licence is applied for to the local council where the premises is, and the council's licensing policy and the views of local police and residents can matter. A family dispute may be heard at the local family court. In these cases a firm that knows the local procedures and people can be an advantage, and attending a hearing is easier if the firm is not far away.

When location matters less

Conveyancing, lease extensions and business debt recovery are now mostly handled by phone, email, post and online portals. Property searches and Land Registry applications are done electronically, county court claims for money are often issued online, and documents can be signed and returned without visiting an office. Many people use a firm in a different town and never meet in person, and are perfectly happy with the service.

Meeting in person

If meeting face to face matters to you, say so when you make your enquiry. Some people find it easier to discuss personal matters, such as a separation, in person. Others are more comfortable with video calls. Many firms offer both, and you can ask what the firm usually does on the first call.

Getting the balance right

In the end, experience in your type of matter, clear communication and fair fees usually matter more than the distance to the office. A nearby firm that rarely handles your kind of matter may not be the best choice, while a firm a few towns away that does it every day may suit you well. The firm we introduce handles your type of work and covers your area.

Area by area

Conveyancing firms routinely act for buyers and sellers all over England and Wales. What matters more is that the firm is on your lender's panel and responds quickly. Local knowledge can help with things like unusual local searches or properties affected by mining or flood risk, but a good conveyancer will know how to deal with those wherever the property is.

Lease extension work is mostly paperwork and negotiation with the freeholder's solicitor, supported by a valuation from a surveyor. The surveyor usually needs to know the local property market, but the solicitor can be based anywhere. If the matter goes to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), hearings are often held remotely or at a regional venue.

Family solicitors often deal with the family court that covers the area where you or your children live. Many hearings now take place by video, but some are in person. Some people prefer a firm they can visit, especially during a difficult separation.

Motoring and licensing cases are the most local. Magistrates' courts, council licensing committees and local police all have their own ways of working. A firm that regularly appears before them can be a real help, and it may be able to attend a hearing at short notice.

Business debt recovery is usually handled through letters, negotiation and, if needed, the county court. Many money claims are issued and managed online, so the firm's location matters less than its experience with commercial debts.

Wales

England and Wales share one legal system, so firms in England can usually act for clients in Wales and the other way round. Some differences do apply in Wales, such as Land Transaction Tax instead of Stamp Duty Land Tax on property purchases, and some housing and planning rules. If you would like to deal with a firm in Welsh, mention it in the notes and the firm will tell you whether it can help.

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, so we do not cover matters there for conveyancing, family law, motoring offences, licensing, lease extensions or debt recovery. Immigration introductions are available across the whole of the UK.

Whether a firm is round the corner or at the other end of the country, it is worth checking it before you instruct it. This is easy to do and takes a few minutes.

Check the register

Solicitors and law firms in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can search for them on the SRA register. Licensed conveyancers are regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers, which has its own register. Regulated immigration advisers can be checked with their regulator, such as the SRA or the Immigration Advice Authority.

Look for the regulatory information

Regulated law firms must show certain information on their websites, including who regulates them and their registration number. Many firms display the SRA's digital badge, which links to the firm's entry on the register. If a firm's website does not say who regulates it, ask.

Check the people, not just the firm

Ask who will handle your matter day to day. That might be a solicitor, a chartered legal executive, a licensed conveyancer or a paralegal working under supervision. All of these can be appropriate. It is reasonable to ask about their experience and who supervises them.

Read the paperwork

Before any work starts, the firm should send a client care letter and terms of business. These set out the fees, who will do the work, how complaints are handled and, if you were introduced by Leaflegal, the fee the firm pays for the introduction. Read them carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

Warning signs

Be cautious if anyone offering legal help will not say who regulates them, asks you to pay large sums before explaining their fees in writing, promises a particular result or pressures you to decide on the spot. Be especially careful with emails asking you to send money for a property purchase to new bank details. Always check bank details by phone using a number you already know before sending money to a firm.

Does a local firm cost more or less

Legal fees vary between firms, and location is only one factor. Firms in some large cities may have higher overheads, but that does not always mean higher prices for a particular piece of work. Fixed fee work, such as many house purchases, can be priced similarly across the country. The best way to understand the cost is to ask the firm for a written estimate or quote.

Travel and attendance

Where a solicitor needs to attend a court or hearing, the cost of travel and waiting time may be included in a fixed fee or charged separately. A local firm may charge less for travel to a local court. Ask how attendance is charged so you can compare like for like.

Disbursements

On top of the firm's own fees there may be disbursements, such as court fees, search fees, Land Registry fees, licensing application fees set by the council, or a barrister's fee. Official fees change from time to time, and GOV.UK lists many of them. The firm will tell you which apply.

Comparing quotes

If you speak to more than one firm, check that each quote covers the same work and includes the same items. A lower headline fee may leave out things that another firm includes. Ask each firm to confirm what is and is not included.

When should I instruct a conveyancer to sell?
Ideally when you put your home on the market. The firm can carry out identity checks, obtain your title, send you the forms and request any leasehold information straight away. That way the contract pack is ready to send on the day you accept an offer, which can make your property more attractive to buyers.
What does my conveyancer need from me at the start?
Your conveyancer will need photo ID, proof of address, your mortgage lender and account details, and the details of your estate agent. They will also ask you to complete the property information form and the fittings and contents form, and to send any certificates, guarantees and planning documents you have.
Do I need my original deeds?
For most registered properties, the official record is held electronically by HM Land Registry, so original paper deeds are not essential. Any old deeds you have can still be useful, especially if they explain rights or boundaries. If your property is unregistered, the deeds are vital and your conveyancer will need to see them.
Do I need an Energy Performance Certificate?
Homes marketed for sale in England and Wales generally need a valid Energy Performance Certificate, and the agent will usually arrange this before marketing begins. If you already have one that is still valid, you can usually use it. Your estate agent can confirm what is needed.
Can I sell if my name has changed since I bought?
Yes. If you have changed your name through marriage, civil partnership or deed poll, your conveyancer will usually ask for evidence, such as a marriage certificate, and may update the register or explain the change to the buyer. It is a common issue and rarely causes a problem if dealt with early.
What if I own the property with someone else?
All registered owners usually need to agree to the sale and sign the contract and transfer. If one owner has died, your conveyancer will need to see the death certificate and may need probate depending on how the property was owned. If owners disagree about selling, it is a different situation and legal advice is needed.
Can I use the same firm as my buyer?
Professional rules usually prevent one firm acting for both buyer and seller in a residential sale, because their interests conflict. There are limited exceptions, but in practice most transactions have a separate firm on each side. Your conveyancer will tell you if a conflict arises.
What is a consent order?
It is a court order that sets out a financial agreement between divorcing spouses or separating civil partners. Both people agree the terms and a judge approves them. Once approved it is legally binding and can be enforced like any other court order. It is the usual way couples make a financial agreement final, whether they reached it themselves, in mediation or through solicitors.
When can a consent order be made?
The court can only approve a consent order once the conditional order in the divorce or dissolution has been made. Couples often start preparing the order earlier so that it is ready to submit at that point. Your solicitor will explain the best timing for you. Pension sharing terms only take effect after the final order.
Do we need a consent order if we have hardly any assets?
It is not compulsory, but many couples with modest finances still choose one with clean break terms. Without it, either person could make a claim in the future if their circumstances change, for example after an inheritance or a lottery win. A solicitor can explain whether it is worth considering.
Can we get a consent order years after our divorce?
Yes. A consent order can be made at any time after the conditional order, even many years after the final order, as long as neither of you has remarried and the claims have not already been dealt with. A solicitor can check whether this is possible in your case. Delay can also make things harder.
Is a consent order the same as a separation agreement?
No. A separation agreement is a contract between a couple who have separated, often before divorce. It is not automatically binding in the same way. A consent order is a court order made within divorce proceedings. A separation agreement is sometimes later turned into a consent order. Your solicitor can explain which suits your situation.
Can unmarried couples get a consent order?
Financial consent orders of this kind are for married couples and civil partners in divorce or dissolution proceedings. Unmarried couples who separate deal with property in other ways, such as a declaration of trust or a separation agreement. A solicitor can explain the options. Orders about children can be made whether or not the parents were married.
Do we have to go to court?
Usually not. Consent orders are normally dealt with on paper by a judge, without a hearing. A short hearing is only listed if the judge has questions that cannot be answered in writing. This keeps costs down and means most couples never see the inside of a courtroom. The judge reads the order and the statement of information, and approves it if satisfied it is broadly fair.
What is drug driving?
Drug driving usually means driving, attempting to drive or being in charge of a vehicle with a specified drug in your blood above its legal limit. There is also an older offence of driving while unfit through drugs, which depends on showing impairment rather than a set level. Both are criminal offences dealt with in the magistrates' court, and both usually lead to a ban.
Which drugs are covered?
The specified limit offence covers a list of drugs set in regulations. It includes illegal drugs, such as cannabis, cocaine, ketamine and MDMA, with very low limits, and some medicines, such as diazepam, morphine and methadone, with higher limits. Amphetamine has a separate limit. The impairment offence can apply to any drug that affects your ability to drive.
Do the police have to prove my driving was affected?
Not for the specified limit offence. If the level of a specified drug in your blood is over the limit, the offence is made out even if your driving seemed normal. For the separate impairment offence, the prosecution does need to show that your ability to drive properly was impaired. Your solicitor will explain which offence you face.
Can I be charged if I was not driving?
Yes. Being in charge of a vehicle with a specified drug above the limit is an offence, even if the vehicle was parked. The penalties for being in charge are different from those for driving, and there can be a defence if there was no likelihood of you driving while over the limit. A solicitor can look at the facts.
Is drug driving treated the same as drink driving?
In many ways, yes. Both carry an obligatory minimum ban of 12 months and stay on your record for eleven years. The differences are mainly in the evidence and sentencing. Drug driving depends on blood analysis, the sentencing guideline is not linked to a simple table of levels, and the rehabilitation course that can shorten a drink driving ban is not usually available.
Is drug driving a criminal offence?
Yes. It is a criminal offence and a conviction appears on your criminal record as well as your driving record. It can affect insurance, some jobs and travel to certain countries. Separately from the criminal case, if you are found to have been in possession of an illegal drug, that could be dealt with as a different offence.
How long do drugs stay in the blood?
It varies a great deal between drugs and between people, depending on things like the dose, how often the drug is used, body weight and metabolism. Some substances can remain in the blood for longer than people expect, even after they no longer feel any effect. There is no reliable rule of thumb, which is why many cases need careful scientific review.
What qualification do I need?
You need an accredited licensing qualification, usually a short course covering licensing law and the role of a personal licence holder, with an exam at the end. Approved training providers run these courses. Some other qualifications are also accepted. Check with the training provider that the course is accredited for personal licence applications.
How long does a personal licence take?
If there is nothing to declare and the application is complete, the council will usually process it within a few weeks, although times vary between councils. If you declare a relevant conviction, the police have time to consider objecting, and a hearing may follow, which adds more time.
Does a personal licence expire?
No. Personal licences no longer need renewing and last indefinitely unless they are surrendered, forfeited, suspended or revoked. If you have an older licence with an expiry date printed on it, that date no longer applies. Your licence remains valid.
Can I use my licence anywhere?
Yes. A personal licence granted by any council in England and Wales can be used at licensed premises anywhere in England and Wales. It is not tied to a particular premises or employer, so you keep it if you change jobs.
Do I need the right to work in the UK?
Yes. Applicants must have the right to work in the UK, and the council will check this. A personal licence can lapse if the holder's right to work ends. If your immigration status is time limited, the licence may be linked to that. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Can I get a personal licence with a criminal record?
Often yes. Many people with past convictions hold personal licences. Spent convictions are generally not relevant. If you have an unspent relevant conviction, the police can object and the council may hold a hearing. The outcome depends on the offence, how long ago it was and your circumstances since.
What is a relevant offence?
Relevant offences are listed in the Licensing Act 2003. They include certain offences involving alcohol, drink driving, violence, dishonesty, drugs, some sexual offences, certain immigration offences and offences under licensing law. Equivalent offences abroad can also count. A solicitor can check whether a particular conviction is relevant.
Can I use a statutory demand for a claim for damages?
Usually not. A statutory demand must be for a liquidated sum, meaning a fixed amount that is due, such as an unpaid invoice. A claim for damages that still needs to be valued by the court is not suitable. Your solicitor will check whether your debt fits.
Is a statutory demand the same as a letter before action?
No. A letter before action is a step before an ordinary court claim and gives the debtor a chance to respond. A statutory demand is an insolvency tool that can lead to winding up or bankruptcy. Some creditors send a letter before action first, and then consider a statutory demand if the debt is undisputed and remains unpaid.
Can a statutory demand be used on a debt owed by a sole trader?
Yes. A sole trader is an individual, so a statutory demand served on them can lead to a bankruptcy petition if the debt meets the bankruptcy threshold and is not paid. Different rules on service and challenges apply to individuals. Your solicitor will explain how this works and whether it is proportionate.
How is a statutory demand served on a company?
It is usually served by leaving it at the company's registered office, as shown on the public register. Your solicitor will check the address, arrange delivery, often through a process server, and keep evidence of when and how it was served. That evidence is needed if a petition follows.
How is a statutory demand served on a sole trader?
For an individual, the demand should usually be handed to them personally where possible. If that cannot be done after reasonable attempts, the rules allow other methods, but the creditor must be able to show the court what efforts were made. Your solicitor will arrange this carefully.
Can I email a statutory demand?
Email alone is not usually enough. The rules on service are strict, and a petition based on a demand that was not properly served can fail. Your solicitor will use a method of service that the court accepts and will keep a clear record of it.
What must the demand say?
It must follow a prescribed form and include details of the creditor and debtor, the amount owed and how it arose, any interest or charges and how they were calculated, and information about how the debtor can respond. Your solicitor will prepare the demand so that it meets these requirements.
What is a deed of variation?
It is a legal document that changes the terms of an existing lease. It is signed by the leaseholder and the freeholder, and sometimes by other parties such as a management company. Once completed, it is registered at HM Land Registry so that anyone looking at the title can see the lease and the changes made to it.
Why would my lease need changing?
Common reasons include a wrong or missing plan, unclear repair or insurance clauses, service charge shares that do not add up, missing rights to use shared parts and onerous ground rent terms. Often the problem only comes to light when a buyer's solicitor or a lender reviews the lease. A solicitor will read your lease and explain whether a variation would help.
Can a lease be changed after it has been signed?
Yes. A lease is a contract, and the parties can agree to change it at any time. In some cases a tribunal or court can also order a change. The change must be properly documented and registered to be effective against future buyers and lenders.
Who needs to agree to a variation?
Usually the leaseholder and the freeholder, plus any management company that is a party to the lease. Mortgage lenders on both sides may also need to consent. If the change affects other flats, such as service charge shares, other leaseholders may need to be involved.
Is a lease variation the same as a lease extension?
No. A variation changes the terms of the existing lease, while an extension gives a longer lease. They can be combined, as a new lease on an extension can sometimes fix defects at the same time. Your solicitor can explain whether doing both together would make sense for you.
Can Leaflegal tell me if my lease is defective?
No. Leaflegal does not give legal advice or review leases. We introduce you to a regulated firm, and the solicitor there can read your lease and explain any problems.
Is a licence different from a variation?
Yes. A licence is a one off permission from the freeholder, for example to carry out particular alterations, and it leaves the lease terms unchanged. A variation permanently changes what the lease says. If you only need permission for something specific, a licence may be simpler. Your solicitor will explain which is appropriate.
What is the difference between naturalisation and registration?
They are two different ways of applying to become a British citizen. Naturalisation is the term usually used for adults. Registration is used for children and some other people in particular situations. Which, if either, is relevant to you is a question for a regulated adviser who can look at your circumstances.
Is citizenship the same as indefinite leave to remain?
No. Indefinite leave to remain is an immigration status that lets a person stay in the UK without a time limit. Citizenship is a nationality. They are separate, with separate applications. A regulated adviser can explain what each means and how they relate to each other in your situation.
Could I already be British without knowing it?
Sometimes people are British without realising, usually because of a parent's or grandparent's history. Nationality law is complicated and has changed many times. A regulated adviser who works with nationality law can look at your family history and explain whether this might be worth exploring.
Will I have to give up my other nationality?
The UK side is something your adviser can explain. Whether you can keep your existing nationality also depends on the law of the other country, which varies. Many people check the position with the other country's embassy or authorities before they apply.
Can Leaflegal tell me whether I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration or nationality advice and does not do so. We introduce you to a regulated adviser, who can look at your situation and explain your options.
What rights does citizenship give?
British citizens can live in the UK without immigration permission, hold a British passport and take part fully in UK democratic life. The full picture depends on your circumstances, including any other nationality you hold. A regulated adviser can explain what becoming British would mean for you.
Is applying for citizenship compulsory?
No. Applying is a personal choice, and many people with long term status in the UK decide not to apply, or to wait. Some think about cost, some about their other nationality and some about family plans. A regulated adviser can explain the practical differences so that you can make an informed decision.
