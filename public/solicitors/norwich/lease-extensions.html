Lease extensions explained

General information for people and businesses in Norwich. Pick a topic to read more.

If you own a flat in England or Wales, you almost certainly own it on a lease. A lease is a long agreement that gives you the right to live in and sell your home for a fixed number of years. The building itself, and the land it sits on, is usually owned by someone else, called the freeholder or landlord. When the lease runs out, the flat goes back to the freeholder.

That is why the number of years left on a lease matters so much. A lease that started at 99 years might feel endless when it is new, but after a few decades it becomes a real issue. Buyers, mortgage lenders and surveyors all look closely at the remaining term, and a shorter lease usually means a lower value and a more expensive extension.

Lease extension and enfranchisement law gives many leaseholders legal rights to deal with this. Flat owners can often extend their own lease, and groups of flat owners can sometimes buy the freehold of their building together. Some leaseholders also need help changing terms in a lease that is badly drafted, out of date or putting buyers off.

Freeholds are owned by all sorts of people and organisations. Some belong to individuals or small family companies, some to large investment companies that own thousands of freeholds, and some to councils, housing associations or the flat owners themselves. Day to day management is often handled by a managing agent. Who owns your freehold can affect how quickly things move and how negotiations go, which is something a solicitor will look at early on.

This is a specialist area. The rules are detailed, the deadlines are strict and the valuation side needs a surveyor who knows leasehold work. A notice served on the wrong person or with the wrong information can cost time and money. That is why most people use a solicitor who handles this kind of work regularly.

The law is changing

The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 makes major changes to leasehold law, but it is being brought into force in stages. Some parts already apply, while others need further regulations before they take effect. This means the right process, the length of the new lease and the way the price is worked out can depend on when you start. A solicitor will confirm which rules apply to you at the time you instruct them.

There are several ways to deal with a lease that is getting shorter or a lease that is causing problems. Which one suits you depends on your building, your freeholder, your plans and how the law applies at the time. These are the main routes a solicitor is likely to talk through with you.

A statutory lease extension

Many flat owners have a legal right to a new lease under the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993. Under the long standing rules, this adds 90 years to the remaining term and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn, which in practice means nothing. The freeholder cannot refuse if you qualify, and disputes about the price can go to a tribunal. The 2024 Act will change the length of a statutory extension and the way the premium is calculated, and these changes are being brought in over time. Find out more on our statutory lease extension page.

An informal lease extension

Some freeholders are willing to agree an extension outside the statutory process. This can be quicker and sometimes cheaper in fees, but the terms are whatever the two sides agree. Informal offers sometimes include a ground rent or other terms that a buyer or lender later dislikes, so it is common to have a solicitor review any offer before accepting it.

Buying the freehold together

Where enough flat owners in a building want to act together, they may have a right to buy the freehold. This is called collective enfranchisement. Once they own the freehold, they can grant themselves long leases and control how the building is run.

Changing the lease

Sometimes the problem is not the length of the lease but what it says. A lease might have a missing clause, a wrong plan, unclear repair duties or a ground rent that rises sharply. These terms can be changed by agreement with the freeholder in a deed of variation, and in some cases through a tribunal.

Dealing with it on a sale

If you are selling, there may be ways to deal with a short lease as part of the sale, such as starting the statutory process and passing the benefit of the notice to your buyer. Your solicitor will explain how that works and whether it fits your timescales.

Leaflegal does not tell you which option to choose. The firm we introduce you to will look at your lease and your circumstances and explain what is open to you.

Leasehold work is technical and deadline driven, so experience matters more than in many other kinds of property work. A general conveyancer may be excellent at buying and selling homes but rarely serve a lease extension notice. Here are some things people often look for when choosing a firm.

Regular leasehold experience

Ask how many lease extensions or enfranchisement claims the firm handles and who will do the work. A team that does this every week will know the common pitfalls, the usual tactics of large freeholders and the tribunal process if it gets that far.

Working with surveyors

The price for a lease extension or a freehold purchase is based on a valuation. A good firm works closely with specialist leasehold surveyors and can explain how the surveyor's report feeds into the notice and the negotiation. Some firms will suggest a surveyor, but you are free to choose your own.

Clear communication

These matters can take months. It helps to know who your contact is, how often you will hear from them and what you need to do at each stage. Ask the firm how it keeps clients updated.

Regulation and insurance

Solicitors in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and must carry professional indemnity insurance. Every firm Leaflegal introduces is regulated. You can check a firm's details on the regulator's website before you instruct it.

Transparent pricing

Ask what is included in the fee, what counts as an extra and what you might have to pay towards the freeholder's costs. A good firm will set this out in writing before any work starts.

Leasehold matters usually involve several different costs. Knowing what they are at the start makes it easier to compare quotes and plan your budget.

The premium

The premium is the price paid to the freeholder for the new lease or the freehold. It is usually the largest cost. Under the long standing rules it depends on factors such as the value of the flat, the years left on the lease, the ground rent and, for leases below 80 years, a share of what is called marriage value. The 2024 Act changes how premiums are worked out, including removing marriage value, but those parts depend on further regulations and are being introduced over time. A solicitor and surveyor will confirm what applies when you start.

Your own legal and valuation fees

You pay your own solicitor and surveyor. Many firms offer a fixed fee for a straightforward lease extension, with hourly rates for anything that becomes contested. Valuation fees vary with the property and the surveyor.

The freeholder's costs

Under the long standing statutory process, a leaseholder pays certain reasonable costs of the freeholder, such as their legal and valuation costs, but not their costs of going to a tribunal. The 2024 Act changes this so that each side generally pays its own costs, with some exceptions, but this change is part of the reforms being brought in over time. Your solicitor will explain the current position.

Other costs

There may be Land Registry fees, fees to your mortgage lender, and in some cases Stamp Duty Land Tax on a larger premium. Rates and thresholds change, so your solicitor will check what applies. If a dispute goes to a tribunal, there may be fees and further professional costs.

How long a lease matter takes depends on the route, the freeholder and whether the price is agreed. These are general ranges, and your solicitor will give you a more accurate picture once they see your lease.

Statutory lease extensions

Once the notice is served, the freeholder has a set period to reply with a counter notice. The date is stated in your notice and must be at least two months away. If the premium is agreed, the new lease can often be completed within a few months of serving the notice. If it is not agreed, there are strict deadlines for applying to the tribunal, and a contested case can take a year or longer.

Informal extensions

An informal extension can sometimes be completed more quickly, because there are no statutory waiting periods. In practice it still depends on how fast the freeholder responds and how long it takes to agree the terms.

Collective enfranchisement

Buying the freehold usually takes longer, because several flat owners have to organise themselves first. Getting participants together, setting up a company and agreeing funding can take months before any notice is served.

Lease variations

A simple variation agreed with a cooperative freeholder can be done fairly quickly. If the freeholder will not agree and a tribunal application is needed, it will take longer.

Planning around a sale

Many people only look at their lease when they decide to sell or remortgage. Starting early gives you more choice. If time is short, ask your solicitor what can be done in parallel with the sale.

Whatever the route, delays most often come from waiting for information, such as a copy of the lease, details of the freeholder or a response from a managing agent. Gathering what you can at the start, and replying quickly when your solicitor asks for something, helps keep things moving.

You do not need to have everything ready before you make an enquiry. These are the things a solicitor is likely to ask for, and having them to hand can speed up the first conversation.

  • A copy of your lease, or the title number of your flat
  • Roughly how many years are left on the lease
  • The name and address of your freeholder and any managing agent
  • Recent ground rent and service charge demands
  • Your mortgage lender's details, if you have a mortgage
  • Any letters or offers from the freeholder
  • Whether you are planning to sell or remortgage, and when
  • For a building wide claim, the number of flats and how many owners are interested

If you cannot find your lease

Most leases are registered at HM Land Registry, and a copy can usually be obtained online for a small fee. Your solicitor can do this for you. Older leases are sometimes not held in full, and the firm will explain how to deal with that.

Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
Is an informal extension the same as a deed of variation?
Sometimes. An informal extension can be done by granting a brand new lease or by varying the existing lease to add years. Your solicitor will explain which method is proposed and how it affects things like ground rent rules and registration.
Is an informal extension common?
Yes, many lease extensions are agreed informally, especially where the freeholder is a small landlord or a company owned by the flat owners. Larger freeholders also agree informal deals, sometimes on standard terms. Whether it is the right route for you depends on the offer and your plans, which is something a solicitor can talk through with you.
My freeholder has written to me with an offer. What now?
Many people ask a solicitor to review the offer before replying. They can explain what the terms mean, how they compare with the statutory route and whether anything might cause problems later. A surveyor can also give a view on value. You do not have to accept, and you do not have to reply straight away.
What ground rent terms should I look out for?
Rents that double at fixed intervals, rents linked to inflation without a cap, and rents that are high compared with the value of the flat can all cause concern for lenders and buyers. Rules introduced in 2022 also limit ground rent on many new leases. Your solicitor will check the proposed rent against these rules.
Can the freeholder add new terms to my lease?
In an informal deal, the freeholder can propose any changes, and you can accept, reject or negotiate them. This is different from the statutory route, where only limited changes are allowed. Your solicitor will point out any new terms and explain their effect.
Can I ask for changes too?
Yes. An informal negotiation works both ways. Leaseholders sometimes ask for defects in the lease to be fixed, for permission for alterations or for clearer service charge terms. Whether the freeholder agrees is a matter for negotiation.
How long should the new lease be?
That is for you and the freeholder to agree. It helps to compare the offer with what the statutory route would give, which is 90 years added under the long standing rules and 990 years under the 2024 Act once in force. Your solicitor can explain how the length could affect value and future plans.
Can my freeholder withdraw its offer?
Yes, until the deal is completed, the freeholder can usually withdraw or change its offer. If certainty matters, for example because of a sale, your solicitor may discuss whether serving a statutory notice would give more protection.
What is a rent review clause?
A rent review clause allows the ground rent to change during the lease, for example by doubling at fixed intervals or by rising with an inflation index. Some reviews are capped and others are not. Lenders and buyers look closely at these clauses, because they can make the rent much higher in future. Your solicitor will explain how any review clause in an offer would work over the life of the new lease.
Do all the owners have to agree?
No. Only the required number of qualifying owners need to take part. Owners who do not want to join can stay out, keep their current leases and have the company as their landlord afterwards. It is still worth involving as many as possible, as this spreads the cost.
How do we decide who pays what?
The group agrees this between themselves, usually in a participation agreement. Common methods include sharing according to the value each flat adds to the price or by floor area. Flats with shorter leases can account for a larger share under the long standing valuation rules. Your solicitor can explain the options but cannot choose for you.
What if someone drops out part way through?
This is one of the main risks in a group claim. A good participation agreement sets out what happens, for example whether a departing owner forfeits money already paid and whether someone else can take their place. If numbers fall below the minimum, the claim may be affected, so it is important to plan for this.
Do we need to set up a company?
The freehold is bought by a nominee purchaser. In practice this is nearly always a company owned by the participating owners, which makes ownership and future sales much simpler. Your solicitor will usually form the company as part of the work.
Can one solicitor act for all of us?
Usually, yes. One firm normally acts for the nominee company and the participating owners as a group. If the interests of some owners conflict, for example over how costs are shared, the firm may suggest that some take separate advice.
How do we get everyone's information together?
Your solicitor will usually send a questionnaire to each participant asking for their details, lease information and mortgage lender. Appointing one or two owners as the main contacts for the group can make communication much easier.
Should we look at the Right to Manage instead?
The Right to Manage lets qualifying owners take over management of the building without buying the freehold. It can suit groups whose main concern is how the building is run. Buying the freehold gives ownership and the ability to grant long leases, but costs more. Your solicitor can explain both options so the group can decide.
What is a section 13 notice?
It is the formal initial notice that starts a collective enfranchisement claim. It identifies the building and any extra land, names the participating owners, proposes a price and gives the freeholder a date to reply that is at least two months away. It has to be accurate and served correctly, so it is normally prepared by a solicitor.
What can the freeholder do after receiving our notice?
The freeholder can ask for information about the participants, require a deposit and arrange access so its surveyor can value the building. It then serves a counter notice accepting the claim, disputing it, or proposing a different price and terms. In some cases it can ask to lease back certain parts, such as commercial units.
Can I extend if I have only just bought the flat?
The old requirement to have owned the flat for two years before using the statutory right was removed by the 2024 Act from 31 January 2025. This means a recent buyer may be able to start an extension straight away. Your solicitor will confirm this before serving a notice.
Is there an option to just remove the ground rent?
The 2024 Act includes provisions allowing qualifying leaseholders to pay to reduce their ground rent to a peppercorn without extending the term. This depends on commencement and supporting regulations. For a short lease, extending is often the main concern. Your solicitor will explain whether any ground rent option is available and relevant.
What if my freeholder cannot be found?
Some short lease flats have a freeholder that cannot be traced, for example because a company has been dissolved or an individual owner has died. There is a separate court process that can allow a lease extension to go ahead in that situation, with the price usually decided by the tribunal and paid into court. A solicitor can explain how long it might take.
Can I sell a flat with a short lease?
Yes, but it can be harder. Buyers who need a mortgage may not be able to get one, and others may offer less to reflect the cost of extending. Some owners start a statutory extension and pass the notice to the buyer, which can make the flat more attractive. A solicitor can explain how this would work for your sale.
How does passing a notice to a buyer work?
Once a statutory notice has been served, its benefit can usually be assigned to a buyer at the same time as the flat is transferred. The buyer then takes over the claim and completes the extension. The assignment must be done correctly and at the right time. Both solicitors will agree the details, including who pays which costs.
Why will my lender not remortgage my flat?
Lenders usually require a minimum number of years on the lease, often counted from the end of the mortgage. If your lease is below that minimum, the lender may decline or set conditions. Requirements vary between lenders and change over time. A mortgage broker can explain the lending side, and a solicitor can explain the extension options.
I am buying a flat with a short lease. What can I do?
You can ask the seller to start a statutory extension and assign the notice to you on completion, or negotiate a price that reflects the cost of extending later. Your own solicitor will explain the options, check the lease carefully and tell you whether you are likely to be able to extend once you own the flat.
Do cash buyers buy short lease flats?
Some do, including investors who plan to extend the lease themselves. They usually take the cost and time of extending into account when deciding what to offer. Your estate agent can talk to you about the market, and your solicitor can explain the legal side of selling without extending.
Will a short lease delay my sale?
It can. Buyers' solicitors will raise questions, lenders may set conditions, and some buyers may want the extension completed first. Dealing with the lease early, before or soon after you put the flat on the market, usually reduces delays.
The service charge percentages do not add up. What happens?
If the shares across all the flats do not add up to 100 per cent, the freeholder may be unable to recover the full cost of running the building, or some owners may pay too much. This can usually only be fixed by varying several leases together, either by agreement or through a tribunal application.
My lease does not mention the reserve fund. Is that a problem?
If a lease does not allow the landlord to collect money for a reserve fund, it may not be possible to require owners to contribute, even if they have been doing so. This can make planning for major works difficult. A variation can add a reserve fund clause where the parties agree or the tribunal orders it.
Can I change a ban on pets or alterations?
Restrictions like these can be changed by agreement with the freeholder, who may ask for a payment. Sometimes a one off licence or consent is simpler than varying the lease. Tribunal applications under the 1987 Act do not usually cover this kind of term. Your solicitor will explain which approach fits.
My flat has no written right to use the shared entrance. What can be done?
A missing right of way or a missing right to use pipes and cables can often be added by a deed of variation. Depending on the facts, there may also be other legal arguments that the right exists. A solicitor will look at the title and the history of the building.
Can a ground rent cause problems with lenders even if it is low now?
Yes. Lenders look at how the ground rent could change over the life of the mortgage and the lease, not just what it is today. A low rent that doubles regularly can become a concern. Your solicitor will look at the whole ground rent clause and explain how a lender is likely to view it.
What if my freeholder agrees?
If the freeholder agrees, the solicitors draft a deed of variation, both sides sign it, lenders consent and it is registered at HM Land Registry. This is usually the quickest and cheapest route. The freeholder may still ask for its legal costs and, sometimes, a payment.
What if my freeholder refuses?
Your solicitor will consider whether the problem falls within the tribunal's powers under the Landlord and Tenant Act 1987, whether a court could rectify a mistake, or whether the issue could be dealt with through a lease extension. Each option has different costs and risks, which your solicitor will explain.
Do other leaseholders need to be involved?
If the change only affects your lease, usually not. If it affects how costs are shared across the building, or several leases share the same defect, the other leaseholders and their lenders may need to be involved. Dealing with all the leases together is sometimes the only practical solution.
Does my mortgage lender need to agree?
Usually, yes. Your lender has security over your lease and will want to know that a change does not reduce the value of its security. Your solicitor will ask for consent and deal with any conditions the lender sets.
Should I extend my lease before selling?
That depends on how long is left, your timescale and the market. Some sellers extend first, some start the statutory process and pass the notice to the buyer, and some sell without extending. A solicitor can explain the pros and cons of each for your flat, and the decision is yours.
Can I pass a lease extension to my buyer?
Under the statutory route, once a notice has been served, its benefit can usually be assigned to the buyer at the same time as the sale completes. The buyer then takes over the claim. This needs to be done in the right order and form, so both solicitors will coordinate it.
My lease has a doubling ground rent. Will that affect my sale?
It can. Some lenders and buyers are cautious about ground rents that double at fixed intervals. Some freeholders have agreed to vary these terms. Your solicitor can explain whether a variation might be needed and how it could be arranged alongside the sale.
I made alterations without the freeholder's consent. What happens?
Tell your solicitor early. Depending on the lease and the work done, it may be possible to get retrospective consent from the freeholder, or indemnity insurance may be offered. Building regulations approval may also be needed. Leaving it until the buyer's solicitor asks can cause delays.
What if my flat has a share of the freehold?
The buyer will usually take over your share or membership of the freehold company. The buyer's solicitor will ask for company documents, such as share certificates, accounts and details of the directors. Having these ready can speed up the sale.
Does the length of my lease affect who can buy?
Yes. Buyers who need a mortgage are limited by their lender's minimum lease requirements, which vary between lenders. Cash buyers are not limited in the same way, but they usually take the lease length into account in their offer.
My lease has a restriction on selling. What does that mean?
Some leases require the freeholder's consent before the flat is sold, or require the buyer to sign a deed of covenant or join a management company. These are usually routine steps with a fee rather than real obstacles. Your solicitor will check the lease and tell the buyer's solicitor what will be needed.
How are service charges dealt with on completion?
Service charges paid in advance are usually split between seller and buyer up to the completion date. Your solicitor works out the figures and includes them on the completion statement. Arrears are normally paid from the sale proceeds. Because service charge years do not always match the date you sell, the figures can look complicated. Your solicitor will explain how they have been worked out and whether any amount is being held back until the accounts are finalised.
What is a service charge retention?
It is money held back from the sale proceeds on completion to cover a possible balancing charge for the current service charge year, or a known future cost. It is released once the final figures are known. The amount is negotiated between the solicitors.
