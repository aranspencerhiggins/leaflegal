Trademarks explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A trademark is the sign customers use to recognise your business. It is usually a name or a logo, but it can also be a slogan, a product name, a shape, a colour combination or even a sound. In the UK, trademarks are registered at the UK Intellectual Property Office, often called the UK IPO, and one registration covers England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Registering a trademark gives you the exclusive right to use it for the goods and services listed in the registration. That right makes it much easier to stop copycats, to take down lookalike listings online and to license or sell your brand later. Without a registration you may still have some protection through the law of passing off, but it is harder and usually more expensive to rely on.

Trademarks sit alongside other kinds of intellectual property. Copyright protects original creative work such as writing, photographs, artwork, music and software. Design rights protect the look of a product. Many businesses need help with more than one of these, which is why the firms we introduce handle copyright and designs as well as trademarks.

Who does trademark work

Trademark work is not a reserved legal activity, so in principle anyone can file an application. In practice most businesses use one of two kinds of regulated professional. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and can handle filings, disputes and court claims. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg, the regulator for patent and trade mark attorneys, and specialise in searching, filing and UK IPO proceedings such as oppositions.

Leaflegal only introduces trademark enquiries to firms regulated by one of those two bodies. Both sorts of firm work by phone, email and video, so the firm does not need to be near you. Because there is one UK register, we can introduce you wherever you are in the UK.

There is rarely only one way to deal with a brand question. The right route depends on what you sell, where you trade, how long you have used the name and how much risk you are prepared to accept. The firm will look at your own situation, but it helps to know the usual options before the first call.

Protecting a brand you already use

If you have been trading under a name for a while without registering it, the usual first step is a clearance search to see whether anyone has earlier rights, followed by an application for the classes that match what you sell. Using a name for years does not stop someone else registering something similar, so many owners file as soon as they know the brand is staying.

Launching something new

Before you commit to packaging, signage or a website, a search can tell you whether the name is likely to be free to use and register. If the search shows a problem, it is far cheaper to change the name at that stage. Some businesses file an application before launch so that the filing date is secured early.

When someone else is using a similar brand

Options range from a polite letter to a formal cease and desist letter, a takedown request to an online marketplace, an opposition at the UK IPO or a court claim. Many disputes settle without a hearing, sometimes through a coexistence agreement in which both sides agree how they will use their marks.

When you are on the receiving end

If you receive a letter, an opposition or a takedown notice, the firm can tell you how strong the other side's case looks and what your options are. Those may include changing the name, narrowing what you sell under it, negotiating a timetable to rebrand or defending your position.

Most trademark firms are small specialist practices or IP teams within larger law firms. Some focus on filings and portfolios, while others spend more time on disputes. When the firm contacts you, it is worth asking a few questions to check they are a good fit.

  • Are you a solicitor or a chartered trade mark attorney, and who regulates you?
  • How much of your work is UK trademark filing and how much is disputes?
  • Do you work with businesses of my size and in my sector?
  • Will you search before filing, and what will the search cover?
  • How do you charge, and what is included in a fixed fee?
  • Who will actually do the work and how will you keep me updated?

You can check a solicitor on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register and a chartered trade mark attorney on the IPReg register. Both registers are free to search online.

Solicitor or attorney

For a straightforward filing, a chartered trade mark attorney or a trademark solicitor can both do the job well. If a dispute looks likely to go to court, a solicitor can take the claim forward, and many attorney firms work closely with solicitors for that stage. Firms outside the UK

If you also need protection abroad, ask whether the firm files international applications through the Madrid system and works with local agents in the countries you need. Most UK trademark firms do this as a matter of routine.

Trademark costs come in two parts. The first is the official fee charged by the UK IPO, which depends on how many classes your application covers. The second is the professional fee charged by the firm for its advice and work. Official fees change from time to time, so check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask the firm.

Many firms quote a fixed fee for a clearance search, a fixed fee for preparing and filing a UK application, and a further fixed fee if the examiner raises an objection that needs a reply. Renewals and recording a change of owner are also often fixed fee work.

Disputes are harder to price at the outset because they depend on how the other side responds. Firms usually charge in stages, for example a fee for reviewing the evidence and sending a letter, and a separate estimate for an opposition or court claim. Ask for an estimate for each stage and for an idea of the costs you could have to pay the other side if you lose.

Most trademark problems are avoidable with a little planning. These are the issues firms see most often, and why they matter.

Choosing a descriptive name

Names that describe the product, its quality or where it comes from are tempting because customers understand them straight away. They are also the names most likely to be refused, and the hardest to enforce against competitors who use similar words. A name with a twist, or an invented word, is usually easier to protect.

Filing too narrowly or too widely

An application that only covers part of what you sell can leave gaps that a competitor can use. An application that claims far more than you will ever sell can attract objections and become vulnerable to a non use challenge after five years. The wording of the goods and services is one of the most important parts of the application.

The wrong owner

Marks are sometimes filed in the name of a director or owner personally, or a company that later stops trading, when the business intends to own the brand. Investors and buyers often check this during due diligence. Putting it right usually means recording an assignment, which is simpler before there is a dispute or a sale.

Ignoring letters and deadlines

Examiner reports, oppositions and renewal reminders all have fixed deadlines. Letters from other brand owners usually have a deadline too. Leaving them until the last minute reduces your options, and missing them can mean losing rights altogether.

Scam invoices

After you file, your details appear on the public register. Businesses often receive official looking letters offering to publish or monitor their mark for a large fee. These are not from the UK IPO. If you are unsure about a letter, ask the firm before you pay anything.

Trademark matters run to official timetables set by the UK IPO, and many of the deadlines are fixed. Knowing roughly how long each stage takes helps you plan launches, investment rounds and marketing.

  • A clearance search usually takes a few days to a couple of weeks, depending on how wide it is
  • The UK IPO usually examines a new application within a few weeks of filing
  • Accepted applications are published for two months, which can be extended to three, so that others can oppose
  • If nobody opposes, registration usually follows around four months after filing
  • An opposition commonly adds many months, and longer if it goes all the way to a decision
  • A registration lasts ten years from the filing date and can be renewed again and again

Disputes outside the UK IPO move at their own pace. A cease and desist letter usually gives the other side a short deadline to reply, often around two weeks. Court claims take much longer, which is one reason many disputes settle early.

Deadlines that matter

The deadlines to watch most closely are the date to reply to an examiner's report, the end of the opposition period for an application you want to challenge, the deadline in any opposition you are defending and the renewal date for each registration. Missing some of these can mean losing rights altogether, so tell the firm about any date on your paperwork.

Trademarks questions and answers

What is the difference between a trademark attorney and a trademark solicitor?
Both can register and protect a UK trademark. A chartered trade mark attorney is regulated by IPReg and specialises in trademark filings and UK IPO proceedings. A trademark solicitor is regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and can also bring or defend infringement claims in court.
Do I need a lawyer to register a trademark?
No, anyone can apply at the UK IPO. Many businesses use a trademark solicitor or attorney because the classes and the wording of the goods and services decide how well the brand is protected, and the application can’t be widened once it is filed.
Is an intellectual property lawyer the same as a trademark lawyer?
Trademarks are one part of intellectual property law. Many IP lawyers also handle copyright, design rights and passing off, which is why we cover those alongside trademarks.
Which areas do you cover?
Trademarks and related IP across the whole of the UK, because there is one UK trademark register.
Is a trademark attorney the same as a solicitor?
Not quite. Both can handle trademark work. Chartered trade mark attorneys are regulated by IPReg and specialise in trademarks, while solicitors are regulated by the SRA and can also take disputes to court.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
What can be registered as a trademark?
Names, logos, slogans, product names and some shapes, colours and sounds can all be registered if they are distinctive and are not simply a description of the goods or services. A mark must also be clear enough to be shown on the register. A trademark professional can tell you how likely your mark is to be accepted and whether a small change would make it stronger.
Do I need a trademark if I have a registered company?
Registering a company at Companies House only puts the name on the companies register. It does not give you trademark rights or stop someone using a similar name for their goods or services. A trademark registration at the UK IPO is separate, and many businesses register their main brand as a trademark as well as forming a company.
How many classes do I need?
Goods and services are divided into 45 classes. You need the classes that cover what you sell now and what you realistically plan to sell. Each extra class adds to the official fee, so firms usually help you focus on the classes that matter most rather than claiming everything. The firm will help you choose.
Can I trademark a common word?
Sometimes. A common word that has nothing to do with your goods can be very distinctive, like a fruit name for computers. A word that describes your goods, such as a quality or a place, is usually refused for those goods. The examiner looks at the mark in relation to what you are selling, so the answer depends on your business.
Should I register my name or my logo?
A word mark protects the name however it is written, so it usually gives the widest protection for the brand itself. A logo mark protects the design and any words as they appear in it. Many businesses start with the word mark and add the logo later. The firm can explain which suits your budget and plans.
Does a UK trademark protect me abroad?
No. A UK registration covers the UK only. If you trade or plan to trade abroad, you can apply in other countries, often through the Madrid system, which lets you extend a UK application or registration to many other countries with one international application. The firm can explain the costs and timing.
What does the R in a circle mean?
The ® symbol shows that a mark is registered. It is an offence in the UK to use it for a mark that is not registered. Until registration, many businesses use ™ instead, which simply tells people you claim the name as a trademark.
What happens after I file?
The UK IPO examines the application to check that the mark is distinctive and acceptable. It also searches for similar earlier marks and may tell their owners about your application. If the examiner is satisfied, the application is published so that others can oppose it. If there is no opposition, the mark is registered and you receive a certificate.
What rights do I have without registering?
If you have built up goodwill under a name, you may be able to stop others misrepresenting their business as connected with yours through a passing off claim. You would need to show goodwill, a misrepresentation that is likely to confuse customers and damage. It is usually harder and more expensive than relying on a registration.
How do I find out if someone copies my brand?
Many businesses use a watch service, which alerts them when someone applies for a similar mark so they can oppose it in time. Online monitoring can also flag marketplace listings and domain names. Customers and suppliers are often the first to notice and tell you. A firm can set up a watch for you.
Can I stop someone registering a similar company name?
Sometimes. The Company Names Tribunal at the UK IPO can order a company to change a name that is the same as or similar to one in which you have goodwill or a reputation, in some circumstances. The firm can tell you whether this or another route suits your situation better.
What is a coexistence agreement?
It is a contract between two businesses with similar marks, setting out how each will use its mark so they can both carry on without confusing customers. It might limit the goods, the markets or the way a logo is shown. Coexistence agreements are a common way to settle oppositions and disputes.
Can I license my trademark to someone else?
Yes. A licence lets another business use your mark on agreed terms, often in return for a royalty. A written licence protects the quality of your brand and your rights. Licences of registered marks can be recorded at the UK IPO. A firm can prepare or check the agreement.
Can my trademark be cancelled?
A registered mark can be revoked if it has not been genuinely used for the registered goods or services for a continuous five year period, and it can be declared invalid if it should never have been registered. Keeping evidence of use helps protect your registration against these challenges.
How often should I review my trademarks?
Many businesses review their marks when they launch a new product, enter a new market, change their logo or prepare for investment or a sale. A review checks that the right company owns each mark, that the goods and services still match what you sell and that renewals are in hand.
What is trademark infringement?
Infringement is using a sign that is identical or similar to a registered mark, for identical or similar goods or services, in a way that is likely to cause confusion, without permission. Marks with a reputation get wider protection. Whether something infringes depends on the facts, and the firm will look at both marks and how they are used.
What should I do if I receive a cease and desist letter?
Don't ignore it and don't reply in anger. Note any deadline and ask a firm to look at it before you respond. The firm can tell you whether the claim looks strong, what your options are and how to reply in a way that protects your position. Many letters can be resolved without going to court.
Is it risky to threaten someone with a trademark claim?
It can be. UK law lets a person sue if they are threatened with infringement proceedings without justification, in some circumstances. This is why letters about trademarks, designs and patents are usually written or checked by a professional who knows what can safely be said and to whom.
Can online marketplaces remove copies of my products?
Most large marketplaces and social media platforms have procedures for reporting listings that infringe a registered trademark or copyright. A registration makes this much easier. A firm can file reports for you, deal with counter notices and decide whether further action is worthwhile.
Where are trademark cases heard?
Oppositions and cancellation actions are decided by the UK IPO tribunal. Infringement claims go to court. In England and Wales, smaller and simpler claims often go to the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court, where recoverable costs are capped, and larger claims go to the High Court. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own courts.
Will my dispute end up in court?
Most don't. Many disputes are settled by letters and negotiation, sometimes with a rebrand timetable, an undertaking or a coexistence agreement. Court is usually the last step. The firm will explain the risks and costs of each option before you decide anything.
Can I use a name if nobody has registered it?
Not always safely. Someone may have unregistered rights through use, or a pending application that has not yet been published. Company names and domain names can also cause problems. A clearance search looks for these as well as registered marks, which is why firms usually recommend one before a launch.
Do I need to register copyright?
No. In the UK copyright arises automatically when original work is created and recorded. There is no official register. Keeping dated drafts, original files and records of publication helps you show that you created the work and when, which matters if someone copies it.
Who owns the copyright in work I paid for?
Usually the person who created it, unless there is a written assignment. If an employee creates the work as part of their job, the employer usually owns it. Commissioning a freelancer does not on its own transfer copyright, so businesses often use written terms that assign it. A firm can check or prepare the contract.
What is a design right?
A design right protects the appearance of a product, such as its shape, pattern or decoration. A registered design can last up to 25 years, renewed every five years. Unregistered design rights arise automatically but last for less time and can be harder to enforce, because you need to show copying.
Can I protect a design I have already shown publicly?
Often yes, if you apply to register it within 12 months of first disclosing it. After that, the earlier disclosure can stop the design being registered. It is best to speak to a firm before launching or showing a new product, especially at trade fairs or online.
I received a bill for using a photo online. What now?
Image claims are common. Don't ignore the letter, and don't pay before checking whether the claim and the amount are justified. A firm can look at who owns the image, how it was used and whether the amount claimed is reasonable, and help you respond or negotiate.
Can copyright and a trademark protect the same thing?
Yes, sometimes. A logo can be protected by copyright as an artistic work and registered as a trademark at the same time. The two rights work differently. Copyright stops copying of the artwork, while a trademark stops others using a confusingly similar sign for similar goods or services, whether or not they copied. A firm can explain which helps most in your case.
