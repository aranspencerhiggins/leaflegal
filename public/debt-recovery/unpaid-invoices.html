Unpaid invoices in brief

Unpaid invoices hurt cash flow. If polite reminders have not worked, a debt recovery solicitor can step in with a formal demand, add statutory late payment interest and compensation, and take the matter to court if needed.

Unpaid invoices explained

Pick a topic to read more.

An unpaid invoice is money your business has earned but not received. You delivered the goods or finished the work, you sent the invoice, and the due date has come and gone. For a small or medium sized business, even one large invoice going unpaid can affect cash flow, and several at once can make it hard to pay staff, suppliers and HMRC on time.

Most unpaid invoices are not the result of a customer refusing to pay. Often the invoice has been lost in an accounts department, is waiting for a purchase order number, or is sitting with someone who has left the business. Sometimes the customer is under financial pressure and is paying the creditors who shout loudest. Sometimes there is a complaint that nobody has told you about. Working out which of these is happening is the first step to getting paid.

This page is about invoices owed by one business to another in England and Wales. Leaflegal does not introduce firms for consumer debts, so if your customer bought something for their own personal use rather than for their business, this service is not the right fit.

When your own reminders have not worked, a debt recovery solicitor can step in. They will look at the contract and the invoices, confirm exactly what is owed and by whom, and suggest the most cost effective route to payment. For many businesses, that route ends with a solicitor's letter and a payment. For others, it means a court claim and enforcement.

The longer an invoice stays unpaid, the harder it usually becomes to collect. Memories fade, staff move on, and a customer that is struggling may run out of money altogether. Debts also have a legal time limit. None of this means you need to rush into court, but it does mean that a clear plan, made early, tends to work better than months of polite reminders.

Common situations

  • A customer who keeps promising payment next week
  • An invoice paid in part with no explanation for the shortfall
  • A customer who has gone quiet and stopped answering calls
  • A complaint about the work raised only after the invoice was chased
  • A customer who has changed its trading name or moved to a new company
  • Several invoices to the same customer building up over months
  • A large final invoice on a project that has been held back

Many businesses try to collect a debt themselves before involving a solicitor. That is sensible, and what you do at this stage can make a later claim stronger. These are some of the steps businesses commonly take. They are general information, not advice about your own debt.

Check the invoice is right

Make sure the invoice shows the correct customer name, address, purchase order number, amount and due date. A surprising number of invoices go unpaid because they do not match what the customer's accounts team needs. Fixing a simple error can release payment quickly.

Speak to the right person

An email to a general inbox can be ignored. Find out who approves payments and contact them directly. A short, polite phone call often achieves more than several automated reminders, and it may bring any complaint into the open early.

Keep a written record

Note the date of every reminder, call and promise to pay. If the customer admits the debt in writing, keep that message. A written acknowledgement can be useful evidence later, and it can also affect the time limit for bringing a claim.

Consider pausing supply

If your terms allow it, some businesses put an account on stop until overdue invoices are cleared. This can be effective, but it can also make a dispute worse. A solicitor can explain what your terms allow before you take that step.

Know when to stop chasing

Repeated reminders over many months can signal that you will not take further action. Many businesses set an internal point, such as a certain number of days overdue, at which they pass the debt to a solicitor. Acting promptly also gives you a better chance of being paid before other creditors if the customer is in difficulty.

Send a final reminder

Before passing a debt on, many businesses send one last written reminder making clear that the matter will be handed to solicitors if payment is not received by a set date. This gives the customer a final chance and shows that you acted fairly. It is not a substitute for a formal letter before action, which a solicitor will usually still send.

Every debt is different, but recovery of an unpaid invoice usually follows a similar path once a solicitor is involved. Your solicitor will tell you which steps apply and how long each is likely to take.

1. Reviewing the paperwork

The solicitor looks at the contract or terms, the order, the delivery records and the invoices. They confirm the correct legal name of the debtor, the amount due, when it fell due and whether interest or compensation can be added. They also ask about any complaint the customer has made.

2. Checking the debtor

Before spending money on a claim, many solicitors check public records. They may look at the company's filing history, whether it is overdue with accounts, whether it has other judgments against it and whether any insolvency notices have been published. This helps judge whether recovery is realistic.

3. A formal letter

The solicitor usually sends a letter before action setting out the debt and a deadline for payment. The letter complies with the court's expectations on pre-action conduct, which matters if the case later goes to court. Many invoices are paid at this point.

4. Negotiation

If the customer responds with an offer, such as payment by instalments, the solicitor can negotiate terms and record any agreement in writing. A clear written agreement makes it easier to act quickly if the customer misses a payment.

5. Court claim or other route

If payment does not arrive, the solicitor will discuss the next step. This is usually a court claim, but in some cases an insolvency route such as a statutory demand may be considered. The choice depends on the amount, whether the debt is disputed and what is known about the debtor.

6. Getting the money in

Once judgment is entered, the debtor is ordered to pay. If they still do not, the solicitor can use enforcement tools. Your solicitor will keep you updated and explain the cost of each further step before taking it.

Staying in control

You remain in charge of the decisions throughout. Your solicitor will explain the options, the likely cost and the risks at each point, but whether to accept an offer, issue a claim or stop chasing is your choice. Many businesses find it helpful to agree in advance the point at which they would rather write a debt off than spend more on it.

When an invoice is chased, customers sometimes raise reasons for not paying. Some are genuine and some are not. How a reason is dealt with affects which routes are open, so it helps to understand the common ones.

Complaints about quality

A customer may say the goods were faulty or the work was poor. If the complaint is raised for the first time only after the invoice is chased, that can be relevant, but it does not automatically mean it is false. A solicitor will look at the contract, any inspection or acceptance terms and the evidence on both sides.

Part disputes

Sometimes only part of an invoice is in dispute. A customer might accept most of the work but challenge one item. In that case, the undisputed part may be recoverable more quickly. A solicitor can help separate what is genuinely in dispute from what is not.

Set off

A customer may claim it is owed money by you, for example for late delivery, and that it can deduct this from what it owes. Whether set off is allowed depends on the contract and the circumstances. Some business terms exclude set off altogether.

Wrong party

A customer may say the contract was with a different company in its group, or with a director personally. This is why it matters to check exactly who placed the order and who the invoices were addressed to.

No purchase order

Larger businesses often refuse to pay invoices that do not quote a valid purchase order number. This is usually an administrative issue rather than a real dispute, but it can delay payment if not dealt with.

Cash flow problems

Sometimes a customer simply admits it cannot pay right now. That is not a defence to the debt, but it does affect what is realistic. A payment plan backed by a written agreement may recover more than pushing a struggling business into insolvency, where unsecured creditors often receive little. A solicitor can help you weigh up the choices.

A genuine dispute does not mean you cannot recover the money. It does mean the route, cost and timescale may change, and some quicker tools may not be suitable. Your solicitor will explain the position honestly.

Recovering an unpaid invoice can cost very little or quite a lot, depending on how the customer responds. It helps to understand the main types of cost before you start.

Solicitor's fees

Many firms charge a fixed fee for reviewing an invoice and sending a letter before action. Fixed fees are also common for issuing a straightforward claim and requesting judgment where the debtor does not respond. If the customer defends, the work becomes more involved and is often charged by the hour or in agreed stages.

Court fees

If a claim is needed, the court charges an issue fee based on the amount claimed, and further fees for some later steps. These are set by the government and change from time to time. Your solicitor will tell you the current fees, or you can check GOV.UK.

Adding interest and compensation

Between businesses, a creditor can often add interest and a fixed sum of compensation to a late invoice under late payment law, unless the contract provides its own remedy. In some cases reasonable recovery costs can also be claimed. These sums can help offset the cost of chasing.

What you may recover

If you win a court claim, you can usually recover some of your legal costs from the debtor, but for many debt claims these are limited to fixed amounts. Ask your solicitor what is likely to be recoverable before you decide how far to take the matter.

Keep in mind that the cost of each step should make sense against the amount owed and the chance of payment. Your solicitor should give you a realistic view of both before recommending the next stage.

Once the current debt is dealt with, many businesses look at how to reduce the risk of the same thing happening again. A solicitor who handles debt recovery can often help with this too.

Clear terms and conditions

Written terms that set out payment dates, interest on late payment, retention of title and the right to suspend supply give you a firmer footing. Terms only help if they form part of the contract, so it matters how they are shared with the customer and accepted.

Credit checks and limits

Checking a new customer before offering credit, and setting a sensible credit limit, can stop large debts building up. Some businesses ask for trade references or a deposit for larger orders.

Personal guarantees

Where a customer is a small company with few assets, some suppliers ask a director to sign a personal guarantee. This gives the supplier someone else to pursue if the company does not pay.

Staged payments

For longer projects, invoicing in stages rather than at the end reduces the amount at risk at any one time.

A clear credit control routine

Sending invoices promptly, following up before the due date and escalating at set points all help. A consistent routine also shows customers that you take payment seriously.

Reviewing slow payers

It can help to look back over the past year and see which customers regularly pay late. A pattern of slow payment is often an early warning sign. Some businesses shorten credit terms for these customers, ask for payment before delivery or reduce their credit limit. A solicitor can explain how to make changes to terms with existing customers so that they are likely to apply.

Unpaid invoices questions and answers

Can I recover my legal costs?
Some costs can be recovered, and fixed compensation and interest may be claimed under late payment law. A solicitor will explain.
What if the debt is disputed?
The solicitor will advise on the strength of the dispute and the right route.
Do you help with consumer debts?
No. We only introduce businesses recovering debts from other businesses.
Can you help with personal debts?
No. We only introduce businesses recovering debts owed by other businesses.
How much does debt recovery cost?
Many firms charge a fixed fee for a letter before action and agreed fees for court stages. They will explain their charges before you instruct them.
Do I need to be a limited company?
No. Sole traders, partnerships and companies can all recover business debts.
How long should I wait before using a solicitor?
There is no fixed rule. Many businesses pass a debt to a solicitor once it is clearly overdue and their own reminders have been ignored or met with empty promises. Waiting a long time can make recovery harder, especially if the customer is struggling. A solicitor can explain the options at any stage.
Will a solicitor's letter make a difference?
Often it does. A letter on a law firm's letterhead shows the customer that you are prepared to take the matter further and sets a clear deadline. Many customers who have ignored ordinary reminders pay or make an offer once a formal letter arrives, though there is no guarantee.
Can I accept a payment plan?
You can agree to accept instalments if you wish. It helps to record the plan in writing, including what happens if a payment is missed. A solicitor can draft a short agreement that makes it easier to take action quickly if the customer falls behind.
The customer paid part of the invoice. What now?
A part payment reduces the debt but does not usually settle it, unless you have agreed to accept less in full settlement. A part payment can also restart the time limit for bringing a claim. A solicitor will work out what remains due and whether interest can be added to the balance.
Can I chase invoices that are several years old?
Possibly. A claim for a contract debt must usually be started within six years of the date it fell due, though a written acknowledgement or a part payment can restart the clock. Older debts can be harder to prove, so gather whatever records you have and speak to a solicitor promptly.
Can I stop supplying the customer until they pay?
That depends on your contract. Some terms allow a supplier to suspend deliveries or work while invoices are overdue, while others do not. Stopping supply without a right to do so could cause problems. A solicitor can check your terms before you decide.
What if the customer promises to pay but never does?
Repeated promises without payment are common. It is worth keeping a note of each promise and asking for it in writing. If a deadline set in writing passes without payment, a formal letter from a solicitor is often the next step.
Do I need to send a letter before issuing a claim?
Courts expect parties to exchange information and try to settle before a claim is issued. Skipping this step can lead to costs penalties, even if you win. A solicitor will usually send a letter before action that meets these expectations, and will explain the deadline that applies to your customer.
What if the customer says the work was not good enough?
A complaint about quality does not end your claim, but it does need to be taken seriously. A solicitor will look at the contract, any acceptance or inspection terms and the evidence, and explain how strong the complaint appears and which routes are still sensible.
Can I still recover the part that is not disputed?
Often, yes. If the customer only disputes part of an invoice, the undisputed amount may be recoverable separately and more quickly. A solicitor can help identify what is genuinely in dispute and pursue the rest.
The customer says it is owed money by us. Can it deduct that?
This is called set off. Whether the customer can deduct money depends on your contract and the nature of its own claim. Some business terms exclude set off. A solicitor will look at your terms and the customer's claim and explain the position.
The customer raised a complaint only after I chased. Does that matter?
It can be relevant. A complaint that appears for the first time months after delivery, and only when payment is requested, may carry less weight than one raised promptly. However, it still needs to be looked at properly. A solicitor will consider the timing along with the other evidence.
Should I offer a discount to settle?
That is a commercial decision for your business. Some creditors accept a reduced sum to avoid the cost and time of a claim. A solicitor can help you weigh up the offer, but they will not decide for you, and any settlement should be recorded clearly in writing.
Will a dispute go to court?
Not always. Many disputes settle through negotiation once both sides have set out their positions. If agreement cannot be reached, a claim may be issued and the court may expect the parties to consider mediation or other ways of settling. Your solicitor will guide you through each stage.
What evidence helps if the customer disputes the invoice?
Useful evidence includes the signed order or accepted quote, your terms and conditions, delivery notes or proof of completion, photos, timesheets and any messages where the customer approved the work or asked for changes. Emails in which the customer accepts the debt or promises to pay can be especially helpful. Your solicitor will tell you what matters most.
How do I know who I contracted with?
Look at the order, the email that placed it, any account application and the name on your invoices. If the customer is a company, check its registered name and number on Companies House. A solicitor will confirm the correct party before sending any formal letter or claim.
The customer has changed its name. Can I still claim?
A company that changes its name is still the same legal entity, so the debt remains. If the business has moved to a different company, the position is more complicated. A solicitor can check the company records and explain who is liable.
What if the customer is a sole trader?
A sole trader is personally responsible for the debts of the business. Different pre-action rules apply where the debtor is an individual, including a longer response period. Insolvency routes are also different. A solicitor will explain how this affects your options.
What if the debtor goes into liquidation?
Once a company enters liquidation, creditors usually cannot sue it without permission, and must instead submit a claim to the liquidator. Unsecured creditors often receive only a small proportion of what is owed, or nothing. A solicitor can explain your options, including any retention of title or personal guarantee.
Can I find out if the debtor can pay?
Some information is public, such as filed accounts, charges over company assets, county court judgments and insolvency notices. These checks are not a full picture but can help. A solicitor can carry them out or arrange more detailed investigations where the debt justifies it.
Can I claim against the directors personally?
Usually not. A company is a separate legal person and its directors are not normally liable for its debts. There are exceptions, such as where a director has signed a personal guarantee. A solicitor will check whether any exception might apply.
The customer has stopped answering. What can be done?
Silence is common and is not a reason to give up. A formal letter sent to the customer's registered office or trading address often prompts a response. If it does not, a court claim can be issued and, if the customer still does not respond, judgment may be entered without a hearing. A solicitor will explain the steps.
How much does it cost to recover an unpaid invoice?
Many firms offer fixed fees for a letter before action and for an undefended claim. Defended claims and enforcement usually cost more. Court fees are extra. Ask the firm for a written quote and an explanation of what each stage might cost.
Can I add the cost of chasing to the invoice?
Between businesses, late payment law often allows a creditor to add a fixed sum of compensation for each late invoice, and in some cases reasonable recovery costs. Your own contract may also deal with this. A solicitor will check what can be added in your case.
Is it worth chasing a small invoice?
It depends on the amount, the cost of recovery and the chance of payment. For a smaller sum, a fixed fee letter may be proportionate. A solicitor will give you an honest view and may suggest a lighter approach for low value debts.
Does Leaflegal charge me anything?
No. Leaflegal is free for businesses to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, which does not change what you pay them.
Will I get all my legal fees back if I win?
Not always. In many debt claims, the costs you can recover from the debtor are limited to fixed amounts set by the court rules, which may be less than you have paid. Statutory compensation and recovery costs under late payment law may help close the gap. Your solicitor will explain what is realistic before you decide.
What should I send the solicitor?
Copies of the invoices, the contract or terms, proof of delivery or completion, any statement of account and your emails with the customer. Include any complaint the customer has made. The more complete the picture, the quicker the solicitor can advise.
Will the solicitor speak to my customer?
Usually the first contact is a formal letter. The solicitor may then deal with any response, including calls or emails from the customer or its own solicitors. You can agree with your solicitor how involved you want to be.
Can a firm handle all our overdue accounts?
Many firms handle regular debt recovery work for businesses and can take on several debts at once. Some offer online systems for sending new debts and tracking progress. Ask the firm what it offers.
Can the solicitor help with our credit control process?
Many firms that recover debts also review terms and conditions, draft credit account application forms and personal guarantees, and suggest wording for reminder letters. Ask the firm whether this is something it offers and how it would charge. Better processes can reduce how often you need a solicitor in the first place.
How quickly will someone contact me?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, and often sooner. If the matter is urgent, for example because you think the customer is about to close down, say so on the form.
What is a letter before action?
It is a formal letter sent to a debtor before a court claim is issued. It sets out what is owed and why, gives a deadline for payment or a response and explains that court proceedings will follow if the matter is not resolved. It is often the first formal step in recovering a business debt and many debts are paid at this stage.
Is a letter before action the same as a letter of claim?
In debt recovery the two terms are often used to mean the same thing. Both describe the formal letter sent before court proceedings. Some people also call it a letter before claim or a pre-action letter. What matters is that the letter contains the right information and gives the debtor a fair chance to respond.
Do I have to send one before going to court?
Courts expect parties to exchange information and try to resolve matters before issuing a claim. There is no absolute bar on issuing without a letter, but doing so can lead to costs penalties or the claim being paused. In almost all debt cases, a solicitor will send a letter before action first.
Can I write the letter myself?
Businesses can send their own letters, and many send a final demand before involving a solicitor. A solicitor's letter often carries more weight and is more likely to meet the court's expectations, which matters if the case goes further. If the debtor is a sole trader, the letter must follow a specific protocol with set enclosures.
Is a letter from a solicitor more likely to get paid?
Often it is, though nothing is guaranteed. A letter on a law firm's letterhead shows that the creditor is prepared to take legal action and has taken advice. Customers who have ignored ordinary reminders frequently respond to a solicitor's letter by paying, proposing a plan or explaining a dispute that can then be dealt with.
Does sending a letter commit me to going to court?
No. A letter sets out what you intend to do if the debt is not paid, but you can still decide not to issue a claim. It is sensible not to threaten steps you have no intention of taking, so your solicitor will discuss your plans with you before the letter is sent.
Will a letter damage my relationship with the customer?
It can feel like a big step, especially with a customer you value. Many solicitors write in a firm but professional tone that leaves room to keep trading once the debt is paid. You can talk to your solicitor about the tone you want, and about whether a phone call from you first might help.
What should the letter include?
Most letters name the creditor and debtor accurately, explain how the debt arose, set out the amount owed including any interest and compensation, give a deadline and explain what will happen if the debt is not paid. Copies of key documents such as invoices and terms are often enclosed. Your solicitor will tailor the contents to your debt.
