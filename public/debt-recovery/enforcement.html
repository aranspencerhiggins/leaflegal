Enforcing a judgment explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Winning a judgment is a big step, but it is not the same as being paid. A judgment is an order from the court telling the debtor to pay. Many debtors do pay once judgment is entered, either straight away or by instalments. Some do not. Enforcement is the process of using the court's powers to turn an unpaid judgment into money.

There are several enforcement methods, and each works in a different way. Some take control of goods. Some freeze money held by a third party, such as a bank. Some secure the debt against property so that it is paid when the property is sold. Others require the debtor to explain its finances in court. Choosing the right method is often the difference between recovering the debt and spending more money without result.

No method works in every case. A company with valuable stock and equipment but no property might be best approached through enforcement agents. A company that owns its premises might be better suited to a charging order. A business with money passing through its bank account might be a candidate for a third party debt order. Information about the debtor is therefore at the heart of good enforcement.

This page is about enforcing judgments obtained by one business against another business in England and Wales. Leaflegal does not introduce firms for consumer debt collection.

Before any enforcement begins, it is worth checking whether the debtor has simply overlooked the judgment. A short letter reminding it of the judgment and the consequences of not paying, including registration on the public register and the costs of enforcement, sometimes produces payment without further expense. Your solicitor will judge whether this is worth trying first.

The main enforcement tools

  • Orders to obtain information about the debtor's finances
  • Writs and warrants of control, carried out by enforcement agents
  • Charging orders over land and property
  • Third party debt orders, often used to freeze bank accounts
  • Attachment of earnings orders, against individuals only
  • Insolvency routes, such as winding up or bankruptcy, in some cases

A solicitor who handles enforcement will look at your judgment, what is known about the debtor and how much is owed, and recommend the methods most likely to succeed.

Before choosing an enforcement method, it helps to know what the debtor owns. Enforcement is far more effective when it is aimed at assets that exist and can be reached. A solicitor can gather information in several ways.

Public records

For a company, the public register shows filed accounts, registered charges over its assets, its directors and its registered office. The Land Registry can show whether the debtor owns land or buildings in England and Wales. Searches of court and insolvency records can show other judgments or insolvency notices against the debtor.

What you already know

Your own records can be valuable. You might know where the debtor trades, which bank it uses from past payments, what vehicles or equipment it has, and who its customers are. Make a note of anything you know before speaking to your solicitor.

Order to obtain information

The court can order the debtor to attend and answer questions on oath about its finances. For a company, an officer such as a director is ordered to attend. They can also be told to bring documents, such as bank statements and accounts. Failing to comply with the order can have serious consequences for the person ordered to attend.

Investigators

For larger debts, some solicitors use specialist tracing or investigation agents to locate assets or people. Whether this is worthwhile depends on the amount owed and what is already known.

Keeping the information current

A debtor's position can change quickly. Property can be sold, accounts can be closed and equipment can be moved. Information that was accurate when the claim was issued may be out of date by the time of judgment, so your solicitor may refresh some searches before choosing a method.

Good information also helps you decide when to stop. If enquiries show that a debtor has no assets and no income, it may be better to write the debt off than to spend more trying to enforce it.

The most familiar enforcement method is sending enforcement agents to take control of the debtor's goods. Enforcement agents used to be called bailiffs. The process is governed by detailed rules on what they can and cannot do.

Writs and warrants of control

In the County Court, the creditor applies for a warrant of control, which is carried out by County Court enforcement agents. In the High Court, the equivalent is a writ of control, carried out by High Court Enforcement Officers and their agents. Some County Court judgments can be transferred up to the High Court for enforcement, and above a certain amount that is usually required. Your solicitor will explain which route is open to you.

What happens

The debtor is given formal notice before agents take any further step. Agents then visit the debtor's premises and can take control of goods belonging to the debtor. Often they agree a payment arrangement with the debtor, with goods left in place under a controlled goods agreement. If the debt is not paid, the goods can be removed and sold at auction.

Limits on what can be taken

Some goods are protected, and agents can only take goods that belong to the debtor. Items that are leased, on finance or owned by someone else cannot usually be taken. For a company, this can reduce what is available.

Fees

Enforcement agents charge fees at different stages, and these are usually added to the debt for the debtor to pay. The fee rules are set by regulations and change from time to time. Your solicitor will explain any upfront cost to you.

When it works best

This method often works well against businesses with valuable stock, equipment or vehicles, and against businesses that want to avoid the disruption of an agent's visit. It is less effective against a company with few physical assets or where most of its goods are leased.

Challenges by the debtor

A debtor can apply to the court to suspend a warrant or writ, for example by offering instalments. Third parties can also claim that goods taken belong to them rather than to the debtor. If this happens, your solicitor will explain the position and how to respond.

Enforcement agents are only one option. The court has several other tools that suit different situations.

Charging orders

A charging order secures the judgment debt against land or property owned by the debtor. The court first makes an interim order, and then decides whether to make it final. Once in place, the charge is registered against the property. The debt is usually paid when the property is sold or refinanced. In some cases, the creditor can apply to the court for an order for sale, though courts consider these carefully.

Third party debt orders

Third party debt orders are usually most effective when the creditor knows which bank the debtor uses and has some idea of when money is likely to be in the account, for example shortly after the debtor's own customers usually pay. An empty account at the moment of service means the order catches nothing.

A third party debt order is directed at someone who owes money to the debtor, most often its bank. An interim order freezes the money in the account, up to the amount of the judgment. If a final order is made, the bank pays that money to the creditor. The order only catches what is in the account when it is served, so timing matters. It can also be used against other third parties who owe the debtor money.

Attachment of earnings

An attachment of earnings order requires an employer to deduct money from a person's wages and pay it to the creditor. It is only available against individuals. It is rarely relevant to company debtors, but may occasionally help where a sole trader has taken up employment.

Insolvency routes

An unpaid judgment can be the basis for a statutory demand or, for a company, a winding up petition. For a sole trader, it may support a bankruptcy petition if the debt is high enough. These routes affect all creditors and carry their own risks, so they are usually considered carefully.

Combining methods

It is often possible to use more than one method, either together or one after another. For example, an order to obtain information might reveal a bank account that is then targeted with a third party debt order.

The right enforcement method depends on the debtor, the amount owed and what you want to achieve. Your solicitor will recommend an approach, but these are some of the questions that usually shape the choice.

What does the debtor own?

If the debtor owns property, a charging order may provide long term security. If it has stock or equipment, enforcement agents may be effective. If it has money moving through a bank account, a third party debt order may be worth trying. If little is known, an order to obtain information may be the first step.

How quickly do you need the money?

Some methods can produce money relatively quickly, such as a third party debt order that catches funds in an account. Others, such as charging orders, may give security but leave you waiting for the property to be sold.

How much is owed?

The cost of each method should be in proportion to the debt. Some methods are more expensive than others, and some debts are too small to justify more than one attempt.

Is the debtor still trading?

A trading business may have stock, income and a bank balance, and an incentive to pay to avoid disruption. A business that has stopped trading may have few assets left, and insolvency processes may be close. Your solicitor will check the debtor's status before recommending action.

Does the debtor have other creditors?

If the debtor is in serious financial trouble, there may be a race among creditors. If it enters an insolvency process, enforcement that has not been completed can be affected. Acting promptly can matter, but your solicitor will explain the risks.

Time limits

Judgments do not simply expire, but some enforcement methods need the court's permission if more than six years have passed since the judgment. Interest may also be affected by delay. Acting promptly after judgment usually gives the best chance of recovery.

Each enforcement method has its own court fees and legal costs. Some costs can usually be added to the judgment debt and recovered from the debtor if enforcement succeeds. If it does not, the creditor may bear them.

Court and enforcement fees

There are court fees for issuing warrants and writs, applying for charging orders and third party debt orders and requesting orders to obtain information. Enforcement agents have their own fees, many of which are added to the debt. These are set by government and change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask the firm.

Solicitor's fees

Many firms charge fixed fees for standard enforcement applications. Tracing work, contested applications and enforcement involving several methods may be charged by the hour or in stages. Ask for a written estimate before each step.

What you can recover

Fixed amounts of costs for many enforcement steps can be added to the judgment debt. You may not recover the full amount you spend. Your solicitor will explain what is likely to be recoverable for each method.

Spending wisely

A good solicitor will tell you if a method is unlikely to work, or if the debtor appears to have nothing to enforce against. That advice can save you from spending good money after bad.

Enforcing a judgment questions and answers

Which enforcement method is best?
It depends on the debtor’s assets. A solicitor will advise.
Can I enforce against a company’s property?
A charging order can secure the debt against property.
How long is a judgment valid?
Different rules apply to different enforcement methods. Act promptly.
Can you help with personal debts?
No. We only introduce businesses recovering debts owed by other businesses.
How much does debt recovery cost?
Many firms charge a fixed fee for a letter before action and agreed fees for court stages. They will explain their charges before you instruct them.
Do I need to be a limited company?
No. Sole traders, partnerships and companies can all recover business debts.
What does enforcing a judgment mean?
It means using the court's powers to make a debtor pay a judgment it has not paid voluntarily. There are several methods, including enforcement agents, charging orders over property, third party debt orders that freeze bank accounts and orders requiring the debtor to explain its finances. A solicitor will recommend the most suitable method.
When can I start enforcing?
Usually once the date for payment in the judgment has passed without payment, or once the debtor has missed an instalment the court ordered. If the judgment says the debt is payable immediately, enforcement can generally begin soon after. Your solicitor will check the terms of your judgment.
Do I need a solicitor to enforce?
Creditors can apply for some enforcement steps themselves, but choosing the right method and following the procedure correctly can be difficult. A solicitor can investigate the debtor, select the method most likely to work and deal with any challenge, which can save time and cost overall.
Will the debtor be told before enforcement?
It depends on the method. Enforcement agents must give the debtor formal notice before taking further steps. Some applications, such as interim third party debt orders and interim charging orders, are usually made without notice so that the debtor cannot move money or property first. Notice follows once the interim order is made.
What if the judgment was obtained in default?
A default judgment can be enforced in the same way as any other judgment. However, if the debtor applies to set it aside, enforcement may be paused while the court decides. Your solicitor will consider whether there is any risk of this before starting enforcement.
Can I enforce against a company that has gone into liquidation?
Generally not. Once a company is in liquidation, creditors usually cannot continue enforcement without the court's permission and must instead submit their claim to the liquidator. Enforcement that has not been completed before the liquidation may also be affected. Your solicitor will explain your position.
What if the debtor has moved premises?
Moving does not wipe out a judgment. Your solicitor can check the debtor's current registered office and any new trading address, and may use enquiries or an order to obtain information to find out where it now operates. Enforcement agents need an accurate address, so up to date information is important.
What is a warrant or writ of control?
It is an order that allows enforcement agents to take control of the debtor's goods and, if the debt is not paid, remove and sell them. A warrant is issued by the County Court and a writ by the High Court. Which applies depends on the court and the amount of the judgment.
What is the difference between County Court and High Court enforcement?
County Court warrants are carried out by County Court enforcement agents, while High Court writs are carried out by High Court Enforcement Officers. Some County Court judgments can be transferred up for High Court enforcement, and above a certain amount that is usually required. Your solicitor will explain which route suits your judgment.
What is an order to obtain information?
It is a court order requiring the debtor, or for a company one of its officers, to attend court and answer questions on oath about its finances, and to bring documents. The answers can show which assets are available and which enforcement method is most likely to work.
Can enforcement agents take anything they find?
No. They can only take goods that belong to the debtor, and some items are protected. Goods that are leased, on finance or owned by others cannot usually be taken. Agents must follow detailed rules on how and when they take control of goods.
Can I use more than one method?
Yes. Methods can often be used together or one after another. For example, information gained through an order to obtain information might lead to a third party debt order or a charging order. Your solicitor will plan the sequence that makes the most sense.
Can enforcement agents enter business premises?
Enforcement agents have powers to enter premises in certain circumstances, but those powers are limited by detailed rules. They usually visit during set hours and must follow the procedure laid down for taking control of goods. Your solicitor can explain how this works in practice.
Can the debtor stop enforcement agents?
A debtor can pay, agree a payment arrangement with the agents or apply to the court to suspend the warrant or writ, often by offering instalments. The court will look at the debtor's finances and decide what is fair. Your solicitor can respond to any application on your behalf.
Is an order to obtain information worth the cost?
It can be, especially where little is known about the debtor. The answers given on oath, and the documents produced, often show which assets exist and where. That helps avoid wasting money on methods that will not work. For a very small debt, though, the cost may outweigh the benefit. Your solicitor will give you a view.
What is a charging order?
A charging order secures the judgment debt against land or property owned by the debtor. It is made in two stages, interim and final, and is registered against the property. The debt is usually paid when the property is sold or refinanced. It can give long term security rather than immediate payment.
Can I force the sale of the property?
Once a final charging order is in place, a creditor can apply for an order for sale. Courts look carefully at these applications, especially where the property is a home. For business property owned by a company, the position may be different. Your solicitor will explain whether this is realistic.
What is a third party debt order?
It is an order directed at someone who owes money to the debtor, most often its bank. An interim order freezes funds in the account up to the judgment amount. If the order is made final, the money is paid to the creditor. It only catches money that is in the account at the time the interim order is served.
How do I know which bank the debtor uses?
You may already know from past payments or invoices. If not, an order to obtain information can require the debtor to disclose its bank details. Your solicitor may also be able to use other enquiries to identify accounts.
Can I get a charging order over a director's house?
Not for a company's debt, unless the director is personally liable, for example under a personal guarantee and a judgment has been obtained against them. A company is a separate legal person, and its debts are not normally the directors' debts.
What if the property already has a mortgage?
A charging order can still be obtained, but it ranks behind existing mortgages and charges. If the property is sold, those lenders are usually paid first and the charging order is paid from what is left. Your solicitor will look at the likely equity before recommending this method.
How long is a judgment valid for?
Judgments do not simply expire, but if more than six years have passed since the judgment, you need the court's permission to issue a writ or warrant of control. Other methods may also be affected by delay. It is generally best to enforce promptly after judgment.
Does interest continue after judgment?
In many cases, interest continues to run on a judgment debt until it is paid, though the rules depend on the court, the amount and the type of debt. Your solicitor will calculate any interest that can be added when enforcing.
How long does enforcement take?
It varies with the method. Enforcement agents can act within weeks of a warrant or writ being issued. Third party debt orders can freeze money quickly once granted. Charging orders can take longer and may leave you waiting for a sale. Your solicitor will give you an idea for your case.
What if the debtor asks to pay by instalments?
A debtor can ask the creditor, or sometimes the court, for time to pay. Accepting a sensible instalment plan can be the best outcome where the debtor cannot pay at once. Your solicitor can help agree terms and keep enforcement options open if payments stop.
Should I enforce quickly if the debtor is struggling?
Speed can matter where a debtor is in financial trouble, because enforcement that is not completed before an insolvency process starts may be affected. However, rushing into the wrong method wastes money. Your solicitor will balance the need to act quickly with choosing a method that is likely to work.
What happens once the judgment is paid?
Once the full amount, including any interest and recoverable costs, has been paid, enforcement stops. If the judgment was registered on the public register, the debtor can ask for it to be marked as satisfied. Your solicitor will confirm when the matter is closed and deal with any paperwork.
How much does enforcement cost?
Each method has its own court fees and legal costs. Many firms offer fixed fees for standard applications, with tracing and contested work charged separately. Enforcement agent fees are often added to the debt. Ask the firm for a written estimate for the method recommended.
Can I add enforcement costs to the debt?
Fixed amounts of costs for many enforcement steps can be added to the judgment debt, along with many enforcement agent fees. You may not recover everything you spend, especially if enforcement does not produce payment. Your solicitor will explain what is recoverable.
What if enforcement fails?
If a method does not produce payment, you may need to try another, or decide that the debt is not worth pursuing further. Costs spent on unsuccessful enforcement are often not recovered. Good investigation at the outset helps reduce this risk.
Will the firm tell me if the debt is not worth chasing?
A good firm should. If investigations show the debtor has no assets, no income and no realistic prospect of paying, your solicitor can explain that further enforcement is unlikely to succeed. The decision about whether to stop is yours, but honest advice at that point can prevent further losses.

What is late payment interest?
It is interest that a business can claim when another business pays it late. Under late payment law, a creditor can usually claim statutory interest at 8% a year above the Bank of England base rate, plus a fixed sum of compensation for each late invoice, unless the contract provides its own substantial remedy for late payment.
Does late payment law apply to my business?
It applies to contracts for the supply of goods or services where both parties are acting in the course of a business. That covers most trade between businesses, including sales to public bodies. It does not apply to sales to consumers. Your solicitor will check whether your contract is covered.
Do I have to tell the customer in advance?
No. The right to statutory interest arises under the law, so you do not need to have warned the customer or included it in your terms. Many businesses do mention it in their terms or on invoices, which can encourage prompt payment and avoid surprises.
Does it apply to sole traders?
Yes, as long as the sole trader bought the goods or services for their business. A sole trader buying for personal use would be a consumer, and late payment law would not apply. Your solicitor will consider how your customer was acting when the contract was made.
Can I claim against a large company or public body?
Yes. Late payment law applies regardless of the size of the customer, and public authorities are covered too. There are also rules on how long public authorities should take to pay. Your solicitor will explain how these rules affect your invoices.
Is claiming interest bad for customer relationships?
It can be, and some businesses choose not to claim with customers they value. Others find that knowing interest will be added encourages prompt payment. It is a commercial decision for your business. Your solicitor can tell you what is at stake so you can decide.
Do you help with consumer debts?
No. Leaflegal only introduces businesses that are owed money by other businesses in England and Wales. Late payment law does not apply to consumer contracts, and consumer debt collection is outside this service.
How is statutory interest calculated?
It is simple interest at 8% a year above the Bank of England base rate, calculated on the unpaid amount for each day the payment is late. The base rate used is fixed for six month periods. Your solicitor will calculate the total and the daily rate that continues until payment.

More questions people ask

How long should I wait before using a solicitor?
There is no fixed rule. Many businesses pass a debt to a solicitor once it is clearly overdue and their own reminders have been ignored or met with empty promises. Waiting a long time can make recovery harder, especially if the customer is struggling. A solicitor can explain the options at any stage.
Will a solicitor's letter make a difference?
Often it does. A letter on a law firm's letterhead shows the customer that you are prepared to take the matter further and sets a clear deadline. Many customers who have ignored ordinary reminders pay or make an offer once a formal letter arrives, though there is no guarantee.
Can I accept a payment plan?
You can agree to accept instalments if you wish. It helps to record the plan in writing, including what happens if a payment is missed. A solicitor can draft a short agreement that makes it easier to take action quickly if the customer falls behind.
The customer paid part of the invoice. What now?
A part payment reduces the debt but does not usually settle it, unless you have agreed to accept less in full settlement. A part payment can also restart the time limit for bringing a claim. A solicitor will work out what remains due and whether interest can be added to the balance.
Can I chase invoices that are several years old?
Possibly. A claim for a contract debt must usually be started within six years of the date it fell due, though a written acknowledgement or a part payment can restart the clock. Older debts can be harder to prove, so gather whatever records you have and speak to a solicitor promptly.
Can I stop supplying the customer until they pay?
That depends on your contract. Some terms allow a supplier to suspend deliveries or work while invoices are overdue, while others do not. Stopping supply without a right to do so could cause problems. A solicitor can check your terms before you decide.
What if the customer promises to pay but never does?
Repeated promises without payment are common. It is worth keeping a note of each promise and asking for it in writing. If a deadline set in writing passes without payment, a formal letter from a solicitor is often the next step.
Do I need to send a letter before issuing a claim?
Courts expect parties to exchange information and try to settle before a claim is issued. Skipping this step can lead to costs penalties, even if you win. A solicitor will usually send a letter before action that meets these expectations, and will explain the deadline that applies to your customer.
