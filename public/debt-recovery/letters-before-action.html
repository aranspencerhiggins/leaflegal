Letter before action in brief

A letter before action, also called a letter before claim, is a formal demand that warns the debtor that court action will follow if they do not pay. It must follow the pre action rules.

A letter before action on a solicitor’s letterhead often gets a debt paid without going further. Many firms send one for a fixed fee.

Letters before action explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A letter before action is a formal written demand sent to a debtor before court proceedings begin. It explains what is owed, why it is owed and what will happen if it is not paid by a set date. In a business debt, it is usually the first step that moves a matter from ordinary credit control into a legal process.

You may also hear it called a letter of claim, a letter before claim or a pre-action letter. The name matters less than what it contains and how it is sent. A good letter is clear, accurate and fair. It gives the debtor a genuine chance to pay or to explain why it disputes the debt, and it makes clear that you are prepared to go to court if needed.

For many businesses, the letter is where the matter ends. A customer who has ignored polite reminders often pays, or offers a payment plan, once a letter arrives from a law firm. That is not guaranteed, but the letter often changes the tone of the conversation. It shows the debtor that the creditor is organised and serious.

The letter also has a legal role. Courts in England and Wales expect parties to exchange key information and try to settle before a claim is issued. A creditor who goes straight to court without a proper letter can face penalties on costs, even if it wins. A well drafted letter protects the creditor's position if the matter does go further.

This page covers letters sent by one business to another business that owes it money, in England and Wales. Leaflegal does not introduce firms for consumer debt collection.

Timing also matters. A letter sent soon after a debt falls overdue gives a better chance of payment than one sent many months later, when the debtor may have other pressing creditors. Sending a letter promptly also keeps the matter moving, so that if a claim is needed, the debt is still fresh and the evidence is easy to gather.

  • Any reply can be dealt with by someone who knows the next steps

There is no single fixed form for a letter before action between businesses, but a good one usually covers the same ground. Your solicitor will decide exactly what to include. These are the parts most letters contain.

Who is owed and who owes

The letter names the creditor and the debtor accurately. If the debtor is a company, that means its full registered name, and the letter is often sent to its registered office as well as its trading address. Getting this right at the start avoids problems if a claim is later issued.

The basis of the debt

The letter explains how the debt arose. It might refer to the contract, the order, the dates the goods were delivered or the work was carried out, and the invoices that were raised. Copies of key documents, such as the invoices and the terms, are often enclosed.

The amount

The letter sets out the sum owed, including any part payments received. It usually shows separately any interest being claimed and how it has been worked out, and any fixed compensation or recovery costs claimed under late payment law or the contract.

A deadline

The letter gives a date by which the debtor must pay or reply. The length of that period depends on who the debtor is, and the rules differ for individuals, including sole traders.

What happens next

The letter explains that the creditor intends to issue proceedings if the debt is not paid or a satisfactory response is not received. It may mention that the creditor will seek interest and costs from the court.

An invitation to settle

Courts expect parties to consider ways of resolving a dispute without a trial. A letter often invites the debtor to propose payment, to explain any dispute, or to consider other ways of settling, such as negotiation or mediation.

Tone

A letter before action does not need to be aggressive to be effective. The strongest letters are usually calm, precise and factual. They state the position clearly, show that the creditor has its paperwork in order and leave the debtor in no doubt about what will happen next. Overstated or emotional language can make a letter easier to dismiss and may look poor if the court later reads it.

The letter may well be read by a judge one day, so it is written with that in mind. Your solicitor will keep to the facts and avoid anything that could be seen as unfair pressure.

Letters before action are shaped by the court rules in England and Wales. Your solicitor will make sure the letter meets the rules that apply to your debtor. This is a general outline of how those rules work.

Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct

Where the debtor is a company or another organisation, the main guidance is the Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct and Protocols. It expects the parties to exchange enough information to understand each other's position, to try to settle, and to consider alternative ways of resolving the matter. The creditor should give the debtor a reasonable time to respond. In straightforward cases between businesses, that is often around 14 days.

Pre-Action Protocol for Debt Claims

Where the debtor is an individual, a more detailed protocol applies. That includes sole traders, even when the debt arose from their business. The protocol sets out what the letter must include and what documents must be sent with it, and it gives the debtor a longer period to respond.

Why it matters

If a creditor does not follow the expected steps, the court can take that into account when deciding costs. It might reduce the costs the creditor recovers, order the creditor to pay some of the debtor's costs or pause the claim so the steps can be taken. A careful letter avoids these risks.

Fair and accurate

A letter before action should be firm but accurate. It should not claim sums that are not due or threaten steps that are not available. For example, threatening insolvency proceedings over a debt that is genuinely disputed can backfire. Your solicitor will make sure the letter says what can properly be said.

Documents the debtor asks for

The pre-action rules expect each side to share key documents that the other reasonably needs to understand the claim. If a debtor asks for a copy of the contract, a delivery note or a statement of account, it is usually sensible to provide it. Refusing a reasonable request can count against a party later when the court decides costs.

Many businesses supply sole traders, such as builders, consultants or shop owners trading in their own name. Even though the debt is a business debt, the sole trader is an individual in law, and that changes how the letter must be written.

The debt protocol applies

The Pre-Action Protocol for Debt Claims applies to any claim by a business against an individual, including a sole trader. The letter must contain set information, including the amount, whether interest or other charges are being added and how payment can be made.

Enclosures

The letter must usually be sent with an information sheet, a reply form and a financial statement form. These help the debtor explain whether they accept the debt, whether they need time to get advice, and what they can afford to pay. The forms are published with the protocol.

A longer deadline

An individual debtor is usually given 30 days to reply. If they say they are getting debt advice, the creditor is expected to allow a reasonable extra period before starting a claim. If they dispute the debt, the creditor is expected to engage with that before going to court.

Partnerships and LLPs

A traditional partnership is made up of its partners, who can be personally liable for its debts, while a limited liability partnership is a separate legal body more like a company. The right approach to the letter can differ. Your solicitor will check what type of business your debtor is before sending anything.

Once the deadline passes, there are several possible outcomes. Your solicitor will discuss the next step with you, and nothing further usually happens without your agreement.

The debtor pays

This is the outcome most creditors hope for. If the full amount is paid, including any interest and compensation claimed, the matter is closed.

The debtor offers a payment plan

If the debtor asks for time, the creditor can choose whether to accept. A solicitor can set out the plan in a short written agreement, so that if payments stop, it is easier to move to the next stage.

The debtor disputes the debt

If the debtor replies with a reason for not paying, the solicitor will review it. Some replies raise real issues that need to be dealt with. Others are general complaints that do not stand up. The solicitor will explain how the response affects the options and the likely cost.

The debtor does nothing

If there is no response, the creditor can usually issue a court claim. If the debtor still does not respond to the claim, the creditor can often ask for judgment without a hearing.

Other routes

Where the debt is clearly not disputed and the debtor is a company, some creditors consider a statutory demand instead of, or as well as, a court claim. This is a more aggressive step with its own risks, and a solicitor will explain whether it is suitable.

Keeping the file ready

Whatever the outcome, the work done for the letter is not wasted. The solicitor will already have checked the debtor's details, the amount owed and the interest calculation, and will have the key documents on file. If a claim is needed, it can usually be issued without starting again from scratch.

A letter before action is usually one of the least expensive steps in debt recovery. Many firms offer it for a fixed fee, which often includes reviewing the invoices and terms, checking the debtor's details and preparing and sending the letter.

What may cost more

Further work, such as dealing with a detailed response, negotiating a settlement or reviewing a complex contract, may be charged separately. Ask the firm exactly what its fixed fee covers and what would be extra.

Adding charges to the debt

Between businesses, a creditor can often claim interest and a fixed sum of compensation on late invoices under late payment law, unless the contract provides a different remedy. In some cases reasonable costs of recovery can be claimed as well. A solicitor will work out what can properly be included in the letter.

Value for money

Because so many debts are paid after a formal letter, it can be a cost effective step even for modest sums. A solicitor will give you an honest view if the amount owed is so small that even a letter may not make sense.

Letters before action questions and answers

Is a solicitor’s letter more effective?
It often is, because it shows you are prepared to go to court.
How long does the debtor have to respond?
It depends on who the debtor is. The solicitor will set the right deadline.
Can I add interest?
Often, under your contract or late payment law.
Can you help with personal debts?
No. We only introduce businesses recovering debts owed by other businesses.
How much does debt recovery cost?
Many firms charge a fixed fee for a letter before action and agreed fees for court stages. They will explain their charges before you instruct them.
Do I need to be a limited company?
No. Sole traders, partnerships and companies can all recover business debts.
What is a letter before action?
It is a formal letter sent to a debtor before a court claim is issued. It sets out what is owed and why, gives a deadline for payment or a response and explains that court proceedings will follow if the matter is not resolved. It is often the first formal step in recovering a business debt and many debts are paid at this stage.
Is a letter before action the same as a letter of claim?
In debt recovery the two terms are often used to mean the same thing. Both describe the formal letter sent before court proceedings. Some people also call it a letter before claim or a pre-action letter. What matters is that the letter contains the right information and gives the debtor a fair chance to respond.
Do I have to send one before going to court?
Courts expect parties to exchange information and try to resolve matters before issuing a claim. There is no absolute bar on issuing without a letter, but doing so can lead to costs penalties or the claim being paused. In almost all debt cases, a solicitor will send a letter before action first.
Can I write the letter myself?
Businesses can send their own letters, and many send a final demand before involving a solicitor. A solicitor's letter often carries more weight and is more likely to meet the court's expectations, which matters if the case goes further. If the debtor is a sole trader, the letter must follow a specific protocol with set enclosures.
Is a letter from a solicitor more likely to get paid?
Often it is, though nothing is guaranteed. A letter on a law firm's letterhead shows that the creditor is prepared to take legal action and has taken advice. Customers who have ignored ordinary reminders frequently respond to a solicitor's letter by paying, proposing a plan or explaining a dispute that can then be dealt with.
Does sending a letter commit me to going to court?
No. A letter sets out what you intend to do if the debt is not paid, but you can still decide not to issue a claim. It is sensible not to threaten steps you have no intention of taking, so your solicitor will discuss your plans with you before the letter is sent.
Will a letter damage my relationship with the customer?
It can feel like a big step, especially with a customer you value. Many solicitors write in a firm but professional tone that leaves room to keep trading once the debt is paid. You can talk to your solicitor about the tone you want, and about whether a phone call from you first might help.
What should the letter include?
Most letters name the creditor and debtor accurately, explain how the debt arose, set out the amount owed including any interest and compensation, give a deadline and explain what will happen if the debt is not paid. Copies of key documents such as invoices and terms are often enclosed. Your solicitor will tailor the contents to your debt.
Who should the letter be addressed to?
For a company, the letter is usually addressed to the company itself at its registered office, and often also sent to its trading address or to the person you have been dealing with. For a sole trader, it goes to them personally. Getting the right legal name and address helps avoid arguments later.
Can I add interest in the letter?
Often, yes. If your contract sets a rate of interest for late payment, that rate usually applies. If it does not, statutory interest under late payment law can usually be claimed on debts between businesses. Your solicitor will calculate the interest and show how it has been worked out.
Can I add compensation for the cost of chasing?
Between businesses, late payment law usually allows a fixed sum of compensation for each late invoice, with the amount depending on the size of the debt. In some cases reasonable recovery costs above that can also be claimed. Your solicitor will check whether your contract affects this and include what can properly be claimed.
Should the letter mention insolvency?
Only where it is appropriate. Threatening a winding up petition or bankruptcy over a debt that is genuinely disputed can be treated as improper and can lead to costs being awarded against the creditor. A solicitor will decide whether any mention of insolvency routes is suitable in your case.
What if I am not sure exactly how much is owed?
Accuracy matters, so it is worth reconciling your account before the letter goes. Check which invoices are outstanding, any part payments and any credit notes. Your solicitor can help you prepare a statement of account. Claiming the wrong amount can weaken your position and give the debtor a reason to delay.
Will the letter include my terms and conditions?
If your terms are relevant, for example because they set the payment date or the interest rate, the solicitor will usually refer to them and may enclose a copy. It helps to show how the terms became part of the contract, such as a signed account form or a link on an accepted quote.
How is the letter sent?
Letters are commonly sent by post to the debtor's registered office or business address, and often by email as well so that it arrives quickly. Your solicitor will keep a record of how and when it was sent. If your contract says how formal notices must be given, the solicitor will check and follow that too.
How long does the debtor have to respond?
It depends on who the debtor is. For a company or other organisation, a reasonable period is expected, often around 14 days in a straightforward debt. Where the debtor is an individual, including a sole trader, the debt protocol usually gives them 30 days. Your solicitor will set the right deadline.
What if the debtor asks for more time?
A creditor can decide whether to agree. If the debtor is an individual who says they are getting debt advice, the protocol expects the creditor to allow a reasonable extra period. For companies, a short extension can sometimes lead to payment. Your solicitor can advise on how to respond in your case.
Can I issue a claim before the deadline ends?
Generally not, unless there is a good reason such as a limitation deadline or a real risk that assets will disappear. Issuing early can undermine the purpose of the letter and may affect costs. If time is short, tell your solicitor so they can plan accordingly.
Does sending a letter stop the limitation clock?
No. Only issuing a claim at court stops time running for limitation purposes. A written acknowledgement of the debt by the debtor, or a part payment, can restart the clock. If your debt is close to six years old, tell your solicitor straight away.
Is the deadline counted in working days or calendar days?
Letters before action usually give a period in days and a specific date, so there is no confusion. Your solicitor will choose a date that allows the period expected by the rules, taking into account how and when the letter will be delivered. A clear date in the letter avoids arguments about when the deadline ended.
What happens if the debtor pays?
If the debtor pays everything claimed, including any interest and compensation, the matter ends there. If they pay only the invoice amount, you can decide whether to pursue the rest. Your solicitor will confirm receipt and close the file or discuss the balance with you.
What if the debtor disputes the debt?
Your solicitor will review the reasons given. Some disputes are genuine and need to be resolved, perhaps through negotiation. Others are not supported by the evidence. The solicitor will explain how the dispute affects your options, including whether a court claim is still the right next step.
What if the debtor offers to pay in instalments?
You can choose whether to accept. If you do, it helps to record the plan in writing, setting out the amounts, the dates and what happens if a payment is missed. Your solicitor can prepare a short agreement so that you can act quickly if the debtor falls behind.
What if the debtor ignores the letter?
If the deadline passes with no reply, the creditor can usually issue a court claim. If the debtor ignores that too, the creditor can often ask the court for judgment without a hearing. Your solicitor will discuss the cost of issuing a claim before taking that step.
The debtor's solicitor has replied. What now?
A reply from a solicitor usually means the debtor is taking the matter seriously. It may raise a dispute, propose settlement or ask for documents. Your solicitor will review the reply, explain what it means and suggest how to respond.
What does without prejudice mean in a reply?
Letters marked without prejudice are usually genuine attempts to settle, and in general they cannot be shown to the court as evidence of an admission. This encourages open negotiation. Your solicitor will explain how any such letter fits into the wider picture.
Can the debtor ask for documents before paying?
Yes. Under the pre-action rules, each side is expected to provide key documents the other reasonably needs. A debtor might ask for a copy of the contract, the invoices or proof of delivery. Providing them promptly usually helps move matters on, and your solicitor will manage this exchange.
What if the debtor says it has already paid?
Mistakes happen on both sides, so it is worth checking. Ask for the date, amount and reference of the payment and compare it with your bank records. If the payment was sent to the wrong account or applied to a different invoice, the solicitor can help sort it out. If no payment can be traced, the claim continues.
How much does a solicitor's letter cost?
Many firms charge a fixed fee for a letter before action, which usually includes reviewing your documents and checking the debtor's details. Further work, such as dealing with a detailed response, may cost extra. Ask the firm what its fee includes before you instruct.
Can I get the cost of the letter back?
Between businesses, late payment law may allow you to claim fixed compensation and, in some cases, reasonable recovery costs, which can help cover the cost of the letter. If a claim later succeeds, some legal costs may be recoverable, but often only fixed amounts. Your solicitor will explain.
Can I send letters for several debts at once?
Yes. If you have several overdue customers, many firms will prepare letters for each of them, often at a fixed fee per letter. Some firms offer online systems for sending debts in bulk and tracking replies. Ask the firm about this if your business regularly has overdue accounts to chase.

No. Leaflegal is free for businesses to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, which does not change what you pay them.
Do I need to send a letter before issuing a claim?
Courts expect parties to exchange information and try to settle before a claim is issued. Going straight to court without a proper letter can lead to costs penalties or a pause in the claim. In almost every debt case, a solicitor will make sure a letter before action has been sent and the deadline has passed before issuing.
Is it worth suing if the debtor has no money?
A judgment is only valuable if the debtor can pay or has assets that can be reached. Before issuing, many solicitors check public records such as filed accounts, existing judgments and insolvency notices. If a debtor appears to have nothing, a solicitor may advise that a claim is unlikely to be worthwhile, which can save you money.
Which court will my claim be in?
Most business debt claims start in the County Court, often through a central online service, and may move to a local hearing centre if defended. Higher value or more complex claims can be started in the High Court. Your solicitor will choose the right court based on the amount, the complexity and any enforcement plans.
Can I bring a claim myself?
Businesses can bring claims without a solicitor, and some do for small debts. A solicitor can help make sure the claim is against the right party, the amount and interest are correct and the procedure is followed. That reduces the risk of delay or costs penalties, especially if the debtor defends.
How long do I have to start a claim?
For a debt under a contract, a claim must usually be issued within six years of the date payment fell due. A written acknowledgement of the debt by the debtor, or a part payment, can restart the clock. If your debt is approaching six years old, speak to a solicitor promptly.
Can I claim against more than one party?
Sometimes. For example, a creditor might claim against a company and against a director who signed a personal guarantee. Claims against several parties can be more complex and may not be suitable for the simplest online route. Your solicitor will explain who can be named and how.
What if the debtor is based outside England and Wales?
Claims against debtors based elsewhere can be more complex. Different rules apply to serving the claim and to enforcing a judgment abroad, and sometimes the contract says which courts should deal with disputes. Your solicitor will check your contract and explain whether a claim in England and Wales is the right approach.
What goes into a claim?
A claim usually includes a claim form and particulars of claim. Together they identify the parties, explain the contract and the debt, set out the amount owed and any interest claimed, and include a statement of truth. Your solicitor will draft these from your invoices, terms and correspondence.
