Everything you need to know

Pick a topic to read more.

Our guides explain in plain English how common legal matters usually work in England and Wales, and how working with a regulated immigration adviser usually goes. They are written for people who have never dealt with a solicitor before, as well as for those who have and want a refresher. Each guide covers what happens, roughly in what order, what tends to affect the time and cost, and what you might want to have ready.

The guides are general information, not legal advice. They describe how things usually work, not what will happen in your case or what you should do. Every situation is different, and small details can change the answer. When you need to know where you stand, a solicitor or regulated adviser will look at your own circumstances and explain your options.

Who the guides are for

  • People about to buy, sell or remortgage a home
  • Leaseholders wondering what a short lease means for them
  • People starting to think about separation or divorce
  • Drivers who have received a notice or court papers
  • Business owners chasing unpaid invoices or applying for a licence
  • Anyone preparing to speak to a regulated immigration adviser

Property matters come with a lot of specialist language. These are some of the words you are most likely to meet when buying, selling or extending a lease in England and Wales.

  • Conveyancing. The legal work involved in transferring ownership of a property from one person to another.
  • Conveyancer. A solicitor or licensed conveyancer who carries out conveyancing.
  • Title. The legal ownership of a property. The title register at HM Land Registry shows who owns it and any rights or restrictions affecting it.
  • Freehold. Ownership of the property and the land it stands on with no time limit.
  • Leasehold. Ownership of a property for a fixed number of years under a lease, usually from a freeholder or landlord.
  • Searches. Enquiries made with the local authority and other bodies about things that could affect a property, such as planning, roads, drainage and environmental issues.
  • Exchange of contracts. The point at which the buyer and seller become legally bound to complete the sale.
  • Completion. The day the money is transferred, ownership passes and the buyer usually gets the keys.
  • Deposit. The sum paid by the buyer on exchange, held until completion.
  • Disbursements. Costs paid by the firm to others on your behalf, such as search fees and Land Registry fees.
  • Stamp Duty Land Tax. A tax that may be payable on buying property in England. In Wales, Land Transaction Tax applies instead. Rates change, so check GOV.UK.
  • Lender's panel. The list of firms a mortgage lender will allow to act for it on a purchase or remortgage.
  • Transfer of equity. Adding someone to, or removing someone from, the legal ownership of a property.
  • Ground rent. A regular payment some leaseholders make to the freeholder under the lease.
  • Service charge. A contribution leaseholders pay towards the costs of maintaining and managing a building.
  • Lease extension. Adding years to an existing lease, either by agreement or through the statutory process.
  • Premium. The price paid to the freeholder for a lease extension or for the freehold.
  • Collective enfranchisement. The process by which qualifying flat owners join together to buy the freehold of their building.
  • Memorandum of sale. A document, usually from the estate agent, confirming the agreed price and the details of the buyer, seller and their conveyancers.
  • Mortgage offer. The lender's formal written offer to lend, with the conditions that apply.
  • Chain. A series of linked sales and purchases that depend on each other to complete.
  • Management pack. Information about a leasehold building provided by the landlord or managing agent, such as service charge accounts and building insurance.

Family law has its own vocabulary, and some older terms are still used even though the law has changed. These are some of the words you may hear if you are separating or arranging things for children.

  • Applicant. The person who starts a divorce, dissolution or court application. In a joint application there are two applicants.
  • Respondent. The other person in a sole application.
  • Conditional order. The first of two court orders in a divorce or dissolution. It used to be called a decree nisi.
  • Final order. The order that legally ends the marriage or civil partnership. It used to be called a decree absolute.
  • Reflection period. The minimum period of 20 weeks between the start of the divorce application and applying for the conditional order.
  • Financial remedy. The court process for dealing with money, property and pensions when a marriage or civil partnership ends. It used to be called ancillary relief.
  • Consent order. A court order that records a financial agreement reached between the couple, approved by a judge.
  • Disclosure. Each person giving full and frank information about their finances, usually on a standard court form.
  • Clean break. An arrangement where ongoing financial claims between former partners are brought to an end.
  • Pension sharing order. An order that divides a pension between former spouses or civil partners.
  • Spousal maintenance. Regular payments from one former partner to the other.
  • Child arrangements order. A court order setting out who a child lives with and spends time with.
  • MIAM. A mediation information and assessment meeting, which most people must attend before applying to court about finances or children, unless an exemption applies.
  • Cohabitation. Living together as a couple without being married or in a civil partnership.
  • Prenuptial agreement. An agreement made before marriage about how finances would be dealt with if the marriage ended.

These terms come up in motoring cases, licensing matters, business debt recovery and in court generally.

Motoring

  • Notice of intended prosecution. A notice telling the registered keeper or driver that a prosecution for certain offences is being considered.
  • Single justice procedure. A process where a single magistrate deals with some minor offences on the papers, without a hearing in open court.
  • Penalty points. Points added to a driving record when someone is convicted of certain motoring offences.
  • Totting up. The rule under which a driver who reaches 12 or more points within three years usually faces a ban.
  • Exceptional hardship. An argument that a totting up ban should not be imposed, or should be shortened, because of the hardship it would cause.
  • Special reasons. Circumstances connected with the offence itself that the court may consider when deciding whether to endorse points or impose a ban.

Licensing

  • Premises licence. A licence from the local council that allows premises to carry on licensable activities such as selling alcohol.
  • Personal licence. A licence held by an individual that allows them to authorise the sale of alcohol.
  • Designated premises supervisor. The personal licence holder named on a premises licence as responsible for alcohol sales at those premises.
  • Responsible authorities. Bodies such as the police and environmental health that can comment on licence applications and ask for reviews.
  • Licence review. A process where the council reconsiders a premises licence, often after concerns are raised.

Debt recovery and court

  • Letter before action. A formal letter setting out a claim and giving the other side a chance to respond before court proceedings start.
  • Claimant. The person or business that brings a court claim.
  • Defendant. The person or business a claim is brought against.
  • County court judgment. A court decision that a sum of money is owed.
  • Enforcement. Steps taken to recover money after a judgment, such as instructing enforcement agents.
  • Statutory demand. A formal demand for an undisputed debt that can be a step towards winding up or bankruptcy proceedings.
  • Limitation period. The time limit for bringing a claim. For most simple debts it is six years.

General court terms

  • Hearing. A session in court or before a tribunal where a judge, magistrates or a panel deal with a case.
  • Adjournment. When a hearing is postponed to a later date.
  • Plea. A defendant's answer to a criminal charge, either guilty or not guilty.
  • Mitigation. Information put to the court to explain the circumstances and ask for a lower sentence.
  • Costs. The legal costs of a case. In some cases the court can order one side to pay some of the other side's costs.
  • Litigation. The process of taking a dispute through the courts.

Guides are most useful when you use them to prepare, not to decide. Here are a few ways to get the most from them.

Read before your first call

Reading the guide for your type of matter before you speak to a lawyer can help you follow the conversation and ask better questions. You will already know the main stages, so the lawyer can spend more time on your own situation.

Write down your questions

As you read, note anything that seems to apply to you or that you do not understand. Those are good questions to take to the first call. A lawyer would much rather answer a question at the start than sort out a misunderstanding later.

Do not rely on general rules

Guides describe the usual position. Many legal rules have exceptions, and the facts of your case may mean something different applies. Deadlines in particular can depend on the exact dates and documents involved. Always check with a regulated lawyer before acting on anything you read.

Check the date and the source

Laws, procedures and official fees change. We review our guides regularly, and where figures change often we point you to GOV.UK rather than quoting them. If you read something elsewhere, check when it was written and whether it applies to England and Wales.

When to stop reading and get help

If you have received a court document, a letter with a deadline, or anything from the police, the council or the Home Office, it is usually better to speak to a lawyer or regulated adviser soon rather than keep researching. The same is true if a lot of money, your home, your children or your right to stay in the UK is involved.

Keep your own notes

It can help to keep a simple timeline of your matter, with dates, who said what and copies of any letters. Many people find that writing things down also makes the situation feel more manageable. Share the timeline with your lawyer on the first call. It saves time and can stop important details being missed.

Questions and answers

Are the guides legal advice?
No. The guides are general information about how things usually work in England and Wales, or for immigration, how working with a regulated adviser usually goes. They do not take account of your circumstances and they do not tell you what to do. A solicitor or regulated adviser can advise you on your own situation.
Who writes the guides?
The guides are written by Leaflegal, using official sources such as GOV.UK and the websites of the courts and regulators. Leaflegal is not a law firm, so the guides are not advice from a firm acting for you. We review them regularly and update them when the law or procedures change.
How often are the guides updated?
We review the guides regularly and update them when something important changes, such as a new law coming into force or a change to a court procedure. Where details change often, such as fees and tax rates, we do not quote figures and instead point you to GOV.UK or the firm.
Do the guides apply in Scotland and Northern Ireland?
Most guides cover the law of England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own legal systems, with different rules for property, family matters and courts. The immigration guides apply across the UK, because immigration law is the same throughout the UK.
Can I suggest a guide?
Yes. If there is a question you think we should answer, email us. We focus on the eight areas we cover, so we may not be able to write about every topic, but suggestions help us understand what people want to know.
Can I share or print the guides?
You are welcome to share links to our guides and to print them for your own use. Please do not republish them elsewhere. Remember that they are general information, so anyone you share them with should also speak to a lawyer about their own situation.
Why do solicitors use so much jargon?
Many legal words have precise meanings that everyday words do not capture, and some are set out in law or court rules. A good solicitor will explain any term you do not understand. If they do not, it is always fine to ask them to say it in plain English.
What is the difference between a solicitor and a barrister?
Solicitors usually deal directly with clients, handle the paperwork and run the matter day to day. Barristers are specialists in advocacy and advice, and are often instructed by solicitors to represent clients at hearings or give an opinion on difficult points. Both are regulated professionals.
What is a legal executive?
A chartered legal executive is a qualified lawyer, regulated by CILEx Regulation, who has trained in a specific area of law. Many work in firms alongside solicitors, handling matters such as conveyancing, family law or litigation. They can be just as experienced as solicitors in their area.
What does without prejudice mean?
Without prejudice usually marks a letter or discussion as a genuine attempt to settle a dispute, which generally cannot be shown to the court as evidence. It encourages people to negotiate openly. There are exceptions, so your solicitor will explain how it applies in your matter.
What is a fixed fee?
A fixed fee is an agreed price for a defined piece of work, such as a house purchase or an uncontested divorce. It usually does not include disbursements or VAT unless the firm says so. Ask the firm exactly what the fixed fee covers and what might be charged on top.
What is money on account?
Money on account is a payment made to a firm before or during a matter, held towards future fees or disbursements. The firm keeps it in a client account and uses it as work is done or costs are incurred. The firm will explain how much it needs and when.
What does it mean to instruct a solicitor?
Instructing a solicitor means formally asking them to act for you. It usually happens when you agree to the firm's terms of business and the firm has completed its identity checks. Until you instruct a firm, it is not acting for you.
What is a client care letter?
A client care letter is the letter a firm sends when you instruct it. It explains who will handle your matter, how fees and billing work, what the firm will do for you and how to complain. If you were introduced by Leaflegal, a solicitor will also mention the introduction fee there.
What is a disbursement?
A disbursement is a cost a firm pays to someone else on your behalf, such as a court fee, a search fee, a Land Registry fee or a barrister's fee. Disbursements are usually listed separately from the firm's own fee, so check whether a quote includes them.
Which guide should I read first?
Start with the guide or area page that matches your situation most closely. If you are not sure, the area pages give a broad overview and link to specific matters. If your situation involves more than one area, it is worth reading both, as they often overlap.
I have a deadline. Should I read the guides?
If you have a court date, a letter with a deadline or anything from the police, council or Home Office, it is usually better to speak to a lawyer or regulated adviser soon. The guides can help you understand the process, but they cannot tell you how to meet your deadline.
The guide does not match my situation
That is quite possible. Guides describe the usual position and cannot cover every case. If your situation seems different, a lawyer can explain how the rules apply to you. Make a note of what seems different and mention it on your first call.
Can the guides help me prepare for a first call?
Yes. Reading the relevant guide can help you understand the main stages, think about the key dates and gather the right documents. It also helps you write down questions about fees, timescales and who will handle your matter.
Do you have guides for every area you cover?
We are adding guides over time. At the moment there are guides on conveyancing, leasehold, divorce, immigration advisers and motoring hearings. Each of our eight areas also has its own page with detailed general information and answers to common questions.
Are the guides suitable for businesses?
Yes. The guides on debt recovery, licensing and property are relevant to many businesses, and the area pages explain how things usually work for business clients. As with individuals, a solicitor will look at your business's own situation before advising.
How do I speak to a solicitor about my matter?
Fill in our short form and we will introduce you to one regulated firm that handles your type of matter and covers your area. The firm will contact you, usually the same working day, to talk it through. Leaflegal is free to use, and the firm pays us for the introduction.
Can Leaflegal answer my question about a guide?
We can help with questions about the guides themselves, such as finding the right one. We cannot tell you how a guide applies to your situation, because Leaflegal does not give legal advice or immigration advice. The firm we introduce can answer those questions.
What if I think something in a guide is wrong?
Please email us and tell us which guide and which part. We take accuracy seriously and will check it against official sources. If something needs correcting, we will update the guide as soon as we can.
Where else can I find reliable information?
GOV.UK has official information on many legal processes, fees and forms. The websites of the courts and tribunals service, the SRA, the Law Society and Citizens Advice are also useful. Be cautious with forums and social media, which may be out of date or about a different legal system.
Do you have guides about immigration rules?
No. Leaflegal does not give immigration advice, so our immigration guides do not explain rules or say which route might apply to anyone. They cover how to choose a regulated adviser and how a first consultation usually goes. A regulated adviser can explain the rules for your situation.
Can I get free legal advice anywhere?
Leaflegal does not give legal advice. Citizens Advice, local law centres and some university law clinics offer free help on certain topics. Legal aid is available for some matters, depending on your circumstances, and GOV.UK has a checker. Some firms also offer a short free initial call.
Is it worth reading guides if I already have a solicitor?
It can be. Understanding the main stages and terms can help you follow what your solicitor is doing and ask better questions. If something in a guide seems different from what is happening in your matter, ask your solicitor. There may be a good reason for the difference.
Will reading the guides save me money?
It might help you use your solicitor's time well, for example by having documents ready and questions prepared. But guides cannot replace advice on your situation, and acting on general information alone can sometimes be more costly in the long run.
What do the immigration guides cover?
They cover how to choose and check a regulated immigration adviser, what a first consultation is usually like, what to have ready in general terms and how fees usually work. They do not explain immigration rules or say which route might apply to anyone, because Leaflegal does not give immigration advice.
How do I check an immigration adviser is regulated?
Ask the adviser who regulates them. Solicitors can be checked on the SRA register, and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority can be checked on its register. A regulated adviser should be happy to tell you and to show their registration details.
What should I bring to an immigration consultation?
In general terms, bring your passport or identity documents, any letters you have received from the Home Office, previous application documents if you have them and a note of key dates. The adviser will tell you exactly what they need for your matter, which may be different.
I have received a letter from the Home Office
Read it carefully and note any deadline. Then contact a regulated adviser as soon as you can, mentioning the deadline. Our guides can explain how working with an adviser usually goes, but they cannot tell you how to respond. Leaflegal can introduce you to a regulated adviser for free.

All advice comes from the regulated firm or adviser we introduce you to. That is why we keep our questions to the type of help you need and how soon.

We introduce one firm so that you are not contacted by lots of businesses competing for your enquiry. It keeps things simple and protects your privacy. If, after speaking to the firm, you decide it is not right for you, you are free not to go ahead and to look elsewhere.
How do you choose the firm?
We match your enquiry to a regulated firm or adviser that handles your type of matter. Every firm we work with is regulated to give immigration advice. Whether you go on to instruct them is entirely your choice, and you should feel free to ask them about their experience.
Do you cover the whole of the UK?
Immigration rules apply across the whole of the UK, and many immigration advisers work with clients remotely by phone, video and email. That means we can usually introduce you to a regulated adviser wherever you are in the UK, and in many cases where your family members are overseas.
Can you help if I am outside the UK?
Yes, in many cases. Lots of immigration applications are made from overseas, and regulated advisers in the UK often help people applying from abroad. Tell us where you and any family members are when you make your enquiry, so we can pass it to a suitable firm.
Is Leaflegal a law firm?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service, not a law firm or an immigration adviser. We do not give legal or immigration advice, and we do not handle applications. We pass your enquiry to one regulated firm or adviser, and they are responsible for any advice and work they carry out for you.
Who is allowed to give immigration advice?
In the UK, immigration advice can only be given by people who are regulated to do so. That includes solicitors regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, some other regulated legal professionals, and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, which was formerly the OISC. Giving immigration advice without being regulated is a criminal offence.
What is the Immigration Advice Authority?
It is the body that regulates immigration advisers who are not solicitors or other authorised legal professionals. It was previously called the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner, or OISC. It keeps a public register of advisers and the level of work each one is registered to do.
How can I check an adviser is regulated?
You can search for a solicitor or firm on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and for an adviser on the Immigration Advice Authority register. Both are free to use online. A genuine adviser will be happy to give you their registration details if you ask.
Is a solicitor better than an immigration adviser?
Not necessarily. Both can be highly experienced, and what matters most is that the person is regulated, has the right level of authorisation for your matter and has experience of cases like yours. Fees and ways of working can differ, so it is worth asking questions before you decide.
What if I am unhappy with my adviser?
Regulated firms and advisers must have a complaints procedure, and you can ask for a copy. If you are still unhappy after using it, you may be able to take your complaint further, for example to the Legal Ombudsman or to the adviser's regulator. The firm should explain how this works at the start.
Can friends or community groups help me?
People can help in practical ways, such as translating or gathering documents, but they should not give immigration advice unless they are regulated. Some charities and community organisations employ regulated advisers and can give advice. If you are unsure, ask whether the person is registered.
What questions should I ask an adviser?
Many people ask how often the adviser handles matters like theirs, who will work on the matter day to day, what the fee is and what it includes, whether government fees are extra, how they will keep in touch and what happens if the Home Office has questions. A good adviser will welcome these questions.

How much do immigration advisers charge?
It depends on the type of matter and the firm. Many advisers offer fixed fees for standard applications, while complex cases may be charged hourly or in stages. Always ask for the fee in writing, including what is covered and whether government fees are included, before you agree to anything.
Are government fees included in the adviser's price?
Usually not. Government fees, such as application fees and the Immigration Health Surcharge, are normally paid separately and can be significant. These amounts change from time to time. Ask your adviser for a full breakdown so you can see the total cost, and check the current figures on GOV.UK.
Is legal aid available for immigration?
Legal aid for immigration matters is limited, and most immigration work is privately funded. It may be available in some situations, often involving children or people in vulnerable circumstances. Whether it could apply depends on the matter and your circumstances. A regulated adviser can tell you whether it is worth exploring in your case.
Can I get a fee waiver?
In some situations, people who cannot afford a Home Office fee can ask for it to be waived. Waivers are not available for every application, and evidence of your finances is usually needed. Whether a waiver could be possible depends on the application and your circumstances, so ask a regulated adviser whether it is worth exploring.
Will my employer pay?
Some employers pay for their workers' visa costs and advisers, some pay part and some leave it to the worker. What your employer pays is a matter between you and them, so it is worth asking early. Be wary of anyone who asks you to pay for a job itself, and check with a regulated adviser before paying.

My visa ends very soon. What should I do?
Leaflegal cannot advise on your situation, but we can introduce you to a regulated adviser quickly. Tell us the end date of your visa when you enquire. Your current permission and what happens when it ends can be very important, so it is best to speak to an adviser as early as possible.
I have just had a refusal. Is there a deadline?
There can be very short time limits for challenging some immigration decisions. The refusal letter usually explains any rights and deadlines. Tell us the date on the decision when you enquire, and have the letter ready when the adviser calls, so they can look at it straight away.
How long do immigration applications take?
It depends on the type of application, where it is made and how busy the Home Office is. The Home Office publishes standard processing times on GOV.UK, and some routes offer faster services for an extra fee. Preparation time matters too. Your adviser can give you a realistic idea for your matter.
Can I travel while my application is being decided?
Travel plans and pending applications can interact in ways that are not obvious, and the effect can depend on the type of application. Leaflegal cannot advise on this. If you have an application in progress, or are about to make one, ask your regulated adviser before you book any travel so that they can explain anything you need to know.
What if the Home Office asks for more information?
The Home Office sometimes writes asking for more documents or an interview, usually with a deadline. Missing that deadline can harm your application. If you receive a request like this, contact your adviser straight away. If you do not have an adviser yet, mention the deadline when you enquire.
How long does the first conversation take?
