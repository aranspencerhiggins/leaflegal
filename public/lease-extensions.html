Lease extension solicitors in brief

Lease extension solicitors help flat owners add years to their lease, usually 90 years on top of the remaining term with ground rent reduced to zero under the statutory route. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated firm that handles lease extensions and works alongside a specialist surveyor.

The lease extension cost has two parts, the premium paid to the freeholder and the professional fees. Online lease extension calculators give a rough idea, but a surveyor’s valuation is what counts. Lease extensions explained

Pick a topic to read more.

If you own a flat in England or Wales, you almost certainly own it on a lease. A lease is a long agreement that gives you the right to live in and sell your home for a fixed number of years. The building itself, and the land it sits on, is usually owned by someone else, called the freeholder or landlord. When the lease runs out, the flat goes back to the freeholder.

That is why the number of years left on a lease matters so much. A lease that started at 99 years might feel endless when it is new, but after a few decades it becomes a real issue. Buyers, mortgage lenders and surveyors all look closely at the remaining term, and a shorter lease usually means a lower value and a more expensive extension.

Lease extension and enfranchisement law gives many leaseholders legal rights to deal with this. Flat owners can often extend their own lease, and groups of flat owners can sometimes buy the freehold of their building together. Some leaseholders also need help changing terms in a lease that is badly drafted, out of date or putting buyers off.

Freeholds are owned by all sorts of people and organisations. Some belong to individuals or small family companies, some to large investment companies that own thousands of freeholds, and some to councils, housing associations or the flat owners themselves. Day to day management is often handled by a managing agent. Who owns your freehold can affect how quickly things move and how negotiations go, which is something a solicitor will look at early on.

This is a specialist area. The rules are detailed, the deadlines are strict and the valuation side needs a surveyor who knows leasehold work. A notice served on the wrong person or with the wrong information can cost time and money. That is why most people use a solicitor who handles this kind of work regularly.

The law is changing

The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 makes major changes to leasehold law, but it is being brought into force in stages. Some parts already apply, while others need further regulations before they take effect. This means the right process, the length of the new lease and the way the price is worked out can depend on when you start. A solicitor will confirm which rules apply to you at the time you instruct them.

There are several ways to deal with a lease that is getting shorter or a lease that is causing problems. Which one suits you depends on your building, your freeholder, your plans and how the law applies at the time. These are the main routes a solicitor is likely to talk through with you.

A statutory lease extension

Many flat owners have a legal right to a new lease under the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993. Under the long standing rules, this adds 90 years to the remaining term and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn, which in practice means nothing. The freeholder cannot refuse if you qualify, and disputes about the price can go to a tribunal. The 2024 Act will change the length of a statutory extension and the way the premium is calculated, and these changes are being brought in over time. Find out more on our statutory lease extension page.

An informal lease extension

Some freeholders are willing to agree an extension outside the statutory process. This can be quicker and sometimes cheaper in fees, but the terms are whatever the two sides agree. Informal offers sometimes include a ground rent or other terms that a buyer or lender later dislikes, so it is common to have a solicitor review any offer before accepting it.

Buying the freehold together

Where enough flat owners in a building want to act together, they may have a right to buy the freehold. This is called collective enfranchisement. Once they own the freehold, they can grant themselves long leases and control how the building is run.

Changing the lease

Sometimes the problem is not the length of the lease but what it says. A lease might have a missing clause, a wrong plan, unclear repair duties or a ground rent that rises sharply. These terms can be changed by agreement with the freeholder in a deed of variation, and in some cases through a tribunal.

Dealing with it on a sale

If you are selling, there may be ways to deal with a short lease as part of the sale, such as starting the statutory process and passing the benefit of the notice to your buyer. Your solicitor will explain how that works and whether it fits your timescales.

Leaflegal does not tell you which option to choose. The firm we introduce you to will look at your lease and your circumstances and explain what is open to you.

Leasehold work is technical and deadline driven, so experience matters more than in many other kinds of property work. A general conveyancer may be excellent at buying and selling homes but rarely serve a lease extension notice. Here are some things people often look for when choosing a firm.

Regular leasehold experience

Ask how many lease extensions or enfranchisement claims the firm handles and who will do the work. A team that does this every week will know the common pitfalls, the usual tactics of large freeholders and the tribunal process if it gets that far.

Working with surveyors

The price for a lease extension or a freehold purchase is based on a valuation. A good firm works closely with specialist leasehold surveyors and can explain how the surveyor's report feeds into the notice and the negotiation. Some firms will suggest a surveyor, but you are free to choose your own.

Clear communication

These matters can take months. It helps to know who your contact is, how often you will hear from them and what you need to do at each stage. Ask the firm how it keeps clients updated.

Regulation and insurance

Solicitors in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and must carry professional indemnity insurance. Every firm Leaflegal introduces is regulated. You can check a firm's details on the regulator's website before you instruct it.

Transparent pricing

Ask what is included in the fee, what counts as an extra and what you might have to pay towards the freeholder's costs. A good firm will set this out in writing before any work starts.

Leasehold matters usually involve several different costs. Knowing what they are at the start makes it easier to compare quotes and plan your budget.

The premium

The premium is the price paid to the freeholder for the new lease or the freehold. It is usually the largest cost. Under the long standing rules it depends on factors such as the value of the flat, the years left on the lease, the ground rent and, for leases below 80 years, a share of what is called marriage value. The 2024 Act changes how premiums are worked out, including removing marriage value, but those parts depend on further regulations and are being introduced over time. A solicitor and surveyor will confirm what applies when you start.

Your own legal and valuation fees

You pay your own solicitor and surveyor. Many firms offer a fixed fee for a straightforward lease extension, with hourly rates for anything that becomes contested. Valuation fees vary with the property and the surveyor.

The freeholder's costs

Under the long standing statutory process, a leaseholder pays certain reasonable costs of the freeholder, such as their legal and valuation costs, but not their costs of going to a tribunal. The 2024 Act changes this so that each side generally pays its own costs, with some exceptions, but this change is part of the reforms being brought in over time. Your solicitor will explain the current position.

Other costs

There may be Land Registry fees, fees to your mortgage lender, and in some cases Stamp Duty Land Tax on a larger premium. Rates and thresholds change, so your solicitor will check what applies. If a dispute goes to a tribunal, there may be fees and further professional costs.

How long a lease matter takes depends on the route, the freeholder and whether the price is agreed. These are general ranges, and your solicitor will give you a more accurate picture once they see your lease.

Statutory lease extensions

Once the notice is served, the freeholder has a set period to reply with a counter notice. The date is stated in your notice and must be at least two months away. If the premium is agreed, the new lease can often be completed within a few months of serving the notice. If it is not agreed, there are strict deadlines for applying to the tribunal, and a contested case can take a year or longer.

Informal extensions

An informal extension can sometimes be completed more quickly, because there are no statutory waiting periods. In practice it still depends on how fast the freeholder responds and how long it takes to agree the terms.

Collective enfranchisement

Buying the freehold usually takes longer, because several flat owners have to organise themselves first. Getting participants together, setting up a company and agreeing funding can take months before any notice is served.

Lease variations

A simple variation agreed with a cooperative freeholder can be done fairly quickly. If the freeholder will not agree and a tribunal application is needed, it will take longer.

Planning around a sale

Many people only look at their lease when they decide to sell or remortgage. Starting early gives you more choice. If time is short, ask your solicitor what can be done in parallel with the sale.

Whatever the route, delays most often come from waiting for information, such as a copy of the lease, details of the freeholder or a response from a managing agent. Gathering what you can at the start, and replying quickly when your solicitor asks for something, helps keep things moving.

You do not need to have everything ready before you make an enquiry. These are the things a solicitor is likely to ask for, and having them to hand can speed up the first conversation.

  • A copy of your lease, or the title number of your flat
  • Roughly how many years are left on the lease
  • The name and address of your freeholder and any managing agent

  • Your mortgage lender's details, if you have a mortgage
  • Any letters or offers from the freeholder
  • Whether you are planning to sell or remortgage, and when
  • For a building wide claim, the number of flats and how many owners are interested

If you cannot find your lease

Most leases are registered at HM Land Registry, and a copy can usually be obtained online for a small fee. Your solicitor can do this for you. Older leases are sometimes not held in full, and the firm will explain how to deal with that.

Lease extensions questions and answers

How much does a lease extension cost?
The cost is the premium payable to the freeholder plus legal and valuation fees. The premium depends on the lease length, the value of the flat and the ground rent. A surveyor values the premium, and the solicitor explains their own fees in writing before starting.
Do I need a lease extension solicitor?
Yes, in practice. The statutory route has strict notices and deadlines, and an informal deal needs careful drafting. A lease extension solicitor protects your position and registers the new lease.
Which areas do you cover?
Leasehold flats in England and Wales.
Can I extend my lease myself?
You can, but the process has strict notices and deadlines. Most owners use a solicitor and a valuer.
Does it cost anything to use Leaflegal?
No. Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
What is a leasehold flat?
When you own a leasehold flat, you own the right to live in it and sell it for the number of years set out in the lease. The freeholder owns the building and the land. Your lease sets out your rights and duties, such as paying ground rent and service charge, keeping the inside in repair and following rules about the building. When the lease ends, the flat goes back to the freeholder unless the lease has been extended.
Why does the length of my lease matter?
As a lease gets shorter, the flat usually becomes less valuable and harder to sell or mortgage. Many lenders have a minimum term they want left on the lease when the mortgage ends. Under the long standing rules, the cost of extending also tends to rise more quickly once the lease drops below 80 years. A solicitor can explain how this affects your flat.
How do I find out how many years are left?
Your lease states when it started and how long it was granted for. Subtract the years that have passed since the start date. If you do not have a copy of your lease, it can usually be obtained from HM Land Registry. A solicitor can check this for you as part of their first review, and it is worth doing as the start date is not always the date the lease was signed.
What is the difference between a lease extension and buying the freehold?
A lease extension gives you a new, longer lease of your own flat, but the freeholder still owns the building. Buying the freehold, usually with other flat owners, means you take over ownership of the building itself. Freehold ownership brings control over how the building is managed, along with the responsibilities that come with it.
Do these rights apply to houses?
Owners of leasehold houses have separate rights under the Leasehold Reform Act 1967, which can include extending the lease or buying the freehold of the house. The rules and valuation methods differ from those for flats. A solicitor who handles leasehold work can explain the position for a house.
Is Leaflegal a law firm?
No. Leaflegal is an introduction service. We do not give legal advice. We introduce you to one regulated firm that handles leasehold matters, and any advice you receive comes from that firm.
What is the Right to Manage?
The Right to Manage lets qualifying flat owners take over the management of their building from the freeholder through a company they set up, without buying the freehold. It does not extend anyone's lease or change who owns the building. It is a separate process with its own rules, and a solicitor can explain how it compares with buying the freehold.
Do I qualify for a statutory lease extension?
Most long leaseholders of flats do, but there are exceptions. The lease usually needs to have been granted for more than 21 years, and some landlords, such as certain charities, are treated differently. The old rule that you must have owned the flat for two years used to apply, but the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025. A solicitor will confirm whether you qualify.
What is a section 42 notice?
It is the formal notice a leaseholder serves on the freeholder to start a statutory lease extension. It sets out the leaseholder's details, the property and the premium they propose to pay. The notice must contain the right information and be served correctly, which is one reason most people use a solicitor. Once it is served, strict deadlines apply to both sides.
What happens if the freeholder disagrees with the price?
The freeholder replies with a counter notice, which may propose a different premium or different terms. The two sides then negotiate, usually through their surveyors. If they cannot agree, either side can apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to decide. There are strict time limits for this, and missing them can mean the claim is treated as withdrawn.
What if I cannot find my freeholder?
Sometimes a freeholder cannot be traced, for example if a company has been dissolved. There is a separate court process for this situation, which can allow a lease extension or freehold purchase to go ahead without the freeholder's involvement. A solicitor can explain how it works and what it is likely to involve.
Do I need a surveyor as well as a solicitor?
Almost always. The premium is based on a valuation, and you need a specialist surveyor's report to decide what to put in the notice and how to negotiate. The solicitor handles the legal process and the surveyor handles the valuation. They usually work closely together.
Can I do it without a solicitor?
It is legally possible, but the process is technical and mistakes can be costly. An invalid notice can mean starting again, and in some cases there is a wait before a new notice can be served. Most people choose to use a solicitor who handles this work regularly.
Does my mortgage lender need to be involved?
Usually, yes. Your lender has security over your lease, so it normally needs to know about a new lease and may need to consent to it. The new lease is then registered with the lender's charge carried over. Your solicitor will contact the lender and deal with its requirements as part of the process.
Can I sell my flat while a lease extension is going on?
Often, yes. Under the statutory route, the benefit of a notice can usually be passed to a buyer when the flat is sold, so the buyer can carry on with the claim. This needs to be handled carefully at the time of the sale. Your solicitor will explain how it works and whether it suits your timescales.
How much does a lease extension cost?
There is the premium paid to the freeholder, your own solicitor's and surveyor's fees, and possibly some of the freeholder's costs. The premium depends on the value of the flat, the years left and the ground rent, among other things. Because the law on valuation is changing over time, your solicitor and surveyor will explain what applies and give you an estimate before you commit.
What is marriage value?
Under the long standing valuation rules, marriage value is the extra value created when a lease is extended. Where a lease has 80 years or fewer left, the leaseholder pays the freeholder a share of it, which can add considerably to the premium. The 2024 Act removes marriage value, but this depends on further regulations and is part of the reforms being brought in over time.
Will I pay my freeholder's costs?
Under the long standing statutory process, leaseholders usually pay certain reasonable legal and valuation costs of the freeholder. The 2024 Act changes this so that each side generally bears its own costs, with some exceptions. That change is being introduced over time, so your solicitor will confirm the rules that apply when you start.
Do solicitors offer fixed fees?
Many do for a straightforward lease extension. The fixed fee usually covers the notice, negotiation and completion of the new lease. Tribunal work, complicated leases or difficult freeholders may be charged separately. Ask for a written quote that sets out what is and is not included.
Does Leaflegal charge me?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that never changes what you pay the firm.
Is there Stamp Duty on a lease extension?
A new lease can sometimes be a taxable transaction, depending on the size of the premium and the rules at the time. Rates and thresholds change. Your solicitor will check whether anything is payable and deal with any return that needs to be filed.
Can I get a mortgage to pay for the premium?
Some people pay the premium from savings, while others borrow, often by remortgaging or arranging further lending from their existing lender. Whether this is possible depends on your lender and your finances. A mortgage broker or your lender can talk through the options, and your solicitor will deal with the legal side of any new borrowing.
What is the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024?
It is a major reform of leasehold law in England and Wales. Among other things, it aims to make it easier and cheaper for leaseholders to extend their leases or buy their freehold. It received Royal Assent in 2024, but many of its changes need further regulations and are being brought into force over time. A solicitor will confirm what applies on the date you start.
Will statutory extensions become longer?
Under the long standing rules, a statutory extension for a flat adds 90 years to the remaining term. The 2024 Act provides for a much longer standard extension of 990 years. This is one of the reforms being introduced in stages, so your solicitor will tell you which length applies when you serve your notice.
Should I wait for the reforms before extending?
That depends on your circumstances and is a decision for you with your solicitor's advice. Waiting means the lease keeps getting shorter, and plans to sell or remortgage may not allow it. Timings for the reforms can also change. A solicitor can explain the possible pros and cons for your own flat.
Has the two year ownership rule gone?
The requirement to have owned a flat for two years before using the statutory right to extend used to apply, but the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025. It means a new owner may be able to start the process straight away. A solicitor will confirm that the rule does not apply to you before serving a notice.
Will my ground rent change?
A statutory extension under the long standing rules reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn for the new lease. Separate rules limit ground rents on most new residential leases granted since 2022. The 2024 Act also includes provisions about buying out ground rent. Your solicitor can explain how these rules apply to your lease.
Do the reforms affect people who have already started?
Whether a claim is dealt with under the old or new rules can depend on when the notice was served and the transitional provisions in the regulations. If you have already started, ask your solicitor how the changes affect your claim.

More questions people ask

When should I instruct a solicitor to sell my flat?
Many sellers instruct a solicitor when they put the flat on the market, or even before. This allows the solicitor to obtain the title and lease, check the remaining term and order the management pack early. With a leasehold flat, an early start can save weeks once you accept an offer.
What is a management pack?
It is information from the freeholder, managing agent or management company about the building and your share of its costs. It usually includes service charge accounts and budgets, insurance details, planned works, ground rent and any arrears. The buyer's solicitor needs it to advise the buyer. There is usually a fee for it.
Who pays for the management pack?
In most sales the seller pays, as the information is needed to sell the flat. The 2024 Act includes provisions about what can be charged for leasehold information, which are being brought in over time. Your solicitor will order the pack and tell you the cost.
What forms will I need to fill in?
Usually the standard property information form, a leasehold information form and a fittings and contents form. These ask about the flat, the building, service charges, disputes and any changes you have made. Your solicitor will send them to you and help with any questions.
What if I cannot find my lease?
Most leases are registered at HM Land Registry, and your solicitor can obtain a copy. Older leases are not always held in full, and your solicitor will explain how to deal with that if it happens. If you have other documents, such as a previous deed of variation or a lease extension, these are useful too. Your solicitor will check that everything affecting the lease is included in the contract pack so the buyer's solicitor has a complete picture.
Do I need to tell the buyer about disputes with neighbours or the freeholder?
The property information forms ask about disputes, and you must answer honestly. Giving incorrect or incomplete answers can lead to claims after the sale. If you are unsure how to answer a question, ask your solicitor.
Can I sell a flat I have let to a tenant?
Yes. You can sell with the tenant in place to an investor, or arrange for the tenancy to end before completion if the buyer wants vacant possession. Ending a tenancy has to follow the correct legal process and can take time. Your solicitor will explain how the tenancy affects the sale, and a letting agent or housing specialist can help with the tenancy itself.
