Family law explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Family law deals with the legal side of relationships, separation and children. It covers how a marriage or civil partnership is ended, how money and property are divided, where children live and how they spend time with each parent, and the agreements couples make before or during a relationship. In England and Wales most of this work is handled by the Family Court, although the large majority of families settle things without a judge ever making a decision.

People usually come to family law at a difficult time. A relationship may have just ended, or it may have been ending slowly for years. There may be children who need stability, a home that one or both people want to keep, and worries about money. It is normal to feel overwhelmed. A family solicitor deals with these situations every day and can help you understand where things stand and what the next steps usually look like.

Family law is also about planning ahead. Couples who are about to marry, move in together or buy a home sometimes take advice to record what they intend, so that there is less to argue about if things go wrong later.

If you are worried about your safety

If you or your children are at risk of harm from a partner or family member, your safety comes first. In an emergency call the police on 999. You can also contact a domestic abuse support service such as the National Domestic Abuse Helpline, which is free and confidential. A family solicitor can explain the protective orders the court can make, but they are not an emergency service.

Paid help and legal aid

Legal aid is only available for some family matters, usually where there is evidence of domestic abuse or a risk to a child, and it depends on your income and savings. GOV.UK has a tool to check whether you might qualify. Leaflegal introduces people who are looking for paid help from a private family law firm. We are not a law firm and we do not give legal advice.

Matters we can help with

Ending a relationship

  • Divorce, which ends a marriage under the no fault process
  • Dissolution, which ends a civil partnership in the same way
  • Separation agreements for couples who want to live apart without divorcing yet
  • Disputes between unmarried couples who owned or lived in a home together

Money and property

  • Financial settlements covering the home, savings, pensions, businesses and maintenance
  • Consent orders that turn an agreement into a binding court order
  • Court proceedings where a financial agreement cannot be reached
  • Prenuptial and postnuptial agreements made before or during a marriage

Children

  • Agreeing where children will live and how they spend time with each parent
  • Applying for or responding to a child arrangements order
  • Disputes about schooling, medical treatment, changing a name or moving away
  • Taking children on holiday abroad or relocating with them

Each of these has its own page on this site with more detail. If you are not sure which one fits your situation, choose the closest option on the form and the firm will talk it through with you.

There is more than one way to sort out a family matter. Going to court is usually the last resort, and the courts expect people to think about other options first. A solicitor will explain which routes might suit your situation, and many families use more than one.

Agreeing between yourselves

Some couples talk things through and reach an agreement on their own, then ask a solicitor to check it and put it into the right legal form. This can be the quickest and cheapest route where both people are calm, honest about their finances and broadly agree.

Solicitor negotiation

Each person has their own solicitor, who exchanges information and proposals by letter or email. The solicitors can help keep discussions focused and make sure nothing important is missed. Most financial settlements are reached this way or through mediation.

Mediation

A trained, neutral mediator helps both of you talk and work towards an agreement. The mediator does not take sides or make decisions. Before applying to court about children or finances, you will usually need to attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting, known as a MIAM, unless an exemption applies. Mediation may not be suitable where there has been domestic abuse.

Collaborative law

Each person has a specially trained collaborative lawyer, and everyone meets together to work things out. Both people usually sign an agreement not to go to court, so if the process breaks down they need to instruct new solicitors.

Arbitration and private hearings

An arbitrator, usually an experienced family lawyer, makes a decision that both people agree in advance to accept. Some couples also pay for a private early neutral evaluation, where an experienced lawyer gives a view on the likely outcome. These options can be quicker and more private than court, though both people pay the arbitrator or evaluator.

Court

Where agreement is not possible, or where someone needs urgent protection, either person can apply to the Family Court. Court proceedings follow set stages and can take many months. Even once a case is started, many families still reach agreement before the final hearing.

One of the first questions people ask is how long it will all take. The honest answer is that it depends on the type of matter, how busy the local court is, and above all how much the two people can agree. Here is a general picture of how timing usually works in England and Wales.

Divorce and dissolution

These have fixed minimum waiting periods set by law. There must be at least 20 weeks between the application being issued and the applicant asking for the conditional order. After the conditional order there is a further wait of six weeks and one day before the final order can be applied for. In practice many divorces take longer, often because people wait to sort out their finances first.

Financial matters

If both people agree early and give full information, a financial settlement can sometimes be reached and approved within a few months. Where negotiation takes longer, or the case goes to court, it can take a year or more. Court cases usually pass through a first appointment, a settlement meeting with a judge and, if needed, a final hearing.

Children matters

Many parents agree arrangements within weeks with help from mediation or solicitors. If a court application is made, the court will usually hold a first hearing and may ask for safeguarding checks and reports. Some cases settle at the first hearing. Others take many months, particularly where there are safety concerns that need to be looked into.

Urgent matters

Where someone needs protection from abuse, or a child is at risk of being taken abroad without consent, the court can act very quickly, sometimes on the same day. Tell the firm straight away if your matter is urgent.

What can slow things down

  • One person not replying or not providing financial information
  • Waiting for valuations of property, pensions or a business
  • Court backlogs in some areas
  • Disagreements that need a judge to decide
  • Changes in circumstances, such as a job loss or a house sale falling through

A solicitor will give you an idea of likely timescales once they understand your situation, and will keep you updated as things progress.

Family matters are personal, and you will be sharing a lot with the solicitor you choose. It helps to feel comfortable with them as well as confident in their experience. Here are some things people often consider.

Regulation

Solicitors in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Every firm we introduce is a regulated law firm. Regulation means the firm has to follow professional rules, hold insurance and have a complaints procedure.

Family law experience

Family law has its own procedures and its own court. A firm that does family work every day will know how the local courts run, what judges expect and how to keep costs proportionate. Some solicitors are members of Resolution, an organisation of family lawyers committed to a non confrontational approach. Some also hold accreditation as a specialist in particular areas such as complex finances or children.

Approach

Ask how the solicitor usually works. Some will try hard to settle things through negotiation or mediation, while being ready to go to court if needed. Others are trained in collaborative law. A good family solicitor will be honest about what is realistic and will keep the focus on reaching a workable outcome.

Clear pricing

Ask for an estimate in writing before you agree to anything. A good firm will explain how they charge, what is included and what might cost more.

Questions you might ask

  • Who will deal with my matter day to day?
  • How do you charge, and what is the likely total?
  • How often will you update me?
  • Do you offer mediation, collaborative law or arbitration?
  • What do you need from me to get started?

Family law costs vary a great deal. A straightforward, agreed divorce costs far less than a contested financial case that goes to a final hearing. The biggest factor is usually how much the two people can agree on.

How firms charge

Many family firms offer a fixed fee for well defined work, such as handling an uncontested divorce or drafting a consent order. Work that is harder to predict, such as negotiating a financial settlement or running court proceedings, is usually charged by the hour or in stages, with an estimate for each stage. Some firms offer a first meeting at a fixed price.

Court and other fees

The court charges fees for applications, such as starting a divorce or asking the court to approve a consent order. Other costs might include mediation, property or pension valuations, and barristers if a case goes to a hearing. Court fees change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask the firm. If you have a low income, you may be able to get help with court fees.

Keeping costs down

  • Gather your documents before the first meeting
  • Put your questions in one email rather than several
  • Be open about your finances from the start
  • Think about mediation or other ways of agreeing
  • Ask for regular updates on costs so there are no surprises

In some financial cases where one person has much less access to money than the other, the court can order the wealthier person to pay towards the other's legal fees. A solicitor can explain whether this might apply.

You do not need to have everything ready before you make an enquiry. A family solicitor will guide you through what they need. But a little preparation can make the first conversation more useful and help keep costs down.

Useful information to gather

  • Your marriage or civil partnership certificate, if you have one
  • The dates you married, separated and started living together
  • Your children's names and dates of birth
  • A rough list of property, savings, pensions, debts and income for both of you
  • Any letters, emails or court papers you have received
  • Any agreement you have already reached, even informally

Thinking about what matters to you

It can help to write down what matters most to you, such as staying in the home, keeping the children's routine, or having a clean financial break. Your solicitor will help you understand which of these are likely to be achievable.

Looking after yourself

Separation is stressful. Many people find it helps to talk to friends, family, a GP or a counsellor alongside the legal process. Family solicitors can often recommend local support services, including counselling and parenting programmes.

Keeping records safe

Keep copies of important documents somewhere private and secure. If there are safety concerns, think about who can see your emails, phone and post. Support services can help with safety planning.

Words you may hear

Family law has its own vocabulary, and some terms changed when no fault divorce arrived in 2022. You may still see older words in letters or online, so here is a short guide.

  • Applicant and respondent, the person who applies to court and the person who responds
  • Conditional order, the middle stage of a divorce, previously called the decree nisi
  • Final order, which legally ends the marriage, previously called the decree absolute
  • Financial remedy, the legal term for a financial settlement decided or approved by the court
  • Disclosure, sharing full details of your finances with the other person
  • Parental responsibility, the legal rights and duties a parent has for a child
  • Child arrangements order, a court order about where a child lives and who they spend time with
  • Clean break, an outcome where former partners have no ongoing financial claims against each other

Your solicitor will explain any term you are unsure about. It is always fine to ask them to put something more simply.

What does a family solicitor do?
A family solicitor helps with the legal side of separation, money and children. They explain how the law usually works, help you gather information, negotiate with the other person or their solicitor, draft agreements and court documents, and represent you if a matter goes to court. They can also suggest other ways of resolving things, such as mediation or arbitration, and point you towards support services.
Do I need a solicitor for every family matter?
No. Some people handle simple matters themselves, such as an agreed divorce. Many still take advice on the parts that carry more risk, such as dividing pensions or property, or arrangements for children. A solicitor can help with one specific part of your matter, such as checking an agreement, rather than handling everything.
How quickly can I speak to someone?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, and often sooner. If your matter is urgent, for example because of a court deadline or concerns about a child's safety, say so on the form so the firm knows to prioritise it. The first call is usually a chance to explain what is happening and ask questions.
Will the solicitor contact my ex partner?
Not without discussing it with you first. A solicitor will talk to you about how and when to approach the other person. Where there are safety concerns, they can explain options that keep your contact details private. Some people want their solicitor to write straight away, while others prefer to raise things themselves first. Your solicitor will explain the options and agree the approach with you before anything is sent.
What if my ex partner already has a solicitor?
That is common. Your solicitor will usually correspond with theirs. You should not normally deal directly with the other person's solicitor once you have your own, as that solicitor acts only for your ex partner. If you have received a letter from their solicitor, keep it and share it with the firm we introduce you to. It often explains what your ex partner is proposing and any deadline for a reply.
Can the same solicitor act for both of us?
No. A solicitor can only act for one person in a family matter because your interests may be different. One firm can draft an agreement and the other person can take advice from a separate firm. This does not stop you working together. Many couples still reach agreement jointly, through mediation or discussion, and simply take separate advice before anything is finalised.
Do we have to go to court?
Most family matters are resolved without a court hearing. The court is involved in a divorce, and it approves financial agreements through a consent order, but this is usually done on paper. A hearing is only needed where people cannot agree or someone needs urgent protection. Even where a court application is made, many cases settle before a final hearing.
What is a MIAM?
A Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting is a short meeting with a mediator to find out whether mediation could work for you. You usually need to attend one before applying to court about children or money. There are exemptions, for example where there is evidence of domestic abuse or the matter is urgent.
What is the difference between mediation and collaborative law?
In mediation a neutral mediator helps you both talk, and you may take legal advice separately. In collaborative law you each have your own trained lawyer, and everyone meets together. Both aim to reach agreement without court. A solicitor can explain which, if either, might suit you. Some families use both at different stages.
Which court deals with family cases?
Most family cases in England and Wales are dealt with by the Family Court, which sits in many locations. Divorce applications are now largely handled online. More complex cases may be heard by more senior judges, and some go to the High Court. Your solicitor will know how your local court works and what to expect there.
Are family court hearings private?
Most family hearings are held in private, and there are strict rules about sharing information from cases involving children. Some journalists can attend certain hearings, but reporting restrictions usually protect the identity of children and families. A solicitor can explain what this means in your case. Your solicitor can also explain what you can and cannot share with friends or on social media.
Can a family matter be dealt with remotely?
Many solicitors offer meetings by phone or video. Some court hearings are also held remotely or partly remotely. You do not need to use a firm in your own town, although a firm that knows your local court can be helpful. Many people find remote meetings easier to fit around work and childcare, and they can make it simpler to share documents securely.
How long do family matters take?
It depends on the matter and on how much the two people agree. A divorce has fixed minimum waiting periods. Financial and children matters that settle early may take a few months, while those that go through court can take a year or more. Your solicitor will give you a better idea once they understand your situation.
How much does a family solicitor cost?
It depends on the work and how complex it is. Many firms offer fixed fees for well defined tasks like an agreed divorce or a consent order. Negotiation and court work is usually charged hourly or in stages. Ask for a written estimate before you agree to anything. A good firm will also update you if the estimate changes.
Can I get legal aid?
Legal aid is available for some family matters, usually where there is evidence of domestic abuse or child protection concerns, and it depends on income and savings. GOV.UK has a tool to check. Leaflegal introduces people looking for paid help from private law firms. If you think you may qualify, it is worth checking before you decide how to proceed.
Can my ex partner be made to pay my legal costs?
In family cases each person usually pays their own costs. In some financial cases the court can order one person to pay towards the other's legal fees, particularly where one has much more access to money. Costs orders can also be made where someone behaves unreasonably during proceedings. A solicitor can explain whether either applies to you.
Are court fees included in the solicitor's price?
Not always. Court fees are paid to the court and are often listed separately. Ask the firm whether their quote includes court fees, barristers' fees and any expert reports. If you have a low income, you may be able to get help with court fees. Fees change from time to time, so check GOV.UK for the current figures.

You agree your fees directly with the firm, and they will explain them before you decide whether to instruct them. There is no obligation to go ahead after the first conversation.
Can I pay in instalments?
Some firms offer monthly payment plans or staged billing. Others may ask for money on account before starting work. Ask the firm what they offer when they contact you. It is worth asking early, so you can plan your budget. A good firm will be open about how and when they bill, and will tell you if the expected cost changes as the matter goes on.
What if I am experiencing domestic abuse?
Your safety comes first. In an emergency call 999. A domestic abuse support service such as the National Domestic Abuse Helpline can give confidential help with safety planning. A family solicitor can explain the court orders that may protect you, such as a non molestation order or an occupation order.
What is a non molestation order?
It is a court order that can stop someone using or threatening violence, or harassing or intimidating you. Breaking one is a criminal offence. In urgent cases the court can make an order without the other person being told first. A solicitor can explain whether it might apply to you.
What is an occupation order?
It is a court order that can decide who lives in the family home, or part of it, for a period of time. It can be used to exclude someone from the home or the area around it. The court weighs up the risk of harm and other factors before making one.
Can I keep my address private?
Yes, where there are safety concerns, the court has ways of keeping your address and contact details confidential. Tell your solicitor as soon as possible so they can use the right forms. If you are in danger, call 999 in an emergency or contact a domestic abuse support service. They can help with safety planning alongside the legal process.
Where can I get emotional support?
Many people find it helpful to talk to a GP, counsellor, or a support line during separation. There are also support groups and parenting courses for separated parents. Your solicitor can often point you to local services. Looking after your wellbeing can also help you make clearer decisions during the legal process. Children may benefit from support too, and schools can often help.
Is what I tell Leaflegal kept private?
Yes. We only share your enquiry with the one firm we introduce you to, with your consent. We do not share the details of your matter with advertisers. Our privacy policy explains how we look after your information. The firm we introduce you to is bound by professional duties of confidentiality, so what you tell them is also kept private.
What will the solicitor ask me?
Expect questions about your relationship, any children, your home and your finances, and what you would like to happen. They will also ask whether there are any safety concerns or court deadlines. Have a rough picture of your circumstances ready, but do not worry if you do not know everything.
Do I have to instruct the firm you introduce?
No. There is no obligation. You can speak to the firm, ask questions about their approach and fees, and then decide whether to instruct them. If you decide the firm is not the right fit, you are free to look elsewhere. Our service is free, and you only pay the firm if you choose to instruct them and agree their fees.
What if I am not happy with my solicitor?
Raise it with the solicitor or the firm first, as every regulated firm has a complaints procedure. If that does not resolve it, you can usually go to the Legal Ombudsman. You can also change solicitor, although you may need to settle any outstanding bill. The firm must tell you how to complain at the start.
Can a solicitor help with just part of my matter?
Yes. Some firms offer limited help, such as advice at the start, checking a draft agreement or preparing you for a hearing. This is sometimes called unbundled or limited retainer work. Ask the firm whether they offer it. Limited help can be a way to keep costs down while still getting advice on the parts that matter most. The firm should explain clearly what they will and will not do.
Do I need a barrister as well?
Not usually for straightforward matters. Your solicitor handles most of the work. If a case goes to a contested hearing, the solicitor may suggest instructing a barrister to present it in court, or to give a specialist opinion on a complex point. They should explain the likely cost before doing so.
Can I use a firm outside my area?
Yes. Many family firms work with clients across England and Wales by phone, video and email. Some people prefer a local firm that knows the nearby court and can meet in person. Either way, your matter is dealt with under the same law. What matters most is that the firm has the right experience.
What if we lived abroad or married abroad?
Marriages that took place abroad can often be ended in England and Wales if one or both of you has a strong enough connection here, such as living here. International cases can raise extra questions about which country's courts should deal with things. A solicitor with international experience can explain the options.
Is it ever too late to sort out finances after divorce?
Financial claims between former spouses can remain open after a divorce unless a court order closes them. This means a claim can sometimes be made years later. Remarrying can also affect your own ability to make a claim. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you. Taking advice sooner usually gives more options.
