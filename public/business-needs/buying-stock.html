The basics

Retailers, wholesalers, manufacturers and online sellers all face the same problem: stock has to be paid for before it can be sold. Stocking up for Christmas, taking a bulk order at a better price or launching a new range can mean a large payment to suppliers weeks or months before the sales come in. Without finance, that gap can limit how much the business is able to buy.

Companies often use short-term or flexible finance for stock, so they are not paying for borrowing once the goods have sold. A revolving credit facility lets a business draw what it needs and repay as sales arrive. Businesses with card takings may use a merchant cash advance, and those selling to other businesses on credit may use invoice finance. An unsecured loan can suit a single, planned purchase.

What businesses use it for

Seasonal stock

Buy in ahead of Christmas, summer or another peak, so the shelves are full when demand is highest and repayments follow the sales.

Bulk buying discounts

Order larger quantities when a supplier offers a better unit price, where the saving helps to offset the cost of the finance.

New product lines

Pay for the first run of a new range or product before it has started selling, so the launch is not held back by cash.

Large customer orders

Buy the stock or raw materials needed to fulfil a big order from a trade customer who will pay on terms after delivery.

Things to consider

How quickly it sells

Finance for stock works best when you are confident the goods will sell within a known period. Slow-moving stock means paying for borrowing for longer, so be realistic about sell-through.

Short-term needs, short-term finance

Taking a long loan for stock that sells in weeks can mean paying interest long after the money has come back in. Flexible facilities that you repay as you sell often fit better.

Supplier terms

Before borrowing, check whether suppliers will offer longer payment terms. Combining supplier credit with a smaller facility can reduce the overall cost.

Questions and answers

What finance can I use to buy stock?
Common options include revolving credit facilities, merchant cash advances, short-term unsecured loans and, for businesses selling on credit terms, invoice finance. Some specialist lenders also offer trade finance to pay suppliers directly. A broker can explain which fits your business.
Can I finance stock for an online shop?
Often, yes. Lenders look at your sales history, which may come from marketplaces or payment providers as well as your bank statements. Some providers offer finance repaid as a share of online revenue, similar to a merchant cash advance. A broker can tell you which providers work with your sales channels.
How much stock finance can my business get?
It depends mainly on turnover, sales history, how quickly stock sells and your existing commitments. Loanleaf handles enquiries from £5,000 upwards, and a broker can tell you what is realistic once they have seen your figures and understood how quickly your stock usually sells.
Is it worth borrowing to get a bulk discount?
It can be, if the discount is larger than the total cost of the finance and you are confident the stock will sell in good time. Compare the saving with the full cost of borrowing, including any fees, before deciding.
Can a lender pay my supplier directly?
Some can. Trade finance and certain stock facilities pay suppliers on your behalf, sometimes overseas, and you repay the lender later. Not every lender offers this, so a broker can tell you whether it is available for your purchase and what the lender would need to see.
