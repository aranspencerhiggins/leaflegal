Fees and costs

Immigration advisers usually charge a fixed fee for each application or stage of work, and some charge hourly for complex matters. Home Office application fees and any Immigration Health Surcharge are separate from the adviser’s fee and are paid to the Home Office.

Ask for the fee and what it covers in writing before you agree to anything. A regulated adviser must explain their costs clearly and tell you how to complain if something goes wrong.

Leaflegal is free for you to use. The firm pays us a fee for the introduction, and they will tell you about it in writing.

Questions to ask the firm

  • Who regulates you, and at what level?
  • What is your fee, and what does it include?
  • Are Home Office fees separate, and roughly how much are they?
  • Who will handle my case day to day?
  • How will you keep me updated?

When to get help

  • When you have a decision letter or a deadline
  • Well before a visa or permission runs out
  • If you have been asked for more information
  • Before you send any application you are unsure about

Things to know

Check who is advising you

Anyone giving you immigration advice must be regulated. You can check a solicitor on the SRA register or an adviser on the Immigration Advice Authority register.

Fees

Immigration firms charge their own fees, and Home Office application fees are separate. The firm should explain both before you go ahead.

Free help

If you can’t afford to pay, GOV.UK lists organisations that may be able to help for free, including for asylum.

A first consultation with a regulated immigration adviser is often the moment when an uncertain situation starts to feel more manageable. It is a chance to explain what is happening, show the adviser your documents and hear what they think the next steps might be. A little preparation helps you get far more out of that time.

Our short guide above lists the basics to have ready. This page goes into more detail about booking, gathering and organising documents, telling your story clearly, what usually happens during the meeting and what to expect afterwards. It does not explain immigration rules or what any application might need. Only a regulated adviser can look at your circumstances and advise you. Leaflegal does not give immigration advice.

Why preparation matters

Consultations are often time limited and usually charged for. If the adviser spends much of the time piecing together dates or asking for documents you do not have with you, there is less time left for advice. If they have a clear picture early on, they can focus on your questions and on what happens next.

Preparation also helps the adviser spot things you might not think are important. A date on an old letter, a past refusal or a change in your circumstances can matter more than you expect, so bringing everything you have, even if it feels irrelevant, is often helpful.

Who this guide is for

This guide is written mainly for individuals and families. Employers meeting an adviser about sponsoring workers will find much of it useful too, although they will usually also bring business documents and information about the roles involved.

If you are short of time

If you have a deadline coming up, such as a date in a Home Office letter or a visa end date, do not let preparation delay you. Book the consultation first, tell the adviser about the deadline straight away and gather what you can in the time available.

Most consultations begin with a short call or an enquiry form. What you say at this stage helps the adviser decide whether they are the right person to help and how long the consultation needs to be.

What to mention when you get in touch

  • A short description of what you need help with
  • Any deadlines or important dates, such as a letter's response date or a visa end date
  • Whether you have received a recent decision or letter from the Home Office
  • Whether anyone else is involved, such as a partner, children or an employer
  • Whether you would like an interpreter or prefer a particular language
  • How you would prefer to meet, such as by phone, video or in person

Asking about the consultation itself

Before you book, it is reasonable to ask how long the consultation will last, how much it costs, whether the fee is deducted if you then instruct the adviser and whether you will get anything in writing afterwards. Some advisers offer a short free call first and then a longer paid consultation.

Checking the adviser

Before sharing personal documents, check that the adviser is regulated. Solicitors can be checked on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and non lawyer advisers on the Immigration Advice Authority's adviser finder. Our guide on choosing an immigration adviser explains this in more detail.

Sending documents in advance

Many advisers ask clients to send copies of key documents before the consultation. This lets them read in advance and use the meeting time well. If they ask, send clear copies through the method they suggest, such as a secure upload link. Keep the originals safe yourself unless the adviser asks to see them.

Every situation is different, and an adviser will tell you exactly what they need. The list below gives a general idea of the kinds of documents people commonly bring to a first consultation. Bring what you have, and do not worry if you do not have everything.

Identity and status

  • Your current passport and any previous passports, especially those with UK visas or stamps
  • Details of your current immigration status, such as access to your UKVI account or eVisa, or any older status documents
  • Identity documents for any family members the consultation is about

Past applications and decisions

  • Copies of any previous applications you made, and what was sent with them
  • Any decision letters, including grants and refusals
  • Any appeal or review paperwork
  • Reference numbers from past applications

Letters and messages

Bring every letter, email or text you have received from the Home Office, a tribunal or another official body about your situation, with the dates you received them. Keep envelopes if they show a postmark, as dates can matter.

Other documents that are often useful

Depending on your situation, an adviser may find it helpful to see things like documents about your family relationships, your work or studies, your address history or your finances. You do not need to work out in advance what is relevant. If you are unsure, bring it or ask the adviser before the meeting.

Organising them

A simple folder with documents in date order makes a big difference. If you are sending copies electronically, clear scans or photos with sensible file names save time. A short list of what you are providing helps the adviser check nothing is missing.

If documents are held by someone else, such as a former adviser, an employer or a family member abroad, ask for them early. Requests like these can take longer than expected, and the adviser can tell you which ones are worth chasing first.

Advisers need to understand your history before they can advise on what comes next. Many people find it hard to remember dates and details on the spot, especially when they are anxious. Writing things down beforehand helps.

A simple timeline

Try writing a short timeline in date order. It does not need to be perfect. Include when you first came to the UK, if you did, any visas or permissions you have held, any applications you made and their results, any time spent outside the UK, and any important changes in your life such as a marriage, a birth or a new job. If you are not sure of an exact date, an approximate one is better than nothing.

Being open

It can be tempting to leave out things that feel embarrassing or worrying, such as a past refusal, a period without status, a criminal matter or a mistake on an old form. An adviser can only help with what they know. Information you share is confidential, and advisers are used to hearing about difficult situations. Things that come to light later can cause bigger problems than if they had been raised at the start.

What you want to achieve

It helps to think about what you are hoping for from the consultation. You might want to understand your current position, know what options there might be, find out about timing and costs or get help with a letter you have received. Saying this at the beginning helps the adviser focus.

Your questions

  • What do you think the main issues are in my situation?
  • What are the next steps, and what do you need from me?
  • Are there any deadlines I need to be aware of?
  • How long might things take, in general terms?
  • What would your fees be for the next stage, and what do they include?
  • Who would handle my matter day to day?

Consultations take different forms, but most follow a similar shape. Knowing what to expect can make the experience feel less daunting.

Try to start the consultation feeling as settled as you can. Have a drink of water nearby, keep your phone charged if you are using it for a call, and if you have children at home, arrange for someone else to look after them during the meeting if possible. These small things help you concentrate on what the adviser is saying.

Practical points

For phone or video calls, find a quiet, private space where you can speak freely, and have your documents and notes in front of you. Check your device and connection beforehand. For in person meetings, allow time for travel and bring your folder of documents and any originals the adviser has asked to see.

How the meeting usually goes

The adviser will usually start by confirming who you are and what you want help with. They will then ask questions about your history and look at your documents. Some questions may feel very detailed or personal. That is normal and is how they build an accurate picture. Towards the end, they will usually explain their initial view, any options they see and what would happen next if you instruct them.

Interpreters and support

If you asked for an interpreter, they will usually join the call or meeting. Speak in short sections so they can interpret accurately. If you have brought someone for support, the adviser may ask to speak to you alone for part of the meeting, particularly about sensitive matters. That is a normal precaution.

Taking notes

It is easy to forget details after a meeting. Taking notes, or asking the adviser whether they will confirm key points in writing, can help. If you do not understand something, ask the adviser to explain it again. Good advisers expect questions.

What a consultation is not

A first consultation is usually the start of a conversation, not the end. The adviser may need more information before giving firm advice, and they may not be able to give you a definite answer on the day.

What happens after the consultation depends on what you discussed and whether you decide to instruct the adviser.

Written confirmation

Many advisers send a written summary of the consultation or an engagement letter setting out what they will do next, what it will cost and what they need from you. Read it carefully. If anything does not match your understanding, raise it with the adviser before agreeing.

Deciding whether to go ahead

You do not have to instruct the adviser straight away, unless a deadline means time is short. Some people take time to think, speak to family or compare advisers. If you do go ahead, the adviser will usually ask you to sign their terms and pay a fee or money on account before starting work.

Gathering what is needed

If the adviser asks for further documents or information, try to provide them promptly. Keep a list of what you have sent and when. If you cannot get something, tell the adviser rather than waiting, as they may be able to suggest alternatives.

Staying in touch

Let your adviser know straight away if your circumstances change or you receive any new letter or message from the Home Office or a tribunal. Keep copies of everything you send and receive.

Questions and answers

How soon should I book a consultation?
As soon as you know you need help, especially if there is a date involved, such as a visa end date or a deadline in a letter. Some advisers have waiting lists, and starting early gives you and the adviser more time to prepare. If you have an urgent deadline, say so when you first contact them. Many advisers can arrange urgent appointments, sometimes for an extra fee.
Is a phone or video consultation as good as meeting in person?
For many people, yes. Phone and video consultations are now common and can be just as thorough, as long as you have your documents to hand and a quiet place to talk. Some people prefer meeting in person, particularly if they have lots of paper documents or find it easier to talk face to face. Ask the adviser which formats they offer and choose whichever suits you best.
How long does a first consultation usually last?
It varies between advisers and depends on the matter. Some first consultations are short calls to understand what you need, while others are longer meetings that include initial advice. The adviser should tell you in advance how long it will be and what it will cover. If your situation is complex, ask whether a longer appointment would be better.
Do I have to pay for a first consultation?
Many advisers charge a fixed fee for an initial consultation, because it involves professional time reading documents and giving advice. Some offer a short free call first, and some deduct the consultation fee from later work if you instruct them. Ask about this when booking so you know what to expect. The fee should be confirmed to you before the consultation takes place.
Can my partner or family member join the consultation?
Usually, yes. If the matter involves a partner or family member, it can be helpful for them to be there. Let the adviser know when booking. For some parts of the conversation, the adviser may want to speak to each person separately, especially about sensitive matters. That is a normal part of good practice and is not a sign of any concern.
Can I book on behalf of someone else?
You can usually make the first contact for a relative or friend, especially if their English is limited. However, the adviser will need to speak to the person themselves, confirm their identity and get their consent before giving advice about their situation. If an interpreter is needed, tell the adviser so they can arrange one.
What if I need to cancel or move my appointment?
Contact the adviser as early as you can. Many advisers have a cancellation policy, and some charge if an appointment is cancelled at short notice, so check their terms when you book. If you have a deadline, try to rebook quickly rather than leaving a gap. Explaining why you need to move the appointment can help the adviser find another slot that works for you.
What if I do not have all my documents?
Bring what you have. Many people do not have every document, especially for older applications. The adviser can often work with what is available and explain how missing items might be obtained. Do not delay booking a consultation just because something is missing, particularly if there is a deadline. Make a note of anything you know is missing so you can tell the adviser.
Should I bring original documents or copies?
It depends on what the adviser asks for. Many advisers are happy to work from clear copies, scans or photos for a first consultation. For in person meetings, some ask to see originals to check them. Keep your originals safe and only hand them over if there is a clear reason. If you do leave originals with an adviser, ask for a written record.
How do I get copies of my old applications?
If you made an application online, you may be able to see some information through your online account. If you used an adviser before, they should have a copy on file and you can ask for it. In some cases you can request information the Home Office holds about you through a subject access request. A regulated adviser can explain the options.
What is a UKVI account and do I need access to it?
A UKVI account is an online account that many people now use to view and prove their immigration status in digital form, often called an eVisa. If you have one, being able to log in and show your status during the consultation can be helpful. If you have trouble accessing your account, mention this to the adviser. Official help is available through GOV.UK.
Do documents in another language need translating?
For the consultation itself, the adviser may be able to work with documents in another language, especially if they or their interpreter speak it. For any application, certified translations are often needed. Ask the adviser before the meeting whether you should arrange translations. They may be able to recommend a translation service and tell you what is required.
Is it safe to send my documents by email?
Many advisers use secure portals or encrypted upload links for documents, which are generally safer than ordinary email. If an adviser asks you to send documents, ask how they would like to receive them. Before sending anything, make sure you have checked that the adviser is regulated and that the contact details are genuine, as scammers sometimes pose as advisers.
Should I write down my address history?
Many people find it helpful to list where they have lived, with approximate dates, both in the UK and elsewhere. Advisers often ask about this, and it can be hard to remember on the spot. Include any periods spent with friends or family. You do not need to know whether it matters for your situation. The adviser will decide what is relevant, and having it ready saves time.
What will the adviser ask me?
Advisers usually ask about your identity, your history in the UK and elsewhere, your previous applications and decisions, your family and other circumstances relevant to your matter, and what you want to achieve. Some questions may feel personal or repetitive. They help the adviser build an accurate picture. You can always ask why a question matters if you are unsure.
Will what I say be kept confidential?
Regulated advisers have strict duties to keep client information confidential. Solicitors' communications are also usually protected by legal privilege. There are limited exceptions set by law, and the adviser can explain these. Being open with your adviser is important, because they can only give accurate advice based on full information.
What if I get upset during the meeting?
Immigration matters can be stressful and emotional, and advisers understand this. If you need a moment, it is fine to say so. You can also ask for a short break or for the adviser to slow down. If you think you might find the meeting difficult, you could bring a supportive person or ask for a longer appointment.
Will the adviser tell me if my application will succeed?
A good adviser will give you an honest view of the issues they see, but no regulated adviser can guarantee a result. Decisions are made by the Home Office or a tribunal. Be cautious of anyone who promises a particular outcome. Your adviser will explain what they think the risks and options are, based on what they know.
Can I record the consultation?
Some people find it helpful to record a meeting so they can listen again later. If you want to do this, ask the adviser first. Some may agree, while others prefer to give you a written summary instead. Taking your own notes and asking for key points in writing are good alternatives.
What if I do not understand something the adviser says?
Ask them to explain it again in a different way. Advisers should use plain language and check that you understand. If English is not your first language, an interpreter can make a big difference. Asking questions is a normal part of a consultation and helps make sure you leave with a clear understanding of what was discussed.
I have received a letter from the Home Office. What should I do?
Read it carefully and look for any date by which something must be done. Keep a note of the date you received it. Contact a regulated adviser as soon as possible and tell them about the letter and any date in it. Send them a copy if they ask. Leaflegal does not give immigration advice, but we can introduce you to a regulated adviser.
My visa is about to expire. How quickly can I see someone?
Tell the adviser about the date straight away when you first make contact. Many advisers can arrange an urgent consultation if needed. The sooner an adviser knows about a date, the more options they may be able to explore. Do not wait until you have every document ready before getting in touch.
What if I have a deadline but cannot afford an adviser?
Free help does exist, though it can be hard to get quickly. Some charities and law centres advise without charge, and for certain immigration and asylum matters legal aid may cover the cost. Official guidance on legal aid is on GOV.UK. Because these services are busy, contact them early and explain your deadline when you do. Leaflegal introduces people to regulated advisers who usually charge for their work, so they may not suit everyone.
Someone I know has been detained. Can I arrange a consultation for them?
You can contact an adviser on their behalf and explain the situation. The adviser will usually need to speak to the person directly and get their consent to act. Many advisers handle urgent detention matters, and some charities specialise in them. Give the adviser as much information as you can, including the person's full name, date of birth and where they are held.
What if I am asked to attend an interview?
If you receive an invitation to an interview with the Home Office, note the date, time and place and anything you are asked to bring. Contact a regulated adviser as soon as you can and send them a copy of the invitation. They can explain what the interview is likely to involve in general terms and how they might help you prepare. If you cannot attend on the date given, tell your adviser straight away.
What happens if I decide to instruct the adviser?
The adviser will usually send you their terms of business or a client care letter setting out what they will do, the fees and how they will keep in touch. You will usually need to sign it and pay a fee or money on account. They will then tell you what they need from you next. Keep copies of everything you sign.
What if I want to think about it first?
That is your choice, unless there is an urgent deadline. Many people take time to think, talk to family or speak to more than one adviser. If you have a deadline, ask the adviser how long you can safely take to decide. They can tell you what timing issues there are, in general terms, for your matter.

Leaflegal's role is to make the introduction. Once we have introduced you to a regulated firm or adviser, your relationship is directly with them. They will contact you, explain their fees and agree terms with you. We do not take part in your matter and do not give immigration advice. What should I do if my circumstances change after the consultation?

Who is allowed to give immigration advice?
In the UK, immigration advice can only be given by people who are regulated to do so. That includes solicitors regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, some other regulated legal professionals, and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, which was formerly the OISC. Giving immigration advice without being regulated is a criminal offence.
What is the Immigration Advice Authority?
It is the body that regulates immigration advisers who are not solicitors or other authorised legal professionals. It was previously called the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner, or OISC. It keeps a public register of advisers and the level of work each one is registered to do.
How can I check an adviser is regulated?
You can search for a solicitor or firm on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register, and for an adviser on the Immigration Advice Authority register. Both are free to use online. A genuine adviser will be happy to give you their registration details if you ask.
Is a solicitor better than an immigration adviser?
Not necessarily. Both can be highly experienced, and what matters most is that the person is regulated, has the right level of authorisation for your matter and has experience of cases like yours. Fees and ways of working can differ, so it is worth asking questions before you decide.
What if I am unhappy with my adviser?
Regulated firms and advisers must have a complaints procedure, and you can ask for a copy. If you are still unhappy after using it, you may be able to take your complaint further, for example to the Legal Ombudsman or to the adviser's regulator. The firm should explain how this works at the start.
Can friends or community groups help me?
People can help in practical ways, such as translating or gathering documents, but they should not give immigration advice unless they are regulated. Some charities and community organisations employ regulated advisers and can give advice. If you are unsure, ask whether the person is registered.
What questions should I ask an adviser?
Many people ask how often the adviser handles matters like theirs, who will work on the matter day to day, what the fee is and what it includes, whether government fees are extra, how they will keep in touch and what happens if the Home Office has questions. A good adviser will welcome these questions.
