What happens at a motoring offence hearing

A plain English guide to your first hearing.

Key points

  • Get advice before the hearing, especially if a ban is possible
  • Arrive early and bring your paperwork
  • The court may sentence you on the same day

Before the hearing

Read your court papers carefully and get advice before the hearing, especially if a ban is possible.

On the day

Arrive early. Your case will be called, you will be asked to confirm your details and enter a plea. If you plead guilty, the court may sentence you the same day.

How a solicitor helps

  • Checking the evidence and procedure
  • Advising on plea
  • Putting forward mitigation or an exceptional hardship argument

What to bring

  • Your court papers and any letters from the police
  • Your driving licence
  • Details of your income and outgoings, as fines are linked to income
  • Any letters supporting an exceptional hardship argument

Possible outcomes

Depending on the offence, the court can impose a fine, add penalty points or disqualify you from driving. For more serious offences other sentences are possible. A solicitor can explain what is likely in your case and how to put forward your side.

Exceptional hardship

If you reach 12 points, the court will usually ban you for at least six months. You can argue that a ban would cause exceptional hardship, for example to people who rely on you. These arguments need careful preparation and evidence.

Tip

If you can’t attend on the date given, contact the court and your solicitor as soon as possible.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. A solicitor can advise on your situation.

Being told to attend court for a motoring offence can be worrying, especially if you have never been to court before. Most motoring cases in England and Wales are dealt with in the magistrates' court, and many are short hearings. Knowing what happens and who you will meet can make the day much less daunting.

Our short guide above covers the basics of the first hearing. This page goes further, explaining how cases reach court, what the court building and courtroom are like, how pleas and trials work, how sentencing usually happens and what happens afterwards. It is general information. A motoring solicitor will look at your own case and explain what applies to you.

How motoring cases reach court

Many minor motoring matters are dealt with by a fixed penalty or a course and never reach court. Others go to court because the offence is more serious, because a fixed penalty was not offered or accepted, or because the driver would reach 12 points. A case might start with a postal requisition or summons through the post, or with being charged at a police station and given a date to attend.

The single justice procedure

Many less serious motoring offences are dealt with first under the single justice procedure. You receive a notice explaining the allegation and can respond online or by post, pleading guilty or not guilty and giving information about your circumstances. A single magistrate then deals with the case on the papers, without a hearing, unless you ask for one or the magistrate decides a hearing is needed, for example because a ban is being considered.

Which court

Almost all motoring offences start in the magistrates' court. The most serious offences, such as causing death by dangerous driving, are sent to the Crown Court, where the procedure is different and there may be a jury. This guide focuses on the magistrates' court, which is where most drivers will find themselves.

Who decides

Cases are heard either by a bench of two or three magistrates, who are trained volunteers from the local community, or by a district judge, who is a legally qualified full time judge. Both are supported by a legal adviser who advises them on the law and procedure.

The days and weeks before a hearing are often the most useful time to prepare. What you do at this stage can shape how the hearing goes.

Reading the papers

Your court papers set out the offence alleged, the date and place of the hearing and, often, a summary of the prosecution evidence. Read them carefully and check the details, such as the date, time, location and vehicle. Mistakes are worth noting, although not every error affects a case.

Getting legal help

Many drivers speak to a motoring solicitor before the hearing, particularly where a ban is possible, where they want to dispute the allegation or where they plan to argue exceptional hardship or special reasons. A solicitor can review the evidence, explain the likely range of outcomes and, if instructed, attend court with you. The earlier they are involved, the more time they have to prepare.

Things to gather

  • Your court papers and any letters from the police or the court
  • Your driving licence, or its details
  • Details of your income, outgoings and any benefits
  • Any evidence that supports your account, such as photos, dashcam footage or receipts
  • Letters from employers, family members or others if a ban would affect them
  • Details of any previous motoring convictions or points

Practical arrangements

Plan how you will get to court, and remember that if a ban is possible you may not be able to drive home. Arrange time off work for the whole day, as cases can be called at any point during the session. If you need an interpreter or have a disability that affects how you take part, let the court know in advance.

If you cannot attend

If you cannot attend on the date given, contact the court as soon as possible and explain why. The court may agree to move the date, but it is not guaranteed. If you simply do not attend, the court may deal with the case in your absence or, in some circumstances, issue a warrant.

Magistrates' courts can feel busy and confusing on a first visit. Knowing the layout and the people helps.

Arriving

Arrive well before the time on your papers. You will go through a security check similar to an airport, so leave plenty of time and avoid bringing sharp items. Once inside, find the list showing which courtroom your case is in, then let the usher know you have arrived. The usher is usually in a gown and carries a clipboard.

Waiting

Several cases are usually listed for the same session, and they are not always called in order. It is common to wait for some time. If you have a solicitor, they will usually meet you before your case is called to go through the hearing. If you do not, the prosecutor or legal adviser may speak to you briefly about the case.

Who is in the courtroom

  • The magistrates or district judge, who sit at the front and make decisions
  • The legal adviser, who sits in front of them and advises on law and procedure
  • The prosecutor, often from the Crown Prosecution Service, who presents the case against you
  • Your solicitor, if you have one, who speaks on your behalf
  • The usher, who calls cases and helps people find their place
  • Sometimes probation officers, members of the public and reporters

Etiquette

Dress smartly and turn off your phone. Stand when the magistrates enter and leave and when you are speaking to them, unless told otherwise. Magistrates are addressed as sir or madam, or your worships, and a district judge as judge or sir or madam. Speak clearly and do not interrupt. If you do not understand something, say so politely.

Remote hearings

Some hearings take place partly or wholly by video. If the court tells you your hearing will be remote, it will send instructions on how to join. The same standards of behaviour apply.

At the first hearing, one of the main tasks is to take your plea. This shapes everything that follows.

Confirming your details

When your case is called, you will usually be asked to stand and confirm your name, date of birth, address and nationality. The legal adviser will then read out the allegation and ask how you plead.

Pleading guilty

If you plead guilty, the prosecutor usually outlines the facts and tells the court about any previous convictions. You or your solicitor then have the chance to put forward mitigation, which means explaining circumstances that might reduce the sentence. The court may sentence you straight away or adjourn for more information. Pleading guilty at the first opportunity usually earns some reduction in sentence, although this does not usually apply to the length of a mandatory ban.

Pleading not guilty

If you plead not guilty, the court will not usually hear the evidence that day. Instead it deals with case management, such as which witnesses are needed, what evidence is disputed and whether there are any legal issues. A trial date is then set, often some weeks or months ahead. At the trial, the prosecution presents its evidence, witnesses may be questioned and you can give evidence.

Special reasons

In some cases, a driver accepts the offence but argues there are special reasons not to add points or impose a ban. Special reasons relate to the circumstances of the offence itself, such as a genuine emergency. They are different from exceptional hardship, which relates to the effect of a ban. Special reasons arguments often involve a short hearing with evidence.

Why advice before pleading matters

The decision about plea is yours, but it can affect the outcome in important ways. A solicitor can look at the evidence in your case and explain the options before you decide.

For most motoring offences, sentencing in the magistrates' court follows published sentencing guidelines. These help the court decide where a case sits and what range of penalty applies.

What the court considers

  • The seriousness of the offence and how it was committed
  • Factors that make it more serious, such as previous convictions
  • Factors that make it less serious, such as a clean record or genuine remorse
  • Whether you pleaded guilty and when
  • Your financial circumstances, for setting any fine
  • Your existing points and driving record

Types of penalty

Common penalties include a fine, penalty points on your licence and disqualification from driving. Most fines are linked to income, which is why the court asks for details of your finances. You may also be ordered to pay prosecution costs and a surcharge. For more serious offences, the court can impose community orders or, for some offences, custody. The amounts and ranges are set by guidelines that change from time to time.

Disqualification

Some offences carry a mandatory ban, such as drink driving. For others, a ban is discretionary. Reaching 12 or more points within three years usually leads to a minimum six month totting up ban, unless the court accepts an exceptional hardship argument. New drivers who reach six points within two years of passing their test face having their licence revoked by the DVLA.

Exceptional hardship hearings

Arguing exceptional hardship usually involves giving evidence about how a ban would affect you and, often more importantly, others such as family, employees or dependants. The court looks for hardship that goes beyond the normal inconvenience of a ban. Preparation and evidence matter a great deal. A solicitor can explain what the court usually looks for.

Adjournments for reports

For some offences, the court may adjourn sentencing to get a report from the probation service. This is more common where a community order or custody is being considered.

Once the hearing is over, there are a few practical things to deal with, and in some cases further steps to consider.

Paying a fine

If you are fined, the court will explain how and when to pay. It may allow payment in instalments. If your circumstances change and you cannot pay, contact the court quickly. Not paying can lead to enforcement action.

Your licence

Points and bans are recorded against your driving record by the DVLA. If you are banned, the ban usually starts immediately, so you cannot drive away from court. For longer bans or certain offences, you may need to reapply for your licence or meet medical requirements before driving again. The court and the DVLA will explain what applies.

Drink drive rehabilitation courses

For drink driving and some related offences, the court may offer a course that can reduce the length of a ban if completed in time. Taking the course is voluntary and you pay for it.

Appeals

If you disagree with a conviction or sentence from the magistrates' court, you can usually appeal to the Crown Court. There are strict time limits, and in some cases you can ask for a ban to be suspended while an appeal is decided. A solicitor can explain whether an appeal is possible and what it would involve.

Insurance

Motoring convictions usually have to be declared to insurers for a period of time, and they can affect premiums. Your insurer can tell you what they need to know.

Questions and answers

Do I have to go to court for a motoring offence?
Not always. Many less serious offences are first dealt with under the single justice procedure, where you respond in writing or online and a magistrate decides the case without a hearing. If you receive a summons or postal requisition with a hearing date, or are charged and bailed to attend, you will normally need to go. If a ban is being considered, the court will usually ask you to attend. Your papers will explain what is required.
What is a postal requisition?
A postal requisition is a formal document sent by post that tells you that you have been charged with an offence and must attend the magistrates' court on a set date. It includes the details of the alleged offence. It is a common way for motoring cases to start. Read it carefully, note the date and contact a solicitor if you want advice before the hearing.
Can I get legal aid for a motoring offence?
Legal aid in the magistrates' court depends on a means test and an interests of justice test. For most motoring offences where custody is unlikely, legal aid is not usually available. It is more likely for serious offences that could lead to prison. The duty solicitor scheme at court also has limits for non imprisonable offences. A solicitor can tell you whether legal aid might be available in your case.
How early should I speak to a solicitor?
As early as possible. A solicitor has more options when they have time to review the evidence, request further information from the prosecution, gather supporting evidence and prepare arguments such as exceptional hardship. Some issues, such as problems with procedure, need to be raised at the right stage. Contacting a solicitor shortly after receiving your court papers gives them the best chance to prepare.
What if I think the details in my papers are wrong?
Note any errors you spot, such as wrong dates, times, places or vehicle details, and tell your solicitor or the court. Some errors make no difference, while others can matter. The prosecution may be able to correct mistakes in some situations. A solicitor can look at the papers and explain whether an error is likely to affect your case.
Can the hearing date be changed?
You can ask the court to change the date if you have a good reason, such as a medical appointment, a pre-booked holiday or a work commitment that cannot be moved. The request should be made as early as possible, with evidence. The court decides whether to agree, and it is not guaranteed. Until you hear that the date has changed, assume you still need to attend.
What time should I arrive?
Arrive well before the time on your papers, as security checks and finding the right courtroom take time. Many people aim to arrive at least half an hour early. The time on your papers is usually the start of the session, not the time your case will be heard. Cases are called in an order set by the court, and you may wait for some time.
What should I wear to court?
There is no formal dress code, but dressing smartly shows respect for the court. Many people wear what they would wear to a job interview. Avoid clothing with slogans and remove hats unless worn for religious reasons. Looking neat and taking the hearing seriously can help you feel more confident. It does not change the law, but it helps the day run smoothly.
Can I bring someone with me?
Yes. Most magistrates' court hearings are open to the public, so a friend or family member can usually come and sit in the public area. They cannot speak for you in court unless the court specifically allows it. If you are giving evidence about exceptional hardship, someone affected by a ban may also be asked to give evidence, and your solicitor will discuss this with you.
What if I need an interpreter?
If you need an interpreter, tell the court as early as possible, ideally when you receive your papers. The court will usually arrange and pay for an interpreter for the hearing. Your solicitor can also help arrange interpretation for meetings with them. Using a professional interpreter helps make sure you understand everything that happens and can take full part.
How long will the hearing take?
The hearing itself is often short, sometimes only a few minutes for a guilty plea with straightforward sentencing. Exceptional hardship arguments, special reasons hearings and trials take longer. However, you may wait for much of the morning or afternoon before your case is called. Plan to be at court for the whole session and arrange childcare and time off work accordingly.
Will there be reporters at my hearing?
Magistrates' court hearings are generally public, and reporters can attend and report on cases, including names and the outcome. In practice, most routine motoring cases attract little attention. Court lists, including names, are also published. There are limited situations where reporting is restricted. A solicitor can explain the position if you are concerned.
What happens if I plead guilty?
If you plead guilty, the prosecutor summarises the facts and your record, and you or your solicitor can put forward mitigation. The court may then sentence you that day or adjourn for more information. Pleading guilty at the first opportunity usually brings some reduction in the sentence, but not usually in the length of a mandatory ban. A solicitor can explain how this works for your offence.
What happens if I plead not guilty?
The court will usually set a trial date rather than hearing evidence on the first day. It will deal with case management, such as which witnesses are needed and what evidence is agreed. You may be asked about your availability. At the trial, the prosecution presents its case, witnesses may be questioned and you can give evidence. The court then decides whether the offence is proved.
What are special reasons?
Special reasons are circumstances connected with the offence itself that may allow the court not to add points or not to impose a ban, even though the offence is admitted or proved. A typical example is driving a short distance in a genuine emergency. They are different from exceptional hardship. Special reasons arguments usually need evidence and careful preparation, and a solicitor can explain whether they might apply.
Can I change my plea later?
Changing from not guilty to guilty is usually straightforward and can be done at any stage, although the reduction for a guilty plea may be smaller the later it is entered. Changing from guilty to not guilty is much harder and is only allowed in limited circumstances, with the court's permission. Speaking to a solicitor before entering a plea helps avoid this situation.
Do I need a solicitor for a trial?
You can represent yourself at a trial, and some people do. However, trials involve rules about evidence, questioning witnesses and legal arguments that can be difficult without training. A solicitor can prepare the case, deal with the prosecution and present your defence. Whether to instruct one is your decision, and many firms offer fixed fees for trials.
What is mitigation?
Mitigation is information put before the court to explain the circumstances of the offence and of the person being sentenced, with the aim of reducing the sentence. It might cover why the offence happened, a clean driving record, remorse, steps taken since, and personal or work circumstances. It does not excuse the offence. A solicitor can help decide what is relevant and present it clearly and briefly to the court.
How are fines worked out?
Fines in the magistrates' court are usually based on sentencing guidelines that link the fine to the seriousness of the offence and your weekly income. That is why the court asks about your finances. You may also have to pay prosecution costs and a surcharge. The bands and amounts change from time to time. A solicitor can explain how the guidelines are likely to apply to your offence.
When does a ban start?
A ban usually starts immediately, from the moment the court imposes it. That means you cannot drive away from court. If you think a ban is possible, arrange other transport for the journey home. In some cases, if you appeal, you can ask the court to suspend the ban until the appeal is decided, but this is not automatic.
What is an interim disqualification?
Sometimes, when a court adjourns sentencing for an offence that carries a ban, it can disqualify the driver in the meantime. This is called an interim disqualification. Any time spent banned under an interim order is usually taken into account when the final ban is decided. A solicitor can explain whether this is likely in your case.
What is a totting up ban?
A totting up ban usually happens when a driver reaches 12 or more penalty points within three years. The minimum ban is normally six months, and it can be longer for drivers with previous totting up bans. The court can decide not to ban, or to ban for a shorter period, if it accepts that a ban would cause exceptional hardship. Strong evidence is usually needed.
Can I go to prison for a motoring offence?
Most motoring offences cannot lead to prison, but some can, such as drink driving, driving while disqualified and dangerous driving. Even for these offences, custody is generally reserved for more serious cases. If prison is possible, the court may ask for a probation report first. A solicitor can explain the realistic range of sentences for your offence.
Do new drivers face different rules?
Yes. Under the new drivers rules, if a driver reaches six or more penalty points within two years of passing their first practical driving test, the DVLA will usually revoke their licence. They then have to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again. The court does not decide revocation, but the points it imposes can trigger it. A solicitor can explain how this might affect a particular case.
How do I pay my fine?
The court will explain how to pay, usually online, by phone or by post. You may be given a deadline or allowed to pay in instalments. If your circumstances change and you cannot keep up payments, contact the court as soon as possible. Ignoring a fine can lead to enforcement, such as deductions from wages or benefits, or enforcement agents.
Can I appeal against a ban or conviction?
Yes, in most cases you can appeal from the magistrates' court to the Crown Court against conviction, sentence or both. There is a strict time limit, usually 21 days from sentence. You may also be able to ask for a ban to be suspended while the appeal is decided. Appeals involve risks as well as possible benefits. A solicitor can explain whether an appeal is worth considering.
Will my conviction show up on background checks?
Motoring convictions are criminal convictions and can appear on some background checks while they are unspent. How long they remain unspent depends on the sentence. Endorsements also stay on your driving record for set periods. Insurers usually ask about recent motoring convictions. GOV.UK has official information about spent convictions and driving records.

When do penalty points come off my licence?
Penalty points stay on your driving record for a set period, which depends on the offence. For many offences they count towards totting up for three years from the date of the offence, but remain on the record for longer. Some serious offences, such as drink driving, stay on the record for much longer. GOV.UK explains the periods for each type of endorsement, and you can check your own record online.

More questions people ask

What is a notice of intended prosecution?
It is a formal warning that you may be prosecuted for an offence such as speeding. It is usually sent to the registered keeper within 14 days of the offence, unless you were warned at the time or there was an accident. It normally comes with a request to name the driver. A solicitor can check whether it was served correctly.
How long do I have to name the driver?
The request usually gives 28 days to reply. Failing to name the driver without a good reason is a separate offence that carries six points, more than a typical speeding offence. If you are not sure who was driving, or the car is used by several people, it is worth getting advice before the deadline passes.
What if the notice arrived after 14 days?
The 14 days are counted from the offence to the date the notice is treated as served on the registered keeper. If the registered keeper is a company or hire firm, later notices to the named driver can lawfully arrive after 14 days. There are also exceptions, such as accidents. A solicitor can look at the dates and explain whether they matter.
What if I was not the registered keeper?
The notice goes to the registered keeper shown on DVLA records. If you sold the car, moved house without updating your details, or drive a company or hire vehicle, the paperwork can take an unusual route. Keep everything you receive and note the dates. A solicitor can explain how the rules apply to your situation.
What if I cannot remember who was driving?
The law expects the keeper to use reasonable diligence to find out who was driving. If, after genuine efforts, you still cannot say, there may be a defence to the offence of failing to name the driver. It is narrow and depends on the steps taken. A solicitor can explain what is expected and what evidence may be needed.
Can I ask to see the photo?
Some police forces will provide a photograph if you ask, often to help identify the driver. They are not always required to, and many camera photos are taken from behind. Asking for a photo does not usually extend the deadline to name the driver, so it is important to keep track of the date.
What if the car is a company car?
Where a company is the registered keeper, the company must name the driver. The notice to the driver then follows. Companies that do not keep proper records of who uses their vehicles can find it hard to respond, and the company itself can be prosecuted for failing to name the driver. Many fleet managers ask a solicitor to review their process.
How many points will I get for speeding?
Speeding carries between three and six points. A fixed penalty is normally three points. If the case goes to court, the number of points depends on how far over the limit you were, along with other factors, using the Sentencing Council guideline. For higher speeds the court can impose a short ban instead of points.
Can I be banned for speeding?
Yes, in two main ways. The court can impose a discretionary ban for the speeding offence itself, usually where the speed was well above the limit. Or the points for the offence can take you to 12 or more within three years, which normally means a totting up ban of at least six months.
Who decides whether I get a speed awareness course?
The police decide, following national guidance. A course is usually offered where the speed was not much above the limit and you have not done a similar course recently. You cannot insist on one, and it is not available once a case has gone to court. If you are offered a course, you pay the provider directly.
Does a speed awareness course go on my record?
Completing a course means no points and no conviction. The police keep a record that you attended, which is used to decide whether you can be offered another course. Some insurers ask whether you have attended a course, so it is worth answering their questions accurately.
How is the fine worked out?
If the case goes to court, the fine is based on the seriousness of the offence and your weekly income, following the Sentencing Council guideline. You will be asked for details of your finances. If you do not provide them, the court can make assumptions about your income. The court also adds a victim surcharge and usually prosecution costs.
What happens if I am a new driver?
If you passed your test within the last two years and reach six or more points, the DVLA will usually revoke your licence. Two speeding offences can be enough. You would then need to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again. The court cannot prevent this if the points are imposed.
Will speeding affect my insurance?
A speeding conviction or points usually needs to be declared to insurers when they ask, and it may increase premiums, particularly if there is more than one. A speed awareness course is not a conviction, but some insurers ask about courses too. Answering insurers' questions accurately protects your cover if you need to make a claim.
How long do speeding points last?
Speeding points stay on your driving record for four years from the date of the offence. For totting up purposes, the court looks at offences committed within three years of each other. So points can still show on your record for a while after they stop counting towards a totting up ban. You can check your record on GOV.UK.
