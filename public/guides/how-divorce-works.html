How divorce works

The steps in a no fault divorce, and what it does and doesn’t cover.

  • Plain English
  • General information, not advice
  • About family law
Make an Enquiry
All guides

Key points

  • No fault divorce means nobody has to be blamed
  • It takes at least 26 weeks from application to final order
  • Money and children are dealt with separately from the divorce itself

No fault divorce

Since April 2022, you only need to state that the marriage has broken down irretrievably. You can apply alone or jointly.

The timeline

  • Application
  • At least 20 weeks, then the conditional order
  • At least six weeks and one day more, then the final order

Money and children

The divorce ends the marriage but doesn’t deal with money or children. A financial order, often a consent order, makes a settlement binding. Many people take advice before applying for the final order.

Applying alone or together

You can apply on your own, as a sole applicant, or together with your spouse as joint applicants. A joint application can feel less confrontational, but both of you need to agree to go ahead at each stage.

Why finances matter

A divorce does not end financial claims between you. Without a financial order, either of you could make a claim in the future. A consent order records what you have agreed and makes it legally binding, and a solicitor can help you prepare one.

Arrangements for children

Many parents agree arrangements for their children between themselves or through mediation. If you can’t agree, the court can make a child arrangements order. Courts usually expect you to consider mediation first.

Tip

Speak to a family solicitor before you apply for the final order if your finances are not yet sorted.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. A solicitor can advise on your situation.

Divorce is the legal process that ends a marriage. In England and Wales it is handled by the family court, and since 6 April 2022 it has worked on a no fault basis. That means nobody has to prove adultery, behaviour or separation, and nobody has to be blamed in the paperwork.

Our short guide above covers the basics of no fault divorce and the overall timeline. This page goes into more depth on each stage, what the forms and orders actually do, what happens if the other person does not respond and how money and children fit around the divorce itself. It is general information. A family solicitor will look at your own situation and explain what applies to you.

What the divorce does and does not do

The divorce process changes your legal status. When the final order is made, you are no longer married and are free to remarry. On its own, though, the divorce does not divide property, savings or pensions, set maintenance or decide where children live. Those issues are dealt with separately, either by agreement or through other court applications, and often run alongside the divorce.

Before you can apply

A divorce application cannot be made until the marriage has lasted at least one year. The court also needs a legal connection to England and Wales, usually based on where one or both of you live or are domiciled. Same sex marriages are ended through the same divorce process, and civil partnerships are ended through a very similar process called dissolution.

Words you will come across

  • Applicant, the person who applies, or applicants if you apply jointly
  • Respondent, the other spouse in a sole application
  • Conditional order, which used to be called the decree nisi
  • Final order, which used to be called the decree absolute
  • Acknowledgement of service, the respondent's reply to the application
  • Financial order, a separate court order dealing with money and property

Most divorces now start online through the GOV.UK divorce service. Solicitors use a professional version of the same system. Paper applications are still possible in some cases. The stages are the same whichever way you apply.

1. The application

The application includes basic details of both spouses and the marriage, and a statement that the marriage has broken down irretrievably. The court treats that statement as conclusive proof. You will need your marriage certificate, or a certified copy, and a certified translation if it is not in English. A court fee is payable unless you qualify for help with fees. The fee changes from time to time, so check GOV.UK for the current amount.

2. Issue

The court checks the application and issues it, which means it is formally started. The date of issue matters because the 20 week waiting period runs from it.

3. Service and response

In a sole application, the application must be sent to the respondent. The court usually does this by email and post, though the applicant can choose to arrange it. The respondent is asked to complete an acknowledgement of service, confirming they have received it and whether they intend to dispute the divorce. In a joint application there is no respondent, so this step is simpler.

4. The 20 week period

There is a minimum period of 20 weeks between the application being issued and the applicant being able to apply for the conditional order. It is sometimes called a period of reflection. Many couples use it to work out arrangements for money and children.

5. Conditional order

After 20 weeks, the applicant or applicants confirm they wish to continue and apply for the conditional order. A judge or legal adviser reviews the file and, if satisfied, the court pronounces the conditional order. It is an important milestone because the court can now approve a financial order, although the marriage has not yet ended.

6. Final order

At least six weeks and one day after the conditional order, the applicant can apply for the final order. Once it is made, the marriage is legally over. The next sections look at that step and the issues around it in more detail.

In a sole application, much of the process depends on the respondent receiving the papers and taking part. Most respondents do respond, but some do not, and the law has ways of dealing with that.

Acknowledging the application

The respondent is usually expected to return the acknowledgement of service within a short period after receiving the application. In it they confirm they have received the papers. They can also say if they want to dispute the divorce, though the grounds for doing so are now very limited.

Disputing a divorce

Under no fault divorce, the respondent cannot defend the divorce by arguing that the marriage has not broken down. A dispute is only possible on narrow grounds, such as the court not having jurisdiction, the marriage not being legally valid or the marriage having already ended. Disputes of this kind are uncommon and usually need legal advice on both sides.

If the respondent does not reply

If the respondent does not return the acknowledgement, the applicant may need to show the court that the papers have been received in another way. Options can include personal service by a process server or court bailiff, asking the court to accept that the papers were received based on other evidence, or asking for permission to serve another way, such as by text or social media. In rare cases the court can dispense with service altogether.

If you do not know where your spouse is

Applicants sometimes have no current address for their spouse. The court expects reasonable efforts to trace them, such as contacting family, friends, former employers or using tracing services. Evidence of those efforts can support an application for an alternative way of serving the papers. A solicitor can explain the options.

Joint applications

In a joint application both spouses are applicants. Each confirms at the conditional order stage that they want to continue. If one stops cooperating, the other can usually continue the application on their own as a sole applicant. Joint applications can feel less confrontational, but they depend on both people engaging at each step.

The final order is the moment the marriage legally ends. It sounds like a formality, but the timing of the application can have real consequences, especially for money and pensions.

When it can be applied for

The earliest an applicant can apply is six weeks and one day after the conditional order. The application is short and usually made online. In a joint application, both can apply together, or one can apply after giving the other notice.

Why some people wait

Many solicitors suggest waiting to apply for the final order until a financial order has been made, or at least agreed. This is because the end of the marriage can change rights to some pensions and other benefits that depend on being married. For example, if one spouse died after the final order but before finances were settled, the survivor might lose entitlements they would have had as a widow or widower. Each case is different, and a solicitor will look at your circumstances.

If the applicant does not apply

If the applicant does not apply for the final order, the respondent can apply once a further period has passed after the earliest date the applicant could have applied. The court may hold a hearing and can make the order or deal with any reasons for delay.

Long gaps

If more than 12 months pass between the conditional order and the application for the final order, the applicant usually has to explain the delay to the court. This happens quite often where finances take a long time to resolve.

After the final order

You receive a copy of the final order, which is the document you will need to prove you are divorced, for example to remarry or update official records. It is worth keeping it safe. Wills are affected by divorce, and many people review their will and pension nominations once the divorce is complete.

For many people, the hardest parts of separating are not the divorce itself but sorting out money, the home and arrangements for children. These run on a separate track, but the timing of each affects the other.

Financial claims stay open

A divorce does not end the financial claims spouses can make against each other. Unless a court makes a financial order dealing with those claims, either person may be able to bring a claim years later. A financial order, often a consent order where terms are agreed, can make an agreement legally binding and, where appropriate, end future claims.

Ways of reaching agreement

  • Direct discussions between the couple
  • Mediation with a trained family mediator
  • Negotiation through solicitors
  • Collaborative law, with each person and their solicitor meeting together
  • Arbitration, where an arbitrator makes a binding decision
  • An application to the court for a financial order if agreement is not possible

Before most applications to court about money or children, applicants are expected to attend a mediation information and assessment meeting, often called a MIAM, unless an exemption applies. Exemptions exist, including for domestic abuse.

Full disclosure

Agreeing finances properly usually involves each person giving full details of their income, assets, debts and pensions. In court proceedings this is done on a standard form. Pensions are often among the largest assets and can need specialist valuation.

Children

Arrangements for children do not form part of the divorce application. Many parents agree arrangements themselves or through mediation. If they cannot, either parent can apply for a child arrangements order. The court's main concern is the welfare of the children. Child maintenance is usually dealt with separately, by agreement or through the Child Maintenance Service.

Remarriage

Remarrying before financial claims have been made or dealt with can prevent a person from bringing some of their own financial claims against their former spouse. A solicitor can explain how this works.

The cost of a divorce depends far more on the money and children issues than on the divorce itself. The divorce process on its own is largely administrative when both people engage.

What you may pay for

  • The court fee for the divorce application, unless help with fees applies
  • Solicitor's fees, if you instruct one, often as a fixed fee for the divorce
  • Mediation fees, if you use a mediator
  • Fees for preparing and filing a consent order, and the court fee for it
  • Costs of valuing pensions, property or a business, if needed
  • Costs of any contested court proceedings, which can be significant

Doing it yourself or using a solicitor

Many people complete the divorce application themselves online, especially when things are amicable. Others prefer a solicitor to handle the process, particularly where there are complex finances, international elements, concerns about safety or a spouse who is unlikely to cooperate. Some people use a solicitor only for specific parts, such as advice on a financial agreement or drafting a consent order.

Support if you feel unsafe

If you are worried about your safety or that of your children, help is available. The court can make protective orders, and specialist organisations offer confidential support. In an emergency, call 999.

Questions and answers

How long do I have to be married before I can divorce?
You cannot apply for a divorce in England and Wales until the marriage has lasted at least one year. This is a fixed rule. If you have been married for less than a year and the relationship has broken down, there are other options, such as a separation agreement or, in some circumstances, annulment. A family solicitor can explain what might be possible in your situation.
Do I need a reason for the divorce?
No. Under no fault divorce, you only need to state that the marriage has broken down irretrievably. You do not have to give details of what went wrong, and the other spouse does not have to agree that it has broken down. The statement is treated as conclusive. This replaced the old system where one person often had to blame the other.
What documents do I need to apply?
You will need your marriage certificate or a certified copy, and a certified translation if it is not in English. You will also need your spouse's full name and, ideally, their current postal and email address. If you have changed your name since marrying, you may need to explain this. Having details of your own and your spouse's addresses during the marriage can also be helpful.
Can I apply for divorce if I married abroad?
Often yes, as long as the court in England and Wales has jurisdiction, which usually depends on where you and your spouse live or are domiciled. You will need your foreign marriage certificate and, if it is not in English, a certified translation. International cases can raise extra questions, such as whether another country's courts could also deal with the divorce. A solicitor can explain the position.
Should we apply jointly or should one of us apply?
Both options lead to the same result. A joint application can feel more cooperative and avoids one person being the applicant and the other the respondent. A sole application gives the applicant control over the timing of each step. Which suits a couple depends on how well they communicate and how likely both are to keep engaging. A solicitor can talk through the practical differences.
Is there an alternative to divorce?
Yes. Some couples choose judicial separation, which lets the court make some financial orders without ending the marriage. People sometimes choose this for religious reasons or because they are not yet ready to divorce. Others simply separate informally or make a separation agreement. In limited circumstances a marriage can be annulled. A family solicitor can explain how each option works.
How long does a divorce take?
The legal minimum from issue of the application to the final order is a little over 26 weeks, made up of the 20 week period before the conditional order and the six weeks and one day before the final order. In practice, court processing times, delays in serving papers and the time taken to agree finances often mean it takes longer. Many people deliberately wait before applying for the final order.
What is the 20 week reflection period for?
The 20 week period gives both people time to reflect on the decision and, where the marriage is ending, to start sorting out practical arrangements such as finances and children. It is a minimum. The applicant cannot apply for the conditional order until it has passed, even if both spouses agree. Many couples use this time to attend mediation or begin financial negotiations.
What is the difference between a conditional order and a final order?
The conditional order is the court's confirmation that the applicant is entitled to a divorce. The marriage still exists at that point. It allows the court to approve a financial order. The final order is what actually ends the marriage. These replaced the old terms decree nisi and decree absolute when no fault divorce came in.
Do I have to go to court?
Usually not for the divorce itself. Most divorces are dealt with on paper or online without any hearing. Hearings can happen if there is a dispute about jurisdiction or the validity of the marriage, or a problem with service. Court hearings are more likely in financial or children proceedings if agreement cannot be reached. A solicitor can explain whether a hearing is likely in your case.
Can I stop a divorce once it has started?
Yes. A sole applicant can withdraw the application before the conditional order, and in some circumstances later on. Joint applicants can both decide not to proceed. If you reconcile, you do not have to complete the divorce. The application will eventually lapse if no further steps are taken. A solicitor can explain how to formally stop the process if that is what you want.
What happens if I lose my final order?
You can usually ask the court for a copy of your final order, sometimes for a fee. You will need details such as your case number and names. If you applied online, you may be able to download it from your account. It is a good idea to keep both a paper and a digital copy, because you will need it if you remarry or to prove your marital status.
What if my spouse will not respond?
If the respondent does not return the acknowledgement of service, you may need to show the court the papers were received in another way. That can include personal service by a process server or bailiff, or asking the court to accept other evidence, such as a message showing they have seen the papers. The court can also allow alternative methods of service. A solicitor can explain which option might suit your case.
Can my spouse stop the divorce?
Not by arguing that the marriage has not broken down. The statement of irretrievable breakdown is conclusive. A respondent can only dispute the divorce on narrow grounds, such as lack of jurisdiction, the marriage not being valid or the marriage having already ended. Disputes like this are uncommon. A respondent who is unhappy can still raise issues about finances or children separately.
What if I do not know where my spouse lives?
The court expects reasonable efforts to find a spouse, such as checking with relatives, friends, former employers or using tracing agents. If those efforts fail, you can ask the court to allow service by another method, such as email or social media, or in exceptional cases to dispense with service. Evidence of the steps you have taken is important. A solicitor can help with this process.
My spouse has applied for divorce. What should I do with the papers?
Read them carefully and note any deadlines, especially for returning the acknowledgement of service. The papers will explain how to respond online or by post. Responding does not mean you agree with everything your spouse wants on money or children. Those are dealt with separately. Many respondents find it useful to speak to a family solicitor about where they stand.
Can my spouse and I use the same solicitor?
No. A solicitor cannot act for both spouses in a divorce because their interests may conflict, particularly over money and children. Each spouse can have their own solicitor, or one can be represented while the other is not. Couples who want to keep things cooperative sometimes use a mediator, who is neutral, alongside separate solicitors for advice.
Does the divorce split our money and property?
No. The divorce only ends the marriage. Dividing money, property, pensions and debts is a separate matter. Couples can agree terms between themselves, through mediation or through solicitors, and then ask the court to approve a consent order. If they cannot agree, either can apply to the court for a financial order. Without an order, financial claims may remain open.
What is a consent order?
A consent order is a court order that records a financial agreement between divorcing spouses. Once approved by a judge, it is legally binding and enforceable, and it can include a clean break that ends future claims. It can only be approved after the conditional order. Each person usually has to provide basic financial information so the court can check the terms look fair.
Who stays in the family home?
There is no automatic rule. Who stays depends on the agreement reached or, if a court decides, on factors such as the needs of any children, each person's housing needs and resources, and ownership of the home. Options include selling, one person buying the other out or one person staying for a period. A family solicitor can explain how these options work in your case.
What happens to pensions in a divorce?
Pensions are often among a couple's largest assets and are considered alongside other property. Options include pension sharing, where part of one person's pension is transferred to the other, offsetting against other assets, or attachment orders. Some pensions need specialist valuation. Because the final order can affect pension rights, many solicitors suggest finances are dealt with first. Your solicitor will explain the options.
Why might I be told to wait before applying for the final order?
Ending the marriage can affect rights that depend on being a spouse, such as some pension benefits and inheritance on death. If finances have not been agreed or approved by the court, some solicitors suggest waiting until they are. The right timing depends on your circumstances. A family solicitor will look at your situation and explain any risks.
Does a divorce affect my will?
In England and Wales, a final order of divorce usually means any gift to the former spouse in a will takes effect as if they had died, and any appointment of them as executor is treated the same way. The rest of the will still stands. Separation on its own does not have this effect. Many people review their will, life insurance and pension nominations during and after a divorce. A solicitor can explain the effect on your own will.
Does the divorce decide where the children live?
No. Arrangements for children are dealt with separately from the divorce. Many parents agree between themselves or through mediation. If they cannot agree, either parent can apply to the court for a child arrangements order. Before applying, most parents are expected to attend a mediation information meeting unless an exemption applies. The court focuses on the welfare of the children.
How is child maintenance arranged?
Many parents agree child maintenance between themselves. If they cannot, the Child Maintenance Service can calculate and collect it. The court can deal with child maintenance in some situations, for example by recording an agreement in a consent order. GOV.UK has official information about how maintenance is calculated. A solicitor can explain how it fits with other financial arrangements.
What if I am worried about my safety?
If you are in immediate danger, call 999. If you are worried about abuse, the family court can make protective orders such as non molestation orders and occupation orders, and specialist organisations offer confidential support. Mediation exemptions often apply where there has been domestic abuse. A family solicitor can explain the protection available and how it fits with the divorce.
How much does a divorce cost?
The court fee for the divorce application is set by the government and changes from time to time, and help with fees may be available. Solicitors' fees vary depending on how much help you want. The biggest costs usually come from financial or children disputes, especially if they go to court. Ask any firm for a written estimate of their fees and what they include.

The firm pays us a fee for the introduction, which never changes what you pay the firm. We are not a law firm and do not give legal advice.
Can I get legal aid for a divorce?
Legal aid for divorce and financial matters is now very limited. It may be available in some cases involving domestic abuse or child abuse, subject to financial eligibility and evidence. Legal aid for mediation is more widely available, and there is a government scheme that has offered vouchers towards mediation about children and some financial issues. Rules change, so GOV.UK has the current position. A solicitor can tell you whether they offer legal aid work.

More questions people ask

What is a separation agreement?
It is a written agreement between a married couple or civil partners who have separated. It sets out how the home, money, debts and arrangements for children will be dealt with while they live apart. It is a private contract rather than a court order, so it works differently from the orders made in a divorce or dissolution.
Is a separation agreement legally binding?
It is a contract, but a court is not automatically bound by it if one of you later applies for a financial order. The courts have said a properly made agreement can carry significant weight. That is more likely where both people gave full disclosure, took independent advice and signed freely, and where the terms are fair. A solicitor can explain how this applies to you.
Do we have to be separated for a set time first?
No. There is no minimum period of separation before you can make an agreement. Many couples make one soon after they decide to live apart, so that everyone knows where they stand from the start. Some couples also make an agreement before one of them moves out, to plan how the move will happen. A solicitor can explain what to think about at each stage.
Can we be separated while living under the same roof?
Yes, some couples separate but stay in the same home for practical or financial reasons. An agreement can still record the arrangements, including who uses which rooms and who pays which bills. Your solicitor can talk through how this would work in practice. If you later divorce, the date you separated may matter for some purposes.
Is it the same as a divorce?
No. You remain married or in a civil partnership. A separation agreement simply records the arrangements while you live apart. You can still divorce or dissolve your civil partnership later if you choose to. Staying married keeps some legal ties in place, such as rights under a will or pension, which is why many people take advice on what that means for them.
Does it make the divorce happen automatically later?
No. A separation agreement does not start or end any legal process. If you decide to divorce or dissolve your civil partnership, that is a separate application. Since April 2022 the process is no fault, so a period of separation is not needed to apply. Your agreement can still be useful at that point.
Can we write our own separation agreement?
You can, and templates are available. The risk is that a homemade agreement may leave out important points, use unclear wording or lack the safeguards a court looks for. Many couples who start with their own draft ask solicitors to check it and put it into proper form. That can be a cost effective middle ground.
Can civil partners make a separation agreement?
Yes. Civil partners can make a separation agreement in the same way as married couples. It records how the home, money, debts and children will be dealt with while you live apart, and the same safeguards apply. A solicitor can explain how it would work for you. It can later form the basis of a consent order on dissolution.
