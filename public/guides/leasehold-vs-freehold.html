Leasehold vs freehold

What you actually own, and why it matters.

  • Plain English
  • General information, not advice
  • About lease extensions
Get matched
All guides

Key points

  • Freehold means you own the building and the land
  • Leasehold means you own the right to live there for a set number of years
  • Lease length affects value and whether lenders will lend

Freehold

If you own the freehold, you own the building and the land it stands on, with no time limit.

Leasehold

If you own a leasehold, you own the right to occupy the property for the length of the lease. Most flats are leasehold. You usually pay ground rent and a service charge, and the lease sets rules about what you can do.

Why lease length matters

As a lease gets shorter, the property can become harder to sell or mortgage. Many leaseholders have the right to extend their lease or buy the freehold with their neighbours.

The law on leasehold is changing. A solicitor can tell you what applies to your property now.

Ground rent and service charges

Leaseholders usually pay ground rent to the freeholder and a service charge towards the upkeep of shared areas, such as the roof, stairs and gardens. Your lease says how these are worked out and when they can go up.

Share of freehold

In some buildings the flat owners own the freehold together, often through a company. This is called a share of freehold. It gives you more control over how the building is run, but you also share the responsibilities.

Buying a leasehold property

  • Check how many years are left on the lease
  • Ask for the latest service charge accounts and any planned major works
  • Check the ground rent and how it can increase
  • Ask your conveyancer to explain any restrictions in the lease
Tip

If the lease has fewer than about 90 years left, speak to a solicitor about extending it before you sell or remortgage.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. A solicitor can advise on your situation.

Fees and costs

A lease extension usually involves two professionals. A solicitor handles the legal process, and a surveyor values the premium you pay the freeholder. Each will quote separately, and many solicitors work with surveyors they can recommend.

At present, if you use the statutory route you usually also pay the freeholder’s reasonable legal and valuation costs. The law on leasehold is changing, so ask the firm which rules apply to your lease and how that affects the overall cost.

Things to know

Timing

The fewer years left, the more an extension usually costs. Many owners start the process well before the lease falls to 80 years.

Costs

As well as legal fees, there is usually a premium to pay the freeholder and a surveyor’s valuation. The law in this area is changing, so ask the firm how recent reforms affect you.

Statutory or informal

A statutory extension follows a set legal process. An informal deal with the freeholder can be quicker but may come with less favourable terms.

Everything you need to know

Pick a topic to read more.

In England and Wales, owning a home does not always mean owning it outright and forever. Most property is held either as freehold or as leasehold, and the difference affects what you own, who you answer to, what you pay each year and how easy the property is to sell or borrow against.

The short version is that a freeholder owns the land and buildings with no end date, while a leaseholder owns a lease, which is a right to occupy the property for a fixed number of years under terms set out in a legal document. Our short guide above covers those basics. This page goes further into how each works in daily life, what to look for and how the law is changing.

Why the difference exists

Leasehold has long been used for flats because a building with many homes stacked on top of each other needs someone to look after the shared structure, such as the roof, foundations and stairs. The freeholder, who is the landlord, keeps ownership of the building and grants long leases of each flat. The leases set out who repairs what and how costs are shared.

Freehold works well for houses where each owner looks after their own building and land. Over time, though, leasehold has also been used for some houses, particularly on new build estates, and that has attracted criticism and new laws.

Commonhold

There is a third, much rarer form of ownership called commonhold. It lets owners of flats in a building each own their own unit freehold, while a company owned by all of them runs the shared parts. It has existed for many years but has hardly been used. Government reforms aim to make it more common in future, so you may hear more about it.

How to tell which you have

The title register at HM Land Registry shows whether a property is held as freehold or leasehold. It will say absolute freehold or absolute leasehold, for example, and for a leasehold title it will refer to the lease, its date and its length. A conveyancer or solicitor can obtain and explain the title for you.

If you own a leasehold property, the lease is the most important document you have. It is a contract between you and the landlord that sets out your rights and your obligations for the whole term. Leases vary a great deal, so two flats in the same street can come with very different rules.

What a lease usually covers

  • The length of the term and the date it started
  • The ground rent, if any, and whether and how it increases
  • Who is responsible for repairs to each part of the building
  • How the service charge is calculated and shared
  • Who insures the building and how the cost is recovered
  • What alterations you can make and whether you need consent
  • Rules about subletting, holiday lets, pets and noise
  • What happens if you break the terms

Covenants

The promises in a lease are called covenants. Leaseholders usually promise to pay what is due, keep their flat in good repair, not cause a nuisance and ask permission before certain changes. Landlords usually promise to insure and maintain the building and to let the leaseholder live there without interference. Breaking a covenant can lead to a claim, and in serious cases a landlord can try to end the lease, although the law gives leaseholders significant protection.

Asking for consent

Many leases say you need the landlord's consent before making structural changes, putting down hard flooring or subletting. Landlords often charge a fee for considering a request and their legal costs. Doing work without consent can cause problems when you come to sell, so solicitors usually check whether any past work was approved.

Lease length

Every lease runs down year by year. When it was first granted it may have been 99, 125 or 999 years. As the remaining term shortens, the lease becomes a less valuable asset and lenders become more cautious. Many leaseholders have a legal right to extend the lease or join with neighbours to buy the freehold, which our other guides explain.

One of the biggest practical differences between freehold and leasehold is the ongoing cost. A freehold homeowner pays for their own repairs as and when they choose. A leaseholder usually pays a share of the costs of running the whole building, decided partly by the landlord.

Service charges

The service charge covers the landlord's costs of maintaining, repairing and managing the building and shared areas, plus insurance. It can include cleaning, lighting, lifts, gardening, fire safety, management fees and contributions to a reserve fund for big future works. The lease sets out what can be charged and what share each flat pays.

Service charges are usually billed in advance based on a budget and then reconciled against actual spending at the end of the year. They can change from year to year, sometimes sharply if major works are needed. The law requires service charges to be reasonable, and for bigger works the landlord usually has to consult leaseholders first.

Ground rent

Ground rent is a yearly payment to the landlord simply for being a leaseholder. Older leases often have small fixed amounts, while some leases granted in more recent decades have ground rents that double at set intervals or rise in line with an index. For most new long residential leases granted since mid 2022, the law limits ground rent to a peppercorn, which in practice means nothing. Existing leases are affected by separate reforms.

Other costs

  • Fees for landlord consent to alterations or subletting
  • Fees charged when you sell for providing information to the buyer
  • Fees for registering a new owner or mortgage with the landlord
  • Administration charges for late payment or other events
  • Costs of extending the lease or buying the freehold

Freehold running costs

Freehold owners pay for their own upkeep and insurance. Some also pay estate charges to a management company for shared roads, open spaces or drainage on newer estates, or a rentcharge under older arrangements. These are explained in the next section.

Owning a freehold house usually means the fewest strings attached. There is no landlord, no lease that runs down and no service charge for the building itself. That does not mean there are no rules at all.

Covenants on freehold land

Freehold titles often carry restrictive covenants from when the land was first sold or developed. They might stop the land being used for business, limit extensions, ban certain types of fences or require consent from a former owner or developer for changes. Some are old and hard to enforce, while others are actively enforced. A solicitor can explain the effect of covenants on a particular title.

Estate charges

On many newer estates, roads, play areas, green spaces or drainage are not adopted by the council. Instead they are owned and run by a management company, and freehold owners pay a yearly estate charge. These charges are sometimes called fleecehold by critics. New laws give freehold owners more rights to information and to challenge unreasonable estate charges, with the detail being brought in over time.

Rentcharges

In some parts of the country, especially around Manchester and Bristol, older freehold houses can be subject to a rentcharge. This is a small annual payment to a third party. Most were set to end under earlier legislation, but some remain, and estate rentcharges are still used on some developments. A solicitor will look for them when checking a title.

Flying freeholds

A flying freehold arises where part of a freehold property sits above or below land owned by someone else, such as a room over a shared passage. These can raise questions about access for repairs and support. Some lenders are cautious about them, so a solicitor usually looks carefully at the title and any rights in place.

Leasehold houses

Some houses are sold as leasehold, which can come as a surprise to buyers. The owner of a leasehold house often has rights to buy the freehold or extend the lease, and the process is different from that for flats. Laws passed in 2024 include changes aimed at leasehold houses, which are being introduced in stages.

Leaseholders in England and Wales have a range of legal rights that go beyond what their lease says. These rights have built up through several Acts of Parliament, and the rules on who can use them and how continue to develop.

Rights over the lease and building

  • The right to extend the lease of a flat
  • The right for qualifying flat owners to buy the freehold together, known as collective enfranchisement
  • The right for owners of leasehold houses to buy the freehold or extend
  • The right to manage, which lets leaseholders take over management without buying the freehold
  • The right of first refusal if a landlord wants to sell the freehold in some cases

Rights over money

Leaseholders have the right to see summaries of service charge costs and to inspect supporting documents. They can ask the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to decide whether service charges or administration charges are reasonable and payable. For major works and long term contracts above set limits, landlords usually have to consult leaseholders before committing to the cost.

Recognised tenants' associations

Leaseholders in a building can form a recognised tenants' association. This gives the group certain rights to be consulted and to ask for information. It can make it easier to raise concerns collectively instead of one by one.

Who can use each right

Each right has its own conditions, and some have changed under recent reforms. Whether you can use a particular right depends on your lease, your building and the law in force at the time. A solicitor who handles leasehold matters can look at your own situation and explain which options might be open to you.

Leasehold law in England and Wales has been under review for years, and significant reforms are now underway. This is one of the main reasons general information about leasehold goes out of date quickly.

What has already changed

The ground rent rules for most new long residential leases have changed, limiting ground rent to a peppercorn. The Building Safety Act 2022 introduced protections for many leaseholders against the cost of fixing certain historical building safety defects. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 made further changes, some of which are already in force.

Changes being brought in

Many parts of the 2024 Act need further regulations before they take effect, and the timetable has moved more than once. Areas affected include how the price of a lease extension or freehold purchase is worked out, standard lease extension terms, service charge transparency, estate charges and the sale of new leasehold houses. Government has also consulted on further reform, including the future of leasehold for flats and the wider use of commonhold.

What this means in practice

Rules that applied to a friend's lease extension a few years ago may not apply to yours now. The cost and process can differ depending on the date things happen. A solicitor will check the law in force when you take each step and explain how it affects your property.

Questions and answers

Is a flat always leasehold?
Almost all flats in England and Wales are leasehold, because the shared structure of the building needs to be owned and managed by someone. There are exceptions. A small number of flats are commonhold, and some flats are held as share of freehold, where the leaseholders also own the freehold together. Even in a share of freehold, each owner normally still has a lease of their flat. The title shows exactly how a property is held.
What does share of freehold actually mean?
Share of freehold usually means the flat owners in a building collectively own the freehold, often through a company or as joint owners, and each also holds a lease of their own flat. It gives the owners control over the building and can make extending leases easier and cheaper. It also means sharing responsibility for insurance, repairs and decisions. How it works depends on the documents, so a solicitor will look at the arrangements.
Can a house be leasehold?
Yes. Leasehold houses exist across England and Wales. Some are in older areas where leasehold was traditional. Others were sold on new estates in more recent years, which led to concern about rising ground rents. Owners of leasehold houses often have rights to buy the freehold or extend the lease under a different set of rules from flats. Recent reforms aim to change how leasehold houses are sold in future.
What is a peppercorn rent?
A peppercorn rent is a nominal rent, meaning in practice nothing is paid. The phrase comes from an old practice of using a peppercorn as token payment. Many lease extensions granted under the statutory route reduce ground rent to a peppercorn. Most new long residential leases granted since mid 2022 must also have a peppercorn ground rent, subject to limited exceptions.
Who is my landlord if I own a leasehold flat?
Your landlord is whoever owns the freehold or a superior lease of your building. It could be an individual, a property company, an investment fund, a housing association, a council or a company owned by the leaseholders themselves. Many landlords appoint a managing agent to run the building day to day. Your title, your service charge demands and the managing agent can tell you who your landlord is.
Is freehold always better than leasehold?
Not always. Freehold usually gives more control and avoids ongoing service charges and a lease that runs down. But leasehold makes sense for flats, where shared parts need managing, and a well run building with a long lease can be a perfectly good place to own. The right answer for any buyer depends on the property, the lease terms and their own plans. A solicitor can explain the effect of the terms.
What does the term of years absolute mean on my title?
It is the formal legal name for a lease. When the title register describes a property as held for a term of years absolute, it means the owner holds a lease, not the freehold. The register should also show the date of the lease, its start date and how many years it was granted for. From that you can work out roughly how many years remain. A conveyancer can confirm the exact figure.
Can I make changes to my flat?
It depends on your lease. Many leases allow decoration and minor changes but require the landlord's consent for structural alterations, moving kitchens or bathrooms, changing windows or laying hard floors. Consent often comes with fees. Making changes without consent can lead to a dispute or cause difficulty when you sell. Your solicitor or managing agent can tell you what your lease says.
Can I rent out my leasehold flat?
Many leases allow subletting, sometimes with conditions such as notifying the landlord, getting consent or paying a fee. Some leases restrict short term or holiday letting. Your mortgage lender may also have its own rules about letting. Reading the lease is the starting point, and a solicitor can explain what it allows and what consents might be needed.
Can I keep a pet in a leasehold flat?
Some leases ban pets, some require consent and some say nothing. Where consent is needed, landlords may set conditions. Because the wording varies so much, it is worth checking your own lease or asking your managing agent before getting a pet. If you are buying, ask your conveyancer to check this if it matters to you.
What if I disagree with my service charge?
The first step is usually to ask the managing agent for a breakdown and supporting documents, which leaseholders have a legal right to see. Many issues are resolved this way. If not, the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) can decide whether a charge is reasonable and payable under the lease. Tribunal cases have their own costs and risks, so many leaseholders talk to a solicitor first.
What is the right to manage?
The right to manage lets qualifying leaseholders in a building take over its management from the landlord through a company they set up, without having to buy the freehold or prove the landlord has done anything wrong. The company then handles repairs, insurance and service charges. The landlord keeps ownership. There are conditions and a set procedure, and a solicitor can explain whether it might suit a particular building.
Who repairs what in a leasehold building?
The lease decides. Typically the leaseholder looks after the inside of their flat, such as plaster, floors, fittings and internal doors, while the landlord looks after the structure, roof, foundations, external walls and shared areas, recovering the cost through the service charge. Windows, balconies and pipes are where leases differ most. If there is a repair problem, checking the lease wording is the starting point, and a solicitor can help interpret it.
Do freehold houses have any rules?
Yes, often. Freehold titles commonly carry restrictive covenants, such as limits on use, extensions or boundary features, and positive obligations such as maintaining a shared fence. Planning rules and building regulations also apply. A freehold owner has more control than a leaseholder, but not unlimited freedom. A solicitor can read a title and explain which covenants affect a particular house.
What is an estate charge?
An estate charge is a payment made by freehold homeowners on some estates towards the upkeep of shared facilities that the council has not adopted, such as roads, green spaces, play areas or drainage. A management company usually collects it. The amount and what it covers depend on the title and any estate documents. Recent laws give homeowners more rights over these charges, introduced in stages.
What is a rentcharge?
A rentcharge is a yearly payment charged on freehold land to someone who does not own it. They are found especially in parts of Greater Manchester and around Bristol. Many old rentcharges were set to end under earlier law, and owners can often redeem them. Estate rentcharges, used to pay for shared estate costs, can still be created. A solicitor checks for rentcharges when reviewing a title.
Are flying freeholds a problem?
A flying freehold is where part of a freehold home overhangs or sits beneath land owned by someone else. The issue is usually whether there are proper rights of support and access for repairs. Some lenders are cautious about them, and some ask for extra protection such as insurance. A solicitor will look at the title and any rights in place and explain what they mean.
Can I buy the freehold of my leasehold house?
Many owners of leasehold houses have a legal right to buy the freehold or extend their lease, but the conditions and price rules differ from those for flats and have been affected by recent reforms. Some landlords also agree a sale informally. A solicitor who handles leasehold work can check the lease and explain the options and process that apply now.
What should I check before buying a leasehold flat?
Buyers usually want to know how many years are left on the lease, the ground rent and how it can change, the service charge and recent accounts, any planned major works, the state of the reserve fund and any building safety issues. The lease terms on alterations, subletting and pets also matter. Your conveyancer will gather this information and explain it in their report.
Why do lenders care about lease length?
A lease is a wasting asset, meaning it loses value as the term runs down. Lenders want their security to hold its value for the life of the mortgage and beyond. Most have minimum lease lengths they will accept, and those vary between lenders. If a lease is too short for a buyer's lender, the sale may stall until the lease is extended.
Does selling leasehold take longer than freehold?
Often it does, because more information is needed. The seller's conveyancer has to obtain a management pack from the managing agent or landlord, which can take time and usually carries a fee. Buyers' conveyancers then raise questions about the lease, charges and building. Sellers who request the management pack early and keep their own records in order can help things move faster.
What is a management pack?
A management pack, sometimes called an LPE1 response, is information provided by the landlord or managing agent when a leasehold flat is sold. It covers service charges, ground rent, insurance, planned works, disputes and any consents. The buyer's conveyancer relies on it to report to the buyer and lender. The fee for producing it is usually paid by the seller, and recent reforms aim to regulate these fees.
Can I buy a share of the freehold when I buy my flat?
If the building is already share of freehold, the buyer normally takes over the seller's share along with the lease. That might mean a share certificate in a company or a transfer of an interest in the freehold. If the building is not share of freehold, the buyer cannot simply add one. The leaseholders would need to buy the freehold together. Your conveyancer will explain the structure.
What is a deed of covenant?
A deed of covenant is a document some leases require a new owner to sign, promising the landlord or management company that they will comply with the lease. It is usually dealt with by the buyer's conveyancer around completion, and there is often a fee. It sits alongside the notice of the sale and mortgage that many leases require. Your conveyancer handles these formalities.
Is leasehold being abolished?
Not across the board. Reforms passed so far change how leasehold works, including ground rents, the price of extending or buying the freehold and the sale of new leasehold houses, with much brought in over time. Government has also talked about moving away from leasehold for new flats and promoting commonhold. Existing leases continue. A solicitor can explain what the current rules mean for a particular property.
Should I wait for reforms before extending my lease?
This is a common question and the answer depends heavily on the individual case. Some reforms may reduce costs for some leaseholders, while waiting means the lease keeps getting shorter. The timetable for changes has shifted before. A solicitor and a valuer can look at your lease, its length and your plans and explain the considerations, so you can decide.
Where can I find out more?
GOV.UK has official information on leasehold, including the rights leaseholders have and updates on reforms. The Leasehold Advisory Service provides free general information. For advice on your own lease or building, you would need a solicitor who deals with leasehold law. What is commonhold and could my building convert?
Commonhold is a form of ownership where each flat owner owns their unit freehold and a commonhold association, owned by all the unit owners, runs the shared parts. There are no leases to run down and no landlord. Converting an existing leasehold building has been possible in theory but has needed the agreement of everyone with an interest, which is rarely achieved. Reforms aim to make conversion easier. A solicitor can explain the current position.

More questions people ask

What counts as a short lease?
There is no fixed legal definition. Many people treat a lease with fewer than 80 or 90 years left as short, because that is when lenders, buyers and valuers start to pay closer attention. Leases with 60 years or less are usually seen as a significant issue. A solicitor can tell you exactly how many years are left on yours and what that means in practice.
How do I check how long is left on my lease?
Your lease sets out the start date and the original term. Subtract the time that has passed since the start date to find what remains. Note that the start date in the lease is not always the date it was signed. If you do not have the lease, a copy can usually be obtained from HM Land Registry, and a solicitor can check it for you.
Why does 80 years matter?
Under the long standing valuation rules, once a lease has 80 years or fewer left, the freeholder is entitled to half of the marriage value when it is extended. This can make the extension noticeably more expensive. The 2024 Act removes marriage value, but this depends on regulations and is being brought in over time, so a solicitor will confirm whether the 80 year point still affects you.
What happens when a lease runs out completely?
In some circumstances the law allows the occupier of a flat to stay on as a tenant when a long lease ends, but usually paying a market rent and without owning a lease they can sell. The flat's capital value effectively goes back to the freeholder. This is rare, because most owners extend well before it happens.
I inherited a flat with a short lease. What are my options?
Personal representatives can often start a statutory lease extension on behalf of the estate within a set period after the grant of probate or letters of administration. They can also sell the flat as it is, or sell with the benefit of a notice. A solicitor can explain the options and the time limits for the estate.
Does a short lease affect my service charge?
No, the service charge is set by the terms of the lease and the costs of running the building, not by how many years are left. However, if you extend informally, the new lease could change some terms. Under the statutory route, only limited changes to the existing terms are allowed.
Does the ground rent make a short lease worse?
It can. Under the long standing valuation rules, part of the premium compensates the freeholder for losing its ground rent, so a higher or rising ground rent usually means a higher premium. Some short leases also have ground rent terms that worry lenders. A surveyor can explain how your ground rent affects the cost of extending.
