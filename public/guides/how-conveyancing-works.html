How conveyancing works

What happens between your offer being accepted and getting the keys.

  • Plain English
  • General information, not advice
  • About conveyancing
Make an Enquiry
All guides

Key points

  • Most purchases take around three to four months from offer to completion
  • Exchange is the point of no return, completion is when you get the keys
  • Replying quickly to your conveyancer is the best way to avoid delays

Instructing a conveyancer

Once an offer is accepted, both buyer and seller instruct a conveyancer. The seller’s conveyancer prepares the contract pack and the buyer’s conveyancer checks it.

Searches and enquiries

The buyer’s conveyancer orders searches and raises questions about anything unclear in the title or the property information.

Mortgage offer and report

If you are buying with a mortgage, the lender issues an offer and your conveyancer checks its conditions. They then report to you before you sign.

Exchange and completion

At exchange, contracts become binding and a completion date is fixed. On completion, the money moves, you get the keys and your conveyancer registers the change of ownership.

  • Ask for a full quote including disbursements
  • Reply to your conveyancer quickly
  • Keep your mortgage broker and estate agent in the loop

How long it takes

A straightforward purchase with no chain often takes eight to twelve weeks. Leasehold flats, new builds and long chains usually take longer, because there are more people to hear back from and more documents to check.

What can slow things down

  • Waiting for searches from the local council
  • Missing documents, such as building regulation certificates
  • Questions about the lease or the management company
  • Someone further up or down the chain not being ready

What it costs

You pay a legal fee to the conveyancer plus disbursements, such as searches and Land Registry fees. If you are buying, Stamp Duty Land Tax may also be due. Ask for a written quote that lists everything, so you can compare firms properly.

Tip

Instruct your conveyancer as soon as your offer is accepted. Starting the paperwork early is one of the simplest ways to keep your move on track.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. A solicitor can advise on your situation.

Fees and costs

Most conveyancing firms quote a fixed legal fee for the transaction, plus disbursements. Disbursements are costs the firm pays on your behalf, such as searches, Land Registry fees, identity checks and bank transfer fees. If you are buying, Stamp Duty Land Tax may also be due, and the firm usually pays it for you from the money you send them.

Leasehold work, new builds, shared ownership and buying with a company usually cost more because there is more to check. A good firm puts everything in writing before you instruct them, so you can compare quotes like for like and avoid surprises at completion.

Everything you need to know

Pick a topic to read more.

Conveyancing is the legal work that moves ownership of a property from one person to another. In England and Wales it starts once an offer has been accepted and ends when the new owner is registered at HM Land Registry. Nothing is legally fixed until contracts are exchanged, which is why a sale can still fall through weeks after everyone has shaken hands.

It helps to think of conveyancing as three jobs running side by side. The first is checking the property, so the buyer knows exactly what they are getting. The second is organising the money, from the deposit and mortgage to the payment of any tax. The third is coordinating with everyone else involved, which in a chain can mean several other buyers, sellers, lenders and agents.

Who is involved

  • The buyer and their conveyancer, who carry out the checks and handle the purchase money
  • The seller and their conveyancer, who prove the seller owns the property and can sell it
  • The estate agent, who passes on news and chases progress but does not do the legal work
  • The buyer's mortgage lender, if there is one, which has its own requirements
  • For leasehold flats, the freeholder or managing agent, who supplies information about the building
  • Other people in the chain, whose own sales and purchases have to line up

Your conveyancer may be a solicitor or a licensed conveyancer. Both are regulated, and both can do the work. In many purchases with a mortgage, the same firm also acts for the lender, which keeps things simpler but means the firm has duties to the lender as well as to you.

Buying and selling at the same time

Most people who move home are selling one property and buying another. The two transactions are separate legal jobs, but they are tied together so that both exchange on the same day and both complete on the same day. That linking is what creates a chain, and it is the main reason moves take the time they do.

This guide explains the usual order of events in more detail than our short overview above. Every transaction is different, and a conveyancer will look at your own property and circumstances and explain what applies to you.

Most of the work in a sale or purchase happens before contracts are exchanged. This is the stage where questions are asked and answered, and where most delays and surprises come to light.

Getting started

When a conveyancer takes on a new client, they first have to check identity and, for buyers, where the money is coming from. Anti money laundering rules mean they need clear evidence of the source of any deposit, including gifts from family. Gathering bank statements and gift letters early often saves days later on.

The firm will send a client care letter setting out who is doing the work, what it costs and how complaints are handled. Signing and returning it, and paying any money on account, lets the file open properly.

The contract pack

The seller's conveyancer gets a copy of the title from HM Land Registry and asks the seller to complete standard forms. These usually include a property information form about things like boundaries, disputes, alterations and services, and a fittings and contents form listing what is staying. For a leasehold flat there is also a leasehold information form and a request to the managing agent for details about the building.

The draft contract, the title and the forms are then sent to the buyer's conveyancer. This bundle is often called the contract pack.

Checking and asking questions

The buyer's conveyancer reads the title and the forms, orders searches and raises written questions, known as enquiries, about anything that is missing, unclear or worrying. Typical enquiries ask for building regulation sign off for an extension, details of a shared driveway or an explanation of a restriction on the title. The seller's conveyancer passes them to the seller and sends back the replies.

There can be several rounds of enquiries. A slow reply at either end is one of the most common causes of delay, so prompt answers from the seller and the buyer make a real difference.

The mortgage side

While the legal checks go on, the buyer's lender values the property and issues a mortgage offer. The conveyancer checks the offer and its conditions and confirms to the lender that the property is suitable security. A survey is separate from the lender's valuation, and many buyers commission their own so they understand the physical condition of what they are buying.

The report and signing

When the checks are complete, the buyer's conveyancer usually sends a report explaining the title, the searches, the replies to enquiries and any points that need the buyer's attention. The buyer signs the contract and, where there is a mortgage, the mortgage deed. Signing does not commit anyone. That only happens at exchange.

Exchange of contracts is the moment the deal becomes legally binding. Before it, either side can walk away, usually without paying anything to the other. After it, pulling out normally means losing the deposit and possibly paying more on top.

What has to be ready

  • All enquiries answered to the buyer's satisfaction
  • Searches back and reviewed
  • A mortgage offer in place, if one is needed
  • The deposit sent to the buyer's conveyancer in cleared funds
  • Both sides' contracts signed
  • A completion date agreed by everyone in the chain

How it happens

Exchange almost always takes place over the phone between the conveyancers. They confirm that the two signed contracts are identical, agree the completion date and read out a set wording. The contracts are then treated as exchanged and are posted or sent to each other afterwards. In a chain, each link exchanges in turn on the same day, usually starting at one end.

The deposit

The buyer pays a deposit at exchange. It is often a set percentage of the price, but the amount can be negotiated and in a chain the deposit received on a sale can sometimes be passed up to the next purchase. Your conveyancer will explain what has been agreed in your case.

Insurance and timing

Once contracts are exchanged, the buyer usually becomes responsible for insuring the building, so many lenders and conveyancers expect buildings insurance to start from exchange. The gap between exchange and completion can be the same day, a week or longer. It depends on what everyone in the chain needs, such as time to book removals or give notice on a rental.

Because exchange is binding, it is worth raising any final concerns with your conveyancer before you give them authority to exchange. They will look at your own position and explain what it means to proceed.

Completion is the day ownership changes hands and keys are released. It is fixed in the contract at exchange, so it is not normally moved unless everyone agrees.

Before the day

In the days before completion, the buyer's conveyancer sends a completion statement setting out the money needed, which normally covers the balance of the price, the legal fee, disbursements and any tax. The buyer sends the balance in good time. The conveyancer also carries out final searches at HM Land Registry and checks for bankruptcy against the buyer, and asks the lender to release the mortgage money.

On the day

On completion day the buyer's conveyancer sends the money to the seller's conveyancer by bank transfer. When it arrives, the seller's conveyancer confirms completion and tells the estate agent to release the keys. In a chain, money moves from one firm to the next, so a hold up at one end can delay everyone further along.

Completion often happens around the middle of the day, but there is no fixed time and it can be later if funds move slowly. Sellers are usually expected to have moved out by a time stated in the contract.

The seller's side

The seller's conveyancer uses the money received to pay off the seller's mortgage and the estate agent's fee, then sends the balance to the seller or on to their next purchase. They also send the documents the buyer needs, including the signed transfer deed.

If completion is late

If one side cannot complete on the agreed day, the contract usually allows the other side to claim interest and, after a formal notice, to take further steps. Late completion is uncommon but stressful, and conveyancers work hard to avoid it. If it happens, your conveyancer will explain the position and what can be done.

Getting the keys is not quite the end of the legal work. There are a few important steps left that mostly happen without the buyer seeing them.

Paying the tax

If Stamp Duty Land Tax is due, the buyer's conveyancer usually files the return and pays the tax on the buyer's behalf. There is a short legal deadline after completion for doing this, and late filing can lead to penalties. The rates and reliefs change from time to time, so the conveyancer works out the figure based on the rules in force at the time.

Registration

The conveyancer then applies to HM Land Registry to register the buyer as the new owner and, where there is a mortgage, to register the lender's charge. The seller's old mortgage is removed from the title. Registration can take some time to be processed, but the buyer is the legal owner in the meantime and the application protects their position.

Leasehold extras

For a leasehold flat, the lease often requires the buyer to give notice of the purchase and the new mortgage to the landlord or managing agent, sometimes with a fee. There may also be a deed of covenant or a share certificate in a management company to deal with.

What you should receive

  • Confirmation that the tax return has been filed, if one was needed
  • A copy of the updated title once registration is complete
  • A final bill and any balance owed to you
  • Information about where your deeds and documents are kept

If you are unsure whether everything has been finished, your conveyancer can confirm where things stand.

The conveyancer does the legal work, but the client has a big part to play in keeping things moving. Small things on the client's side often make the difference between a smooth move and a frustrating one.

Things that help

  • Choosing a conveyancer early, ideally before an offer is accepted
  • Sending identity documents and proof of funds as soon as they are asked for
  • Filling in property forms carefully and fully if you are selling
  • Digging out guarantees, planning papers and certificates for any work done
  • Replying to questions in days rather than weeks
  • Telling your conveyancer about any dates that matter to you

Keeping in touch

Many firms use online portals where you can upload documents and see progress. Others work mainly by email and phone. It helps to know at the start who your main contact is, how they prefer to be reached and how often you can expect updates. Regular short updates are usually more useful than long silences followed by a rush.

Costs

Conveyancing is usually charged as a fixed legal fee plus disbursements, which are third party costs such as search fees and Land Registry fees. Leasehold work, new builds, shared ownership and some lender requirements can add to the fee. Ask for a written quote that lists everything, and ask what could cause it to change.

Questions and answers

When should I choose a conveyancer?
Many people pick a conveyancer before they start viewing seriously or put their home on the market. That way, as soon as an offer is accepted, the firm can open a file and start checks straight away. Sellers in particular can save time by letting their conveyancer prepare the contract pack early. Choosing late is not a disaster, but it can mean a week or two is lost while identity checks and paperwork are done.
What is the difference between a solicitor and a licensed conveyancer?
Both are qualified and regulated to carry out conveyancing in England and Wales. A solicitor is regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and may work in a firm that handles other kinds of law too. A licensed conveyancer specialises in property transactions and is regulated by the Council for Licensed Conveyancers. For most residential moves either can do the work. What matters more is experience with your kind of property and how well the firm communicates.
Why do I have to prove where my money comes from?
Conveyancers must follow anti money laundering rules. They have to understand where the money for a purchase comes from, including savings, the sale of another property and gifts. This usually means providing bank statements covering a period of time and, for gifts, a letter and identity documents from the person giving the money. It can feel intrusive, but it applies to everyone and the firm cannot proceed without it.
Can I use the same conveyancer as the seller?
In limited situations the rules allow one firm to act for both sides, but many firms will not do so because of the risk of a conflict of interest. Most buyers and sellers have separate conveyancers so that each side has someone looking only after their interests. If a firm offers to act for both, they will explain the safeguards and when they would have to stop acting.
Does my estate agent do any of the legal work?
No. The estate agent markets the property, negotiates the price and passes messages between buyer and seller. They can be very helpful in chasing progress through a chain, but they do not check the title, raise enquiries or handle the purchase money. That is the conveyancer's job. Agents and conveyancers often work closely together, so it helps if both have your up to date contact details.
Can I do my own conveyancing?
The law does not stop you doing the legal work yourself on your own property, and some people do for simple sales. In practice it is rare. Most mortgage lenders require a regulated conveyancer to act for them, and a missed problem with a title or search can be expensive to fix later. A conveyancer can explain what is involved in your case and what they would cover.
What is in the contract pack?
The contract pack is the bundle the seller's conveyancer sends to the buyer's conveyancer at the start. It usually includes the draft contract, official copies of the title and title plan from HM Land Registry, the seller's property information form, the fittings and contents form and copies of any relevant certificates or planning documents. For a leasehold flat it also includes the lease and information about service charges and the building. The buyer's conveyancer reviews all of it before raising enquiries.
What are enquiries?
Enquiries are written questions from the buyer's conveyancer to the seller's conveyancer. They are raised when something in the contract pack, title or searches is missing or needs explaining. Common examples ask about building work, boundaries, rights of way, guarantees or disputes with neighbours. The seller answers through their conveyancer. Several rounds are normal, and the process ends when the buyer's conveyancer is satisfied enough to report to the buyer.
Do I need a survey as well as the lender's valuation?
The lender's valuation is for the lender's benefit and is mainly about whether the property is worth the money being lent. It is not a detailed look at its condition. Many buyers commission their own survey to find out about defects, repairs and possible costs. A conveyancer does not inspect the property, so a survey fills a different gap. Your conveyancer or surveyor can explain the types of survey available.
What happens if the survey or searches find a problem?
It depends on the problem. Sometimes more information from the seller resolves it. Sometimes the buyer asks for a price reduction, a repair before completion or an insurance policy to cover a risk. In some cases the buyer decides not to go ahead. Your conveyancer will explain what the finding means legally, and a surveyor can explain the physical side, so you can make your own decision.
What is the report on title?
Near the end of the checks, the buyer's conveyancer usually sends a written report. It explains who owns the property, any rights or restrictions affecting it, what the searches found and the answers to enquiries. For leasehold it summarises the key lease terms. It is worth reading carefully and asking about anything that is not clear. Once you are comfortable, you sign the contract ready for exchange.
Can the price change after an offer is accepted?
Until exchange, nothing is binding, so a price can be renegotiated. This sometimes happens after a down valuation by the lender or a poor survey result. It can also happen in a changing market. Renegotiation is between buyer and seller, usually through the estate agent. Once contracts are exchanged, the price is fixed in the contract and cannot be changed without both sides agreeing.
What does exchange of contracts actually mean?
Exchange is the point at which the buyer and seller become legally committed. The conveyancers confirm by phone that the signed contracts match, agree the completion date and treat the contracts as exchanged. From that moment the buyer has to buy and the seller has to sell on the agreed terms. Before exchange either side can withdraw, which is why the period before it can feel uncertain.
Can exchange and completion happen on the same day?
Yes. Simultaneous exchange and completion is quite common, particularly for first time buyers and properties with no chain. It removes the gap where one side is committed but the move has not happened yet. The downside is that nothing is certain until the day itself, which makes booking removals harder. Your conveyancer can explain how it would work in your transaction.
How is the completion date chosen?
The completion date is agreed between everyone in the chain before exchange and then written into the contracts. It has to suit buyers and sellers at every link, so some give and take is normal. Practical points such as notice periods, removal bookings and mortgage offer expiry dates all play a part. Once contracts are exchanged, the date is fixed and changing it needs everyone's agreement.
What happens on completion day?
On completion day the buyer's conveyancer sends the purchase money to the seller's conveyancer. When the seller's conveyancer receives it, they confirm completion and authorise the estate agent to release the keys. In a chain, money passes along from firm to firm, so timings depend on everyone. There is no set hour for completion, and some days it happens later than people hope, especially on busy Fridays.
What if the money does not arrive on time?
Delays on completion day usually come from banking cut off times or a hold up elsewhere in the chain. Conveyancers keep in close contact to sort things out. If completion is genuinely late, the contract normally allows the party waiting to claim interest for the delay. More serious steps are possible if a delay continues. Your conveyancer will keep you informed and explain your position.
When do I need buildings insurance?
As a buyer, you usually become responsible for insuring the building from exchange, because you are committed to buying it even if it is damaged before completion. Many lenders require cover from exchange. If you are buying a leasehold flat, the building is usually insured by the freeholder and you pay towards it through the service charge, so you may only need contents cover. Your conveyancer can confirm what applies.
What are disbursements?
Disbursements are costs the conveyancer pays to other organisations on your behalf. For a purchase they often include search fees, HM Land Registry fees, bankruptcy and title search fees and bank transfer fees. For a leasehold property there may also be fees charged by the managing agent for information and for registering your purchase. A good quote lists disbursements separately from the legal fee so you can see the full picture.
Why do quotes from different firms vary so much?
Firms price their work differently. Some quote a low headline fee and add extras for things like leasehold, gifted deposits, Help to Buy style schemes or lender work. Others include more in one price. Service levels also differ, from high volume teams to smaller firms with a named lawyer. Comparing like with like means checking what each quote includes and what could make it go up.
When do I pay my conveyancer?
Practice varies. Many firms ask for a payment on account at the start to cover searches and other early disbursements. The rest of the fee is usually taken from the completion funds or paid just before completion. If the transaction falls through, some firms charge for work done and others offer no move, no fee terms. Check the client care letter so you know what applies to you.
Who pays Stamp Duty Land Tax and when?
Stamp Duty Land Tax is paid by the buyer, where it applies. The conveyancer normally works out the amount, files the return and pays it after completion using money the buyer provides. The rates, thresholds and reliefs change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask your conveyancer for the current position. There is a short deadline after completion and late filing can lead to penalties.
How much is the deposit?
There is no single fixed deposit. It is often expressed as a percentage of the price, but it can be negotiated, and in a chain the deposit from a sale is sometimes used towards the next purchase. Your mortgage deposit and the contract deposit are related but not always the same thing. Your conveyancer will explain what deposit has been agreed and when the money needs to be with them.

Why does conveyancing take so long?
Most of the time is spent waiting for information from other people. Searches have to come back, enquiries have to be answered, lenders have to issue offers and, for flats, managing agents have to supply information. In a chain, every link has to be ready at the same time. A conveyancer can push things along, but they cannot make a third party reply faster. Prompt replies from clients help a lot.
What is a chain and why does it matter?
A chain is a series of linked sales and purchases where each move depends on another. If one person cannot proceed, everyone else is held up. Chains can be short or long, and the longer the chain, the more chances there are for delay. Being chain free as a buyer, or buying from someone who is, often makes things quicker but does not remove all delays.
What is gazumping?
Gazumping is when a seller accepts a higher offer from someone else after already accepting yours. Because nothing is binding until exchange, it is legal in England and Wales, though frustrating. The reverse, called gazundering, is when a buyer lowers their offer just before exchange. Moving quickly on your side can reduce the window for either to happen, but it cannot rule them out.
What if the seller has no building regulation sign off for work?
This comes up often with extensions, loft conversions, window replacements and electrical work. Options can include getting a retrospective certificate from the council, arranging an indemnity insurance policy or, in some cases, having the work checked. Each has pros and cons, and lenders have their own views. Your conveyancer will explain what the missing paperwork means legally and what options exist in your case.
Can a sale fall through after exchange?
It is rare, because after exchange both sides are legally bound. If a buyer fails to complete, they usually lose their deposit and may have to pay further losses. If a seller fails to complete, the buyer may be able to claim against them. Because the consequences are serious, conveyancers do not exchange until they are confident completion can happen on the agreed date.
Who do I complain to if things go wrong?
Start with the firm itself. Every regulated firm must have a complaints procedure, and it should be set out in your client care letter. If you are not happy with the firm's response, you can usually take the complaint to the Legal Ombudsman. Conduct concerns about solicitors can go to the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and concerns about licensed conveyancers to the Council for Licensed Conveyancers.
