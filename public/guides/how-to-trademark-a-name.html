How to trademark a name

A plain English guide to protecting your brand name in the UK.

  • Plain English
  • General information, not advice
  • About trademarks
Make an Enquiry
All guides

Key points

  • A company name at Companies House is not a trademark
  • Search before you file, because a filed application can’t be widened
  • Registration usually takes around four months if nobody objects

Check the name can be registered

A trademark must be distinctive. Names that simply describe the product, such as Fresh Bread for a bakery, are usually refused. Invented words and names with no link to what you sell are the easiest to register.

Search for earlier marks

Search the UK IPO register for identical and similar marks used for similar goods or services. A professional clearance search also looks at company names and unregistered use.

Choose your classes and specification

Goods and services are grouped into 45 classes. You list exactly what you sell in each class you choose. The list decides what your registration protects, and it cannot be widened after filing.

File, examination and publication

Once you file at the UK IPO, an examiner checks the application. If it is accepted, it is published for two months so others can oppose it. If nobody does, it is registered, usually around four months after filing.

  • Search before you spend money on branding
  • Describe your goods and services carefully
  • Keep evidence of when you started using the name

What it costs

The UK IPO charges an official fee for the first class and a further fee for each extra class. If you use a trademark solicitor or attorney, they usually quote a fixed fee for searching and filing on top.

Trademarking a logo as well as a name

A word mark protects the name however it is written. A logo mark protects the design. Many businesses start with the word mark because it gives the widest protection for the name itself.

Protecting the name abroad

A UK registration only covers the UK. If you trade overseas, a trademark attorney can extend protection to other countries, for example through the Madrid system.

Tip

Use the ® symbol only once your mark is registered. Until then you can use ™ to show you claim the name as a trademark.

Everything you need to know

Trademarking a name means registering it at the UK Intellectual Property Office so that you have the exclusive right to use it for the goods and services you list. Once registered, the protection covers the whole of the UK and lasts for ten years, renewable indefinitely. This guide walks through how it usually works, step by step, so you know what to expect whether you file yourself or use a professional.

Before anything else, be clear about what you want to protect and why. Most businesses start with their main business or brand name, then add product names, logos and slogans as they grow. If budget is limited, focus first on the name customers use to find and recommend you.

A company name is not a trademark

Registering a company at Companies House stops another company registering an identical name on that register, but it gives you no trademark rights. Someone else could still use, and even register as a trademark, a similar name for their goods. A trademark registration is a separate application to a separate office.

A domain name is not a trademark either

Owning the matching domain name is useful, but it doesn't stop others using the name in trade. It also doesn't protect you if someone with earlier rights complains. Trademarks, company names and domains are best thought of together, but each does a different job.

Who can apply

Any person or business can apply. The applicant should normally be whoever will own and use the brand, usually your company if you trade through one. If the company doesn't exist yet, you can apply in your own name and transfer the mark later, recording the change at the UK IPO.

When to start

The best time to think about trademarks is while you are still choosing a name, before you print anything or launch a website. Your rights run from the filing date, so the earlier you file, the earlier your protection starts. If you have already been trading for some time, it is still well worth registering, because a registration is far easier to enforce than rights built up through use.

Doing it yourself or using a professional

Filing yourself costs only the official fee and suits simple cases where the name is clearly distinctive, a search is clear and you know exactly what you sell. A professional costs more but brings judgement about distinctiveness, a wider search, carefully drafted wording and help if anything goes wrong. Many businesses decide based on how important the name is and how much they plan to invest in it.

A trademark must be distinctive, meaning customers will see it as a sign of where your goods or services come from rather than a description of them. This is the first hurdle, and many applications filed without help fail here.

Names that tend to work

  • Invented words with no existing meaning
  • Real words that have nothing to do with your goods, like a fruit for a technology company
  • Names that hint at a quality without describing it directly
  • Surnames and place names that are not commonly used for your type of goods

Names that tend to be refused

  • Words that describe the goods, such as their type, quality, quantity or purpose
  • Laudatory words like best, premium or ultimate on their own
  • Common geographical names linked to the goods
  • Generic words for the product in your industry
  • Names that could deceive the public about the goods

If your preferred name is descriptive, you may still be able to protect a logo that combines the name with a distinctive design, or a variation of the name with a twist. A professional can tell you how likely the examiner is to accept it and suggest changes before you file.

Thinking ahead

A distinctive name isn't only easier to register. It is also easier to enforce, because competitors can't argue that they are simply describing their own goods. That makes it a better long term investment.

Examples to think about

Imagine a bakery called Fresh Bread. The name tells customers exactly what is sold, so the examiner would very likely refuse it for bread. A bakery called Copper Kettle has nothing to do with bread and would be much easier to register. A name like Crumbology hints at baking without describing it and might sit somewhere in between, depending on the goods. These are illustrations only, and the real test always depends on the goods and services you list.

The UK IPO does not refuse an application simply because a similar earlier mark exists. Instead, it may tell the owner of that mark about your application, and they may oppose. That is why searching before filing is so important.

Free checks you can do

  • Search the UK trade mark register online for identical and similar names
  • Search Companies House for similar company names
  • Search the main marketplaces, app stores and social media platforms
  • Search the web for businesses trading under similar names in your field

A professional clearance search

A trademark firm can run a wider search that looks for names that sound alike, look alike or mean the same thing, in related classes, and give you a written view on the risk. For a business about to invest in branding, packaging or a website, this is usually money well spent.

If the search finds a problem, you can change the name, adjust it, narrow what you sell under it or approach the owner of the earlier mark. Doing this before you file and launch is far cheaper than dealing with a dispute later.

Reading what you find

Almost every search finds something. What matters is how close the earlier names are, whether they are used for similar goods or services and whether they are actually in use. Two identical names for unrelated goods may both be fine. Two similar names for the same goods may well clash. If you are unsure, that is the point at which professional advice is most useful.

Keep a record of what you searched and when. If a dispute arises later, being able to show you checked carefully can help, and the record is useful background if you expand into new goods or new countries.

If you are choosing between several names, search all of them before you fall in love with one. It is much easier to let go of a name at the shortlist stage than after you have shared it with customers and suppliers.

Every application lists the goods and services the trademark will cover, sorted into 45 classes. Classes 1 to 34 cover goods and classes 35 to 45 cover services. This list, called the specification, decides what your registration protects.

Examples

  • A clothing brand might need class 25 for clothing, class 18 for bags and class 35 for online retail
  • A café might need class 43 for restaurant services and class 30 for coffee and baked goods
  • A software business might need class 9 for downloadable software and class 42 for software as a service
  • A beauty brand might need class 3 for cosmetics and class 44 for beauty treatments

Getting it right

The UK IPO provides a searchable list of pre approved terms, which makes filing easier. Cover what you sell now and what you realistically plan to sell in the next few years. Each extra class costs more, so don't claim classes you will never use, and remember that a mark not used for five years can be challenged.

The specification can be narrowed after filing but never widened. If you leave out something important, you will need a new application with a later filing date.

Online retail

If you sell other people's products online as well as your own, think about whether you need cover for retail services in class 35 as well as for the goods themselves. Many online brands include both. The firm or the UK IPO's guidance can help you decide.

If you are unsure which class something falls into, the UK IPO's online tools let you search by keyword and suggest the right class. Getting the class wrong usually leads to an objection rather than a refusal, but it can delay registration.

You can file online through the UK IPO website, or a professional can file for you. The application includes the owner's details, the mark, the classes and the list of goods and services, and the official fee.

Examination

An examiner checks the application, usually within a few weeks. If there is a problem, such as the name being descriptive or the wording being unclear, they send an examination report with a deadline to respond. The UK IPO also searches for similar marks and may tell you about them, giving you the option to continue, amend or withdraw.

Publication and opposition

Accepted applications are published in the Trade Marks Journal for two months, extendable to three, during which anyone with earlier rights can oppose. Most applications are not opposed. If yours is, many oppositions settle through negotiation.

Registration

If there is no opposition, the mark is registered, usually around four months after filing. Your rights date back to the filing date. You can then use the ® symbol, and you should note the renewal date ten years from filing.

Watch out for scam invoices

After filing, your details are public. Businesses often receive official looking letters offering to publish or record their mark for a fee. These are not from the UK IPO. Check with the UK IPO or your adviser before paying anything.

Fast track

For an extra fee, the UK IPO offers a fast track examination for some applications, which speeds up the first stage. It doesn't shorten the opposition period, so it won't get you a registration within days, but it can give you an earlier answer on whether the mark is acceptable.

Keeping track of deadlines

If you file yourself, make sure the UK IPO has an address and email you check regularly. Examination reports and opposition notices have fixed deadlines, and missing them can mean the application is refused or treated as abandoned. If you use a firm, official letters go to them and they will let you know what needs doing.

Registration is valuable only if you look after it. A few simple habits keep your trademark strong.

  • Use the mark as registered, for the goods and services listed
  • Keep dated evidence of use, such as invoices, packaging and website pages
  • Watch for similar applications and object in time
  • Record any change of owner, name or address at the UK IPO
  • Renew every ten years, and diary the date well in advance
  • Consider protection abroad if you start trading overseas

If someone copies your name, a registration makes it much easier to send a cease and desist letter, report listings to marketplaces and, if necessary, take legal action. If you later sell the business or raise investment, a clean registration in the right name is an asset buyers and investors look for.

Logos, rebrands and new products

If you later change your logo, the old logo registration may not cover the new design, although a word mark for the name continues to protect the name. When you launch new products or services that fall outside your existing classes, you will need a new application to cover them. A short review whenever your brand changes keeps your protection in step with your business.

Getting help

If anything in this guide doesn't fit your situation, or you are unsure about a step, a trademark solicitor or chartered trade mark attorney can help. Many offer fixed fees for searches and filings, and an initial conversation will usually tell you what level of help you need.

Questions and answers

How do I trademark a name in the UK?
Check the name is distinctive, search for earlier marks, choose your classes and list of goods and services, and file an application with the UK IPO. The UK IPO examines it, publishes it for opposition and, if all goes well, registers it, usually around four months after filing.
Can I trademark a name myself?
Yes. Anyone can apply online at the UK IPO. Many people use a trademark solicitor or attorney because the choice of mark, classes and wording decides how well the brand is protected, and mistakes can't be fixed by widening the application later.
How much does it cost to trademark a name?
There is an official UK IPO fee for the first class and a further fee for each extra class. If you use a professional, they charge for their work, often as a fixed fee. Official fees change from time to time, so check GOV.UK for current figures.
How long does it take to trademark a name?
Around four months from filing to registration if there are no objections or oppositions. Objections and oppositions add time. Searching first and preparing the application carefully reduces the chance of delays.
Do I need to be a limited company to trademark a name?
No. Sole traders, partnerships, individuals and companies can all apply. The owner should be whoever will own the brand. If you later form a company, you can transfer the mark to it.
Should I trademark my business name or my product name?
Start with the name that matters most to customers and is most at risk of copying. For many small businesses that is the business name. As you grow, you can add key product names and logos.
What is the difference between ™ and ®?
™ can be used with any name you claim as a trademark, registered or not. ® can only be used once the mark is registered. Using ® for an unregistered mark is an offence in the UK.
Can I trademark a name before I start trading?
Yes. You can apply for a name you genuinely intend to use. Filing before launch secures an earlier filing date and gives you time to deal with any objections before your marketing begins.
Is it worth trademarking a name for a side business?
It can be, especially if you plan to grow it, sell online or invest in branding. Copying can hurt small businesses as much as large ones, and a registration makes it much easier to deal with copies on marketplaces.
Can I trademark a common word?
Sometimes. A common word can be registered for goods it has nothing to do with. It is usually refused for goods it describes. The examiner looks at the word in relation to what you sell.
Can I trademark a name someone else uses?
If someone else already uses or has registered the same or a similar name for similar goods, your application may be opposed and your use may be challenged. A search helps you find out before you invest.
Can I trademark my own name?
Often yes, if it is distinctive for your goods and nobody else has earlier rights in a similar name. Common surnames can be harder to register. Others with the same name may sometimes still use it honestly.
Should I trademark a name or a logo?
A word mark protects the name in any style and usually gives the widest protection. A logo mark protects the design. If the name is weak on its own, a logo may be easier to register. Many businesses file the word first and the logo later.
Can I trademark a hashtag or a username?
A hashtag or username can sometimes be registered if it works as a trademark for your goods or services and meets the usual tests. In practice most businesses register the underlying brand name instead.
Can I trademark a name in another language?
Yes, but the examiner will consider what the word means. A foreign word that describes the goods in its own language may still be refused if UK customers would understand it. A foreign word with no descriptive meaning for the goods can be very distinctive.
Can I trademark a misspelt word?
A deliberate misspelling of a descriptive word is often still treated as descriptive, because customers would recognise the word. Misspellings that change the word enough to create a new impression may be more acceptable.
Is the UK IPO search enough?
It is a useful free first check for identical marks. It is less reliable at finding similar marks and doesn't cover company names or unregistered use. For a launch, a professional clearance search gives a fuller picture.
How many classes should I choose?
Enough to cover what you sell now and plan to sell soon. Many small businesses need two or three. Each extra class costs more, and unused classes can be challenged after five years, so focus on what matters.
Can I add classes later?
No. You can't add new goods, services or classes to an application once filed. You would need a new application, which has a later filing date.
What happens if my search finds a similar mark?
It depends on how similar the marks and goods are. You might change or adjust the name, narrow what you sell under it or approach the other owner. A professional can tell you how serious the risk is.
Does searching tell me if I can use the name, as well as register it?
A professional clearance search looks at both. Registration risk is about whether your application is likely to be opposed. Use risk is about whether trading under the name could lead to an infringement or passing off claim.
What are the pre approved terms?
They are standard descriptions of goods and services that the UK IPO has already accepted. Using them makes examination smoother and reduces the risk of objections about unclear wording.
Should I search before choosing a domain name?
Ideally, search the name first, then register the domain. Owning a domain doesn't give you the right to use the name in trade if someone else has earlier trademark rights.
What if the examiner objects?
You receive an examination report with a deadline. You can argue the point, narrow the goods and services or, sometimes, show that the mark has become distinctive through use. If the objection can't be overcome, the application may be refused.
What if someone opposes my application?
You will have a deadline to respond. Many oppositions settle, for example by narrowing the goods or agreeing how both marks will be used. A professional can advise on your chances and negotiate for you.
Can I use the name while my application is pending?
You can, but registration doesn't guarantee that your use won't infringe someone else's earlier rights. A search reduces that risk. While pending, use ™ rather than ®.
What if I receive an invoice after filing?
Be careful. Many official looking letters offering to publish or monitor your mark are not from the UK IPO. Check before paying anything.
Can I withdraw my application?
Yes, at any time before registration. Official fees are not usually refunded. Withdrawing may be sensible if a search or an opposition shows a serious problem.
Does an objection mean my name is taken?
Not necessarily. Examiner objections are usually about whether the name is distinctive or the wording is clear, not about other owners. Conflicts with other marks are normally raised through oppositions.
Can I appeal if my application is refused?
Yes. You can usually ask for a hearing before the refusal becomes final, and there is a right of appeal afterwards. Deadlines are short, so act quickly. A professional can advise on whether an appeal or a fresh application is the better route.
How long does a trademark last?
Ten years from the filing date, renewable every ten years indefinitely. There is a six month late period after expiry in which you can still renew with an extra fee.
Does my UK trademark protect me abroad?
No. It covers the UK only. You can extend protection through the Madrid system or by filing in other countries. If you file abroad within six months of your UK filing, you can usually claim the UK date.
What can I do if someone copies my registered name?
You can send a cease and desist letter, report listings to online marketplaces and, if needed, bring an infringement claim. Registration makes each of these much easier.
Can I sell or license my trademark?
Yes. A registered trademark can be sold, licensed or used as security. Transfers and licences should be in writing and recorded at the UK IPO.
How can Leaflegal help?
If you want help trademarking a name, answer a few questions and we will introduce you to one regulated firm, either a solicitor or a chartered trade mark attorney. Using Leaflegal is free, and the firm explains its fees before you decide anything.
Do I need to use my trademark to keep it?
Yes. If a registered mark isn't genuinely used for five years for the registered goods or services, anyone can apply to revoke it. Keep dated evidence of use.
Will a trademark stop people using my name on social media?
It makes it easier. Most social media platforms have processes for reporting accounts and posts that misuse a registered trademark, and a registration number usually speeds things up.
Can I register my name in Scotland or Northern Ireland separately?
No need. A UK registration covers England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland together. There is one UK register at the UK IPO.

What can be registered as a trademark?

Does a UK trademark protect me abroad?
No. A UK registration covers the UK only. If you trade or plan to trade abroad, you can apply in other countries, often through the Madrid system, which lets you extend a UK application or registration to many other countries with one international application. The firm can explain the costs and timing.
What does the R in a circle mean?
The ® symbol shows that a mark is registered. It is an offence in the UK to use it for a mark that is not registered. Until registration, many businesses use ™ instead, which simply tells people you claim the name as a trademark.
What happens after I file?
The UK IPO examines the application to check that the mark is distinctive and acceptable. It also searches for similar earlier marks and may tell their owners about your application. If the examiner is satisfied, the application is published so that others can oppose it. If there is no opposition, the mark is registered and you receive a certificate.
What rights do I have without registering?
If you have built up goodwill under a name, you may be able to stop others misrepresenting their business as connected with yours through a passing off claim. You would need to show goodwill, a misrepresentation that is likely to confuse customers and damage. It is usually harder and more expensive than relying on a registration.
How do I find out if someone copies my brand?
Many businesses use a watch service, which alerts them when someone applies for a similar mark so they can oppose it in time. Online monitoring can also flag marketplace listings and domain names. Customers and suppliers are often the first to notice and tell you. A firm can set up a watch for you.
Can I stop someone registering a similar company name?
Sometimes. The Company Names Tribunal at the UK IPO can order a company to change a name that is the same as or similar to one in which you have goodwill or a reputation, in some circumstances. The firm can tell you whether this or another route suits your situation better.
What is a coexistence agreement?
It is a contract between two businesses with similar marks, setting out how each will use its mark so they can both carry on without confusing customers. It might limit the goods, the markets or the way a logo is shown. Coexistence agreements are a common way to settle oppositions and disputes.
Can I license my trademark to someone else?
Yes. A licence lets another business use your mark on agreed terms, often in return for a royalty. A written licence protects the quality of your brand and your rights. Licences of registered marks can be recorded at the UK IPO. A firm can prepare or check the agreement.
Can my trademark be cancelled?
A registered mark can be revoked if it has not been genuinely used for the registered goods or services for a continuous five year period, and it can be declared invalid if it should never have been registered. Keeping evidence of use helps protect your registration against these challenges.
How often should I review my trademarks?
Many businesses review their marks when they launch a new product, enter a new market, change their logo or prepare for investment or a sale. A review checks that the right company owns each mark, that the goods and services still match what you sell and that renewals are in hand.
