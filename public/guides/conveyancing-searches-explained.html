Conveyancing searches explained

The checks your conveyancer carries out on a property before you buy it.

  • Plain English
  • General information, not advice
  • About conveyancing
Make an Enquiry
All guides

Key points

  • Searches are checks on the property and the area around it
  • Most lenders insist on them
  • They usually take a few weeks to come back

Searches are enquiries made to councils and other bodies about a property and its surroundings. Lenders usually require them, and they help you avoid surprises.

Common searches

  • Local authority search, covering planning, building control, roads and local charges
  • Water and drainage search, showing whether the property is connected to mains services
  • Environmental search, covering contaminated land and flood risk
  • Other searches depending on location, such as mining

What if a search shows a problem?

Your conveyancer will explain what it means and what your options are, which might include asking questions, negotiating or reconsidering the purchase.

How long searches take

Many searches come back within a couple of weeks, but some councils take longer. Your conveyancer will usually order them as soon as you instruct them and pay the search fees, so it helps to instruct a firm early.

Do I need searches if I am a cash buyer?

There is no legal requirement for a cash buyer to have searches, but skipping them means you could buy a property with problems you didn’t know about. Most conveyancers strongly recommend them.

Search indemnity insurance

Sometimes a seller or conveyancer suggests insurance instead of a local authority search, to save time. It can help in some cases, but it does not tell you anything about the property. Ask your conveyancer what it covers before you agree.

Tip

Read the summary your conveyancer sends you after the searches come back, and ask about anything you don’t understand.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. A solicitor can advise on your situation.

Fees and costs

Most conveyancing firms quote a fixed legal fee for the transaction, plus disbursements. Disbursements are costs the firm pays on your behalf, such as searches, Land Registry fees, identity checks and bank transfer fees. Leasehold work, new builds, shared ownership and buying with a company usually cost more because there is more to check. A good firm puts everything in writing before you instruct them, so you can compare quotes like for like and avoid surprises at completion.

Everything you need to know

Pick a topic to read more.

When you buy a property in England and Wales, the seller has to answer some questions honestly, but there is a lot they are not obliged to tell you and much they may not know. Searches fill that gap. They are requests for information sent to councils, water companies and specialist data providers, and the answers come back as written reports about the property and the land around it.

Searches are about records rather than the physical building. They can tell you that a road scheme is planned nearby, that the house is not connected to the public sewer or that the land was once used for industry. They cannot tell you about damp, cracks or a leaking roof. That is what a survey is for.

Why they matter

Problems uncovered after completion become the buyer's problem. A missing planning consent, an outstanding council notice or a private drain that needs replacing can all cost money and make the property harder to sell later. Finding these things out before exchange gives the buyer time to ask questions, renegotiate or decide not to proceed.

Lenders care about searches for the same reason. A property with a serious legal or environmental issue may be worth less than the loan secured on it, so most lenders require a standard set of searches before they release the mortgage money.

Who orders them

The buyer's conveyancer normally orders searches soon after being instructed and once money has been paid to cover them. Some sellers now commission searches in advance as part of a ready to go pack, which can save time. The buyer's conveyancer will check whether those results are recent and complete enough to rely on.

The usual set

A typical residential purchase includes a local authority search, a drainage and water search and an environmental search. Depending on where the property is, the conveyancer may add others, such as a coal mining or chancel repair search. The rest of this guide explains each in more depth than our short summary above, and what the results usually mean.

Every property is different. Your conveyancer will decide which searches suit the property you are buying and explain what the results mean for you.

The local authority search is often the one people have heard of. It comes in two parts and covers information held by the council about the property and its immediate area.

Local land charges

The first part is a search of the local land charges register. This records things that bind the property whoever owns it, such as conditional planning permissions, tree preservation orders, listed building status, conservation areas and some financial charges for work the council has carried out. Responsibility for this register is gradually moving from individual councils to HM Land Registry, so where the search is sent depends on the area.

Enquiries of the council

The second part is a standard set of questions to the council, often called the CON29 enquiries. They cover a wide range of topics, including the following.

  • Planning and building control history for the property
  • Whether the road outside is adopted and maintained by the council
  • Road, rail and other transport schemes planned nearby
  • Outstanding notices, such as enforcement or repair notices
  • Whether the land has been identified as contaminated
  • Smoke control orders and some environmental matters
  • Compulsory purchase orders affecting the property

Optional enquiries

There is also a list of optional questions that the conveyancer can add when relevant. These cover things like common land and village greens, public paths crossing the land, pipelines, houseboat moorings and noise abatement. They are not ordered for every purchase, but they can matter a great deal for rural properties or homes near open land.

What it does not cover

The local authority search is about the property itself and its immediate frontage. It will not usually tell you about a planning application on the plot next door or a large development a few streets away. Some buyers look at the council's planning portal themselves to get a sense of what is happening in the wider area.

Drainage and water

The drainage and water search goes to the local water company. It confirms whether the property is connected to the public water supply and the public sewer, and whether public sewers run within the boundaries. That last point matters because building over or near a public sewer can need permission and can limit future extensions.

The search also covers whether surface water drains to the public system, who supplies the water and whether the supply is metered. If a property is not on mains drainage, it may use a septic tank, cesspit or small treatment plant. Those systems have their own rules and maintenance costs, and the conveyancer will usually ask the seller further questions about them.

Environmental searches

An environmental search is a desktop report produced by a specialist provider. It draws on historical maps, official datasets and other records to assess risks to the property. Nobody visits the site. The report usually looks at the following.

  • Contaminated land from past industrial or commercial use
  • Flood risk from rivers, the sea, surface water and groundwater
  • Ground stability, including natural subsidence risks
  • Radon gas in the area
  • Nearby landfill and waste sites
  • Energy infrastructure, such as oil and gas exploration or wind and solar projects

Most reports give an overall pass or a further action rating. A further action rating does not mean the property is unsafe. It means something in the data needs a closer look, which may be a more detailed report, a question to the seller or a conversation with an insurer.

Flood risk and insurance

Flood findings can affect whether buildings insurance is available and at what cost. Buyers sometimes find it useful to get an insurance quote before exchange if a report shows a higher flood risk. Your conveyancer can explain what the report says, and an insurer or broker can explain the insurance side.

Beyond the usual three, there are a range of searches that are ordered only when the location or the property calls for them. A conveyancer decides which are needed based on where the property is and what the title and other searches show.

Mining searches

Large parts of England and Wales have a history of coal mining. In those areas a search of coal mining records shows whether the property is within an area of past or present mining, whether there have been subsidence claims and whether there are known mine entries nearby. Other mining searches cover areas known for tin, clay, limestone, brine or other extraction, such as Cornwall and parts of Cheshire.

Chancel repair liability

Some properties sit on land that historically carried a duty to contribute to repairs of a local parish church chancel. It is an unusual and old fashioned liability, but it can still arise in some places. A chancel search checks whether the property is in a parish where this could apply. If it is, the conveyancer may discuss insurance or further checks.

Searches linked to location

  • Rail and transport schemes, for properties near major routes
  • Canal and river trust searches, for waterside properties
  • Commons registration, for land near village greens or open spaces
  • Highways searches, where access to the property is unclear
  • Utility and pipeline searches, for rural or development land

Searches by the buyer's conveyancer near completion

Close to completion, the buyer's conveyancer also carries out a title search at HM Land Registry to check nothing new has been registered since the title was first checked, and a bankruptcy search against the buyer and, where relevant, the seller. These protect the buyer and the lender in the final days of the transaction.

Search results can be long and full of technical language. Most buyers rely on their conveyancer to read them and pick out what matters, but it helps to understand how they are usually presented.

What your conveyancer looks for

The conveyancer compares the search results with the title, the seller's property information form and the replies to enquiries. A planning consent in the local search should match the extension the seller says they built. A private road in the local search may explain why the title has rights of way over a driveway. Things that do not line up usually lead to more enquiries.

Common findings

  • The road is not adopted, so the owners may share maintenance costs
  • A planning consent has conditions that still apply
  • The property is in a conservation area or is listed
  • A public sewer runs under the garden
  • The environmental report shows a higher flood risk
  • There is a tree preservation order on a tree in the garden

None of these on their own means a property is a bad buy. Many homes have one or more such features. What matters is understanding the practical effect and any costs, and making sure the lender is satisfied.

What happens next

Where something needs attention, the conveyancer may raise enquiries with the seller, ask the lender whether it is acceptable, suggest further reports or explain the option of indemnity insurance. They will usually summarise the key points in a report to the buyer before exchange. Your conveyancer will look at what the findings mean for your purchase, and you decide how to proceed.

Searches are useful, but they are not a complete picture. Knowing their limits helps buyers understand why they are one part of the checks, alongside the survey, the title and the seller's answers.

What searches will not tell you

  • The physical condition of the building
  • Whether neighbours are difficult or noisy
  • Plans for land a short distance away from the property
  • Problems the council or provider does not hold records about
  • Anything that changes after the date of the search

How long results stay useful

Search results show the position on the date they were produced. If a purchase drags on, the conveyancer may need to update them, because many lenders will not rely on results that are more than a few months old. Updating can mean another fee, which is one more reason delays can be costly.

Official and personal searches

Some local searches are carried out by the council itself, and others by private search companies who inspect council records and other data. Both are widely accepted. The difference is mostly in timing and in who stands behind the results. Personal search companies usually carry insurance against errors. Your conveyancer will use the kind of search that suits the property and the lender.

What searches cost

Search fees are disbursements, meaning the conveyancer pays them to the provider on your behalf. The total depends on the area, the provider and how many searches are needed, and prices change, so a written quote from the firm is the best guide.

Questions and answers

Are searches a legal requirement?
There is no law that says a buyer must have searches. In practice, almost every mortgage lender requires them before it will lend, so buyers with a mortgage nearly always have them. Cash buyers can choose, but skipping searches means buying without information that could affect the value or use of the property. A conveyancer can explain what you would be giving up if you chose not to have them.
Which searches are ordered for a typical home?
For an ordinary house or flat, the usual set is a local authority search, a drainage and water search and an environmental search. Depending on the area and the property, the conveyancer may add a mining search, a chancel repair search or others. The decision is based on the location, the title and any lender requirements. Your conveyancer will explain why each search is being ordered.
Who pays for searches?
The buyer normally pays for searches, because they are carried out for the buyer's and lender's protection. The cost is a disbursement, which the conveyancer pays to the search provider and then includes in their bill. Many firms ask for money on account at the start so they can order searches without delay. Occasionally a seller provides searches as part of a sale pack, and the buyer may pay towards them.
Can the seller provide the searches?
Some sellers order searches in advance to speed up their sale. The buyer's conveyancer and lender then decide whether they can rely on them. Points they consider include how recent the results are, whether all the right searches were done and whether the buyer can benefit from any insurance behind them. In some cases the buyer's conveyancer will still order extra or updated searches.
How long do search results take?
It varies by area and by search. Drainage and environmental searches often come back quite quickly. Local authority searches depend on the council, and some councils take much longer than others. Personal search companies can sometimes be faster. Your conveyancer will usually tell you which searches are outstanding and when they expect them back.
Can I see the search results myself?
Yes. The searches are carried out for you, and you can ask your conveyancer for copies. Most firms send a summary in their report and attach the full results or make them available on request. The full documents can be long and technical, so the summary is usually the easiest place to start. Ask your conveyancer about anything that is not clear.
What does an unadopted road mean?
An unadopted road is one that the council does not maintain at public expense. The owners of the homes along it may be responsible for repairs, sometimes under an agreement and sometimes informally. It can also affect access rights. Many homes are on unadopted roads without any trouble, but it is something to understand before buying. Your conveyancer will check the title for access rights and ask the seller about maintenance arrangements.
Will the local search show planning applications next door?
Usually not. The local authority search is focused on the property itself. Planning applications on neighbouring land are not normally included unless they directly affect the property being bought. Many buyers look at the council's online planning register for the street and surrounding area to see what is proposed. Your conveyancer can explain what the search does and does not cover.
What is a tree preservation order?
A tree preservation order is made by the council to protect a particular tree or group of trees. It usually means you need permission before cutting down, lopping or topping the tree. It shows up in the local land charges part of the local search. It does not stop you buying the property, but it can affect plans for extensions, driveways or garden changes.
What does it mean if the property is in a conservation area?
Conservation areas are designated by councils to protect the character of an area. Living in one can mean extra controls on things like demolition, some types of extension, replacement windows and work to trees. The details depend on the area and on any extra directions the council has made. Your conveyancer will flag it, and the council's planning team can explain the local rules.
What if the search shows building work without approval?
If the seller has carried out work and the local search shows no planning permission or building regulation approval for it, the conveyancer will raise enquiries. Options might include the seller obtaining retrospective approval, an indemnity insurance policy or a price discussion. Each has advantages and drawbacks, and the lender's view matters too. Your conveyancer will explain what applies in your purchase.
What are local land charges?
Local land charges are restrictions and obligations registered against land by public bodies. They include things like conditional planning permissions, listed building status, tree preservation orders and some charges for work the council has paid for. They bind whoever owns the property, so a buyer takes the property subject to them. The register is gradually moving from councils to HM Land Registry, and your conveyancer will search whichever holds the records for your area.
What if the house is not on mains drainage?
Homes without mains drainage usually rely on a septic tank, cesspit or small sewage treatment plant. These systems need regular emptying and maintenance and are covered by environmental rules on discharges. The conveyancer will usually ask the seller for details of the system, where it is, whether it is shared and how it has been maintained. A surveyor or drainage specialist can look at its condition.
Why does a sewer under the garden matter?
Public sewers within a property's boundaries belong to the water company, which has rights to access them for maintenance. Building over or close to them often needs the water company's agreement. If you are planning an extension, a sewer in the wrong place could add cost or limit what you can do. The drainage search shows whether public sewers run within the boundaries.
What does further action on an environmental report mean?
A further action result means the report has found something that needs a closer look. It might relate to past land use, flood risk or ground stability. It does not automatically mean the property is unsafe or that the purchase cannot go ahead. The conveyancer will explain the finding and may suggest a more detailed report, questions to the seller or a check with an insurer.
Will a flood risk finding stop me getting a mortgage?
Not necessarily. Lenders look at flood risk alongside whether buildings insurance is available on normal terms. A higher risk finding may mean the lender asks for evidence of insurance before releasing funds. Some buyers get an insurance quote before exchange for peace of mind. Your conveyancer and mortgage adviser can explain how your lender is likely to treat the finding.
What is radon and should it worry me?
Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas found in some parts of the country. Environmental searches usually show whether the property is in an area where higher levels are more likely. A finding does not mean the home has a problem, and testing and simple measures are available where levels are raised. Your conveyancer can point you towards official information, and specialists can carry out testing.
Can an environmental search be wrong?
An environmental search is based on records and data, not a visit to the property. It can only reflect the information available to the provider, and records are sometimes incomplete or out of date. Reports usually explain the data they rely on and come with professional indemnity cover. If something in a report looks inconsistent with what you know about the area, your conveyancer can ask the provider or arrange a more detailed check.
When is a coal mining search needed?
A coal mining search is usually ordered when the property is in an area that has been mined for coal in the past or where mining still takes place. Conveyancers use official guidance and mapping to decide when one is needed. The report shows past and present mining, known mine entries and any subsidence claims. Other specialist mining searches exist for areas known for tin, clay, brine or other materials.
What is chancel repair liability?
It is an old legal liability under which some landowners can be asked to pay towards repairs to the chancel of a parish church. It only affects land in certain parishes and is uncommon in practice. A chancel search shows whether the property is in a parish where the liability could exist. If it is, the conveyancer may suggest insurance or explain other options.
Do flats need different searches?
The core searches are usually the same for a flat as for a house. What changes is the leasehold side. The conveyancer also needs information from the freeholder or managing agent about service charges, building insurance, planned works and any disputes. That information comes through a separate enquiry pack rather than a search. Your conveyancer will request both.
Are there searches for new build homes?
New build purchases still need searches, and some extra checks are common. The conveyancer may look at planning consents for the whole development, agreements for the roads and sewers to be adopted, and any estate management arrangements. Because roads and drains on new estates are often not yet adopted, the results need careful reading. Your conveyancer will explain what they mean for the plot you are buying.
What searches are done just before completion?
Shortly before completion the buyer's conveyancer checks HM Land Registry to make sure nothing new has been registered against the title since it was first checked. They also carry out a bankruptcy search, which lenders require. Both searches give a short period of priority, which protects the buyer while the purchase is completed and registered.
Why might my conveyancer order a highways search?
A highways search is usually ordered when it is unclear whether the property has direct access to a public road. It shows where the publicly maintained highway ends, which matters if there is a strip of land between the boundary and the road. A gap like that could affect access or create a ransom strip owned by someone else. Your conveyancer will explain why they think the search is needed.
Can I pull out because of a search result?
Before contracts are exchanged, buyers are not legally committed and can withdraw for any reason, including a worrying search result. Whether that is the right choice depends on how serious the finding is, what it would cost to deal with and how it affects your plans. Your conveyancer will explain the legal side, and you make the decision. After exchange, withdrawing has serious consequences.
What is search indemnity insurance?
Search indemnity insurance is sometimes offered instead of a local authority search, usually to save time. It covers certain financial losses if a matter that a search would have revealed later reduces the property's value. It does not give you any information about the property, and some lenders will not accept it. Your conveyancer can explain what a particular policy covers and its limits.
Can a price be renegotiated after searches?
Yes, until exchange the price can be renegotiated. A buyer might ask for a reduction if a search reveals something that will cost money to deal with, such as a private drainage system needing work or a liability for road repairs. Negotiations usually happen through the estate agent. The seller does not have to agree, so the outcome depends on both sides.
What happens if results are out of date?
If a purchase takes longer than expected, search results can age. Many lenders set a limit on how old searches can be at completion. If that limit is close, the conveyancer may need to update some or all searches, which usually means another fee. Keeping the transaction moving helps avoid this. Your conveyancer will tell you if updates are needed.

Does a clear set of searches mean the property is problem free?
Not quite. Clear searches mean the records checked did not show anything of concern on the date they were produced. They do not cover the physical condition of the building, matters outside the records searched or anything that happens later. That is why searches sit alongside a survey, a review of the title and the seller's replies to enquiries. Your conveyancer will explain how these fit together in your purchase.

More questions people ask

When should I instruct a conveyancer to sell?
Ideally when you put your home on the market. The firm can carry out identity checks, obtain your title, send you the forms and request any leasehold information straight away. That way the contract pack is ready to send on the day you accept an offer, which can make your property more attractive to buyers.
What does my conveyancer need from me at the start?
Your conveyancer will need photo ID, proof of address, your mortgage lender and account details, and the details of your estate agent. They will also ask you to complete the property information form and the fittings and contents form, and to send any certificates, guarantees and planning documents you have.
Do I need my original deeds?
For most registered properties, the official record is held electronically by HM Land Registry, so original paper deeds are not essential. Any old deeds you have can still be useful, especially if they explain rights or boundaries. If your property is unregistered, the deeds are vital and your conveyancer will need to see them.
Do I need an Energy Performance Certificate?
Homes marketed for sale in England and Wales generally need a valid Energy Performance Certificate, and the agent will usually arrange this before marketing begins. If you already have one that is still valid, you can usually use it. Your estate agent can confirm what is needed.
Can I sell if my name has changed since I bought?
Yes. If you have changed your name through marriage, civil partnership or deed poll, your conveyancer will usually ask for evidence, such as a marriage certificate, and may update the register or explain the change to the buyer. It is a common issue and rarely causes a problem if dealt with early.
What if I own the property with someone else?
All registered owners usually need to agree to the sale and sign the contract and transfer. If one owner has died, your conveyancer will need to see the death certificate and may need probate depending on how the property was owned. If owners disagree about selling, it is a different situation and legal advice is needed.
Can I use the same firm as my buyer?
Professional rules usually prevent one firm acting for both buyer and seller in a residential sale, because their interests conflict. There are limited exceptions, but in practice most transactions have a separate firm on each side. Your conveyancer will tell you if a conflict arises.
