Immigration matters can affect where you live, who you live with, whether you can work and how secure your future feels. When so much is at stake, the person you trust with your case matters a great deal. This guide explains how immigration advice is regulated in the UK, how to check an adviser, what good practice looks like and what to do if something goes wrong.

Our short guide above covers the basics. This page goes further, with more detail on the different regulators, the questions people often ask before instructing someone, how fees are usually set out and the warning signs that something is not right.

Why regulation matters

In the UK it is a criminal offence for someone to provide immigration advice or services in the course of a business unless they are qualified to do so under the law. That rule exists to protect people from advisers who are not trained, not insured or not accountable to anyone. A regulated adviser has to meet professional standards, follow rules about how they treat clients and handle money, and answer to a regulator and a complaints scheme.

Using someone who is not regulated can cause real harm. Mistakes in applications can lead to delays, refusals or lost fees, and there may be no way to recover money or hold the person to account.

Different kinds of help

People look for immigration help for many reasons. Some want a one off consultation to understand where they stand. Some want an adviser to prepare and submit an application from start to finish. Others need help responding to a Home Office letter, dealing with a refusal or preparing for an appeal. Businesses may need help with sponsorship duties. The right adviser is one who is regulated for the type of work involved and has experience with matters like yours.

What this guide does not cover

This guide is about finding and working with an adviser. It does not explain immigration rules or whether any application might succeed. Only a regulated adviser can look at your circumstances and advise you. Leaflegal does not give immigration advice.

Several kinds of professional can lawfully give immigration advice in the UK. They are regulated by different bodies, and each has its own register and complaints process.

Solicitors

Solicitors in England and Wales, and the firms they work in, are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Solicitors can provide immigration advice and services as part of their wider legal practice. Many firms have dedicated immigration teams, and some focus on particular areas such as family, business or appeals work. Solicitors in Scotland and Northern Ireland are regulated by their own professional bodies.

CILEx members

Chartered legal executives and some other authorised practitioners are regulated by CILEx Regulation. Some are authorised to carry out immigration work in their own right, and many work within solicitors' firms.

Barristers

Barristers are regulated by the Bar Standards Board. Many immigration barristers focus on appeals and court work, often instructed by a solicitor. Some are authorised to accept instructions directly from the public, which is known as public access.

Immigration Advice Authority advisers

Many immigration advisers are not lawyers but are registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, the body that took over from the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner in 2025. Registered advisers work at one of three levels, which limit the kind of work they can do.

  • Level 1, covering initial advice and straightforward applications
  • Level 2, covering more complex casework
  • Level 3, covering the widest range of work, including representation at appeals

An adviser registered at a lower level should only take on work within their level and their registered categories. If your matter needs work beyond that, they should say so and refer you on.

Other exempt advisers

Some other organisations, such as certain charities and public bodies, can give limited advice under special arrangements. If you are getting help from an organisation like this, they should be able to explain how they are regulated or exempt.

Checking that an adviser is regulated takes only a few minutes and is one of the most useful things you can do before sharing your documents or paying anything.

Using the registers

  • For solicitors and firms in England and Wales, the Solicitors Regulation Authority register and the Law Society's Find a Solicitor service
  • For CILEx regulated practitioners, the CILEx Regulation register
  • For barristers, the Bar Standards Board's Barristers' Register
  • For non lawyer advisers, the Immigration Advice Authority's online adviser finder, which shows the level and categories they are registered for

Search for both the firm and the individual who will handle your case. Check that the name and address match what you have been told. Regulated firms usually display their regulator and registration number on their website and letters.

Checking the right person

In some firms, the regulated person is not the person you speak to day to day. That can be fine, as long as the work is properly supervised. It is reasonable to ask who will be working on your matter, who supervises them and who is responsible if something goes wrong.

Looking at experience

Being regulated is the minimum. Many people also want an adviser who has handled similar matters before. You can ask how often they deal with your type of case, whether they have dealt with any complications you are worried about and how they usually run matters like yours. A good adviser will answer these questions openly without making promises about the result.

Reviews and recommendations

Online reviews and personal recommendations can give a sense of how an adviser treats clients, how responsive they are and how clearly they explain things. They cannot tell you whether an adviser is regulated, so they are best used alongside a register check rather than instead of one.

If you cannot find an adviser on any register, or the details do not match, it is sensible to pause before going further and ask the adviser to explain. A genuine regulated adviser will be able to point you to their entry straight away.

Fees for immigration work vary widely, and comparing advisers is much easier when you know how charges are usually set out.

Common ways of charging

  • A fixed fee for an initial consultation
  • A fixed fee for a defined piece of work, such as preparing one application
  • Staged fixed fees, with separate amounts for different stages
  • Hourly rates, often used for more complex or uncertain work
  • Separate charges for extras such as urgent work, translations or attending interviews or hearings

Home Office and other fees

Government fees, such as Home Office application fees and the Immigration Health Surcharge where it applies, are separate from the adviser's fees and are paid to the government, not to the adviser. These amounts change from time to time, so check GOV.UK for current figures. Other third party costs might include translations, document certification, medical reports or biometric appointments.

What to expect in writing

A regulated adviser should give you clear written information about their fees and what is included before they start work. This is usually set out in a client care letter or terms of business. It should explain what happens if the work takes longer than expected, if things change or if you decide to stop. Reading it carefully and asking about anything unclear before signing helps avoid misunderstandings.

Paying

Regulated advisers follow rules about handling client money. Many ask for payment in advance for fixed fee work, or money on account for hourly work. Be cautious about large cash payments with no receipt, or requests to pay into personal accounts.

Most immigration advisers work hard and honestly. Sadly, some people take advantage of others at a stressful time. Knowing the common warning signs can help you avoid problems.

Signs to be careful of

  • They cannot or will not tell you who regulates them
  • Their name does not appear on any register
  • They guarantee a particular outcome or say they have special contacts at the Home Office
  • They ask you to give false information or leave out true information
  • They pressure you to decide or pay immediately
  • They will not give you their fees in writing
  • They keep your original passport or documents without good reason
  • They are hard to contact once you have paid

Scams claiming to be official

Some scammers contact people pretending to be from the Home Office or another government body, often by phone or message, and demand urgent payment to avoid removal or a penalty. Government bodies do not normally ask for payment in this way. If you receive a contact like this, it is wise to check it through official channels before doing anything.

Unregulated helpers

Friends, community contacts or travel agents sometimes offer to help with applications. Even if well meaning, they may not be allowed to give immigration advice, and errors can be costly. Translators and interpreters can help with language, but they should not be advising on your matter unless they are regulated.

Reporting concerns

If you think someone is giving immigration advice without being regulated, you can report them to the Immigration Advice Authority. Concerns about a regulated adviser can be raised with their regulator.

Once you have chosen an adviser, a good working relationship makes a real difference. Both sides have a part to play.

What a good adviser does

  • Explains things in plain language and checks you understand
  • Tells you what they need from you and by when
  • Keeps you updated and replies within a reasonable time
  • Is honest about risks and uncertainty
  • Tells you about any deadlines as soon as they know of them
  • Keeps your information confidential

Your part

Advisers can only work with the information they have. Being open about your history, including past applications, refusals or problems, helps them give accurate advice. Sending documents when asked and telling them about any change in your circumstances, such as a new address, job or relationship, keeps your matter on track.

Letters and deadlines

If you receive any letter or email from the Home Office or a tribunal, it is important to send a copy to your adviser straight away. Some deadlines are very short. Keeping a record of when each letter arrived can help.

If you want to change adviser

You can usually change adviser if you are unhappy. The new adviser will need your file, and the old adviser should provide it, although there may be questions about unpaid fees. Your client care letter should explain how this works.

Complaints

Every regulated adviser must have a complaints process. If you are not satisfied with their response, complaints about solicitors and many other lawyers can go to the Legal Ombudsman, and complaints about advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority can be made to that authority.

Questions and answers

Is it illegal to give immigration advice without being regulated?
Yes. In the UK it is a criminal offence for someone to provide immigration advice or services in the course of a business unless they are qualified to do so. Qualified people include solicitors, barristers, CILEx regulated practitioners and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, along with some exempt organisations. This protects people from untrained or unaccountable advisers. If someone offering help cannot say how they are regulated, that is a serious warning sign.
What is the Immigration Advice Authority?
The Immigration Advice Authority is the body that regulates immigration advisers who are not lawyers. It took over from the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner in 2025. It registers advisers, sets standards, deals with complaints about registered advisers and takes action against people giving advice illegally. Its online adviser finder lets you check whether someone is registered and at what level.
What do the adviser levels mean?
Advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority are authorised at level 1, level 2 or level 3. Level 1 covers initial advice and simpler applications. Level 2 covers more complex casework. Level 3 covers the widest range of work, including representing clients at appeals. Advisers should only take on work within their level. If your matter is beyond it, they should tell you and help you find someone suitably authorised.
Is a solicitor better than an Immigration Advice Authority adviser?
Not necessarily. Both can be properly regulated and experienced. Solicitors are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority and can do all kinds of immigration work. Registered advisers are limited to their level and categories but may specialise heavily in certain types of matter. What matters most is that the person is regulated for the work involved and has relevant experience. You can ask any adviser about both.
Can a barrister help me directly?
Some barristers are authorised to take instructions directly from members of the public under the public access scheme. Others only work when instructed by a solicitor or other authorised adviser. Barristers are often involved in appeals and court work. The Bar Standards Board's register shows whether a barrister is practising and whether they can accept public access work.
Are immigration advisers in Scotland and Northern Ireland regulated differently?
Immigration law applies across the UK, but lawyers in Scotland and Northern Ireland are regulated by their own professional bodies. The Immigration Advice Authority regulates non lawyer advisers across the whole UK. Wherever you are, the same basic point applies. Check that the person helping you is regulated for immigration work and can tell you who regulates them.
Do regulated advisers have insurance?
Regulated advisers are generally required to have professional indemnity insurance or equivalent cover, which can protect clients if a mistake causes loss. The exact arrangements depend on the regulator. This is one of the reasons using a regulated adviser matters. An unregulated person is unlikely to have any such cover, which can leave clients with no way to recover losses if something goes wrong. You can ask any adviser about their cover.
What questions should I ask before instructing an adviser?
Useful questions include who regulates them, who will handle your matter day to day, who supervises that person, how often they deal with matters like yours, what the fee covers and what could increase it, how they will keep you updated and how quickly they usually reply. You might also ask what they need from you to get started. A good adviser will welcome questions and answer clearly.
Does it matter if an adviser is local to me?
Many immigration advisers work remotely, using phone, video calls, email and secure portals, so being local is not essential. Some people prefer face to face meetings, especially if they find it easier to explain things in person or have lots of paper documents. If attending interviews or hearings might be needed, location can matter more. It is reasonable to ask how an adviser usually works.
Can I find an adviser who speaks my language?
Many advisers speak more than one language, and many work with professional interpreters. If you would feel more comfortable discussing your matter in your own language, it is reasonable to ask about this when you first make contact. Using a professional interpreter rather than a friend or relative can help with accuracy and confidentiality. Ask whether interpreter costs are included in the fee.
My employer has recommended an adviser. Can I use them?
Some employers work with advisers on sponsorship matters and may offer their help to employees. That can be convenient, but it is worth being clear whose interests the adviser represents. If the adviser acts for your employer, there may be limits on what they can advise you about personally. You can ask the adviser to explain who their client is and whether you might want separate advice.
Should I get more than one quote?
Many people find it helpful to speak to more than one adviser before deciding, especially for complex or high value matters. Comparing quotes is easier when you check what each includes, such as the number of meetings, preparation of documents and handling of Home Office correspondence. The cheapest quote is not always the best value. Clear communication and relevant experience matter too.
Can a friend or community organisation help me instead?
Friends and community groups can offer valuable support, such as helping you gather documents or come to meetings. Unless they are regulated or exempt, they cannot lawfully give immigration advice or prepare applications as a service. Some charities are registered or exempt and offer free help. If an organisation offers advice, ask how they are regulated.
How quickly can an adviser start work?
It varies. Some advisers can offer a consultation within days, while busy firms may have a waiting list. If you have a deadline, such as a date on a Home Office letter or a visa end date, mention it when you first make contact so the adviser can tell you whether they can help in time. Many advisers offer urgent appointments, sometimes for an extra fee, and will tell you about that in advance.
How much does an immigration adviser cost?
Fees vary widely depending on the type of matter, its complexity, the adviser's experience and how they charge. Some offer fixed fees, others charge by the hour. Government fees are separate. Because prices differ so much and change over time, the best approach is to ask each adviser for a written breakdown of what they charge and what that includes before you agree to anything.
Are Home Office fees included in an adviser's quote?
Usually not. Home Office application fees and the Immigration Health Surcharge, where it applies, are government charges paid to the government. Advisers normally quote their own professional fees separately. Some may help you pay government fees as part of the application process. Ask the adviser to confirm which costs are theirs and which are government charges, and check GOV.UK for the current government fees.
Can I get free immigration advice?
Some charities and law centres offer free advice, and legal aid is available for some immigration and asylum matters. GOV.UK has information about legal aid and how to find a legal aid provider. Free services are often in high demand and availability varies by area. Leaflegal introduces people to regulated firms and advisers who usually charge for their services, and our introduction service itself is free to use.
What should a client care letter include?
A client care letter or terms of business usually sets out who will handle your matter, who supervises them, what work is included, the fees and how they are calculated, when payment is due, how you will be kept updated and how to make a complaint. It should also explain what happens if you want to stop. Read it carefully and ask questions before signing.
Can I get a refund if I change my mind?
It depends on the terms you agreed and how much work has been done. Some advisers refund unused money on account, while fixed fees may be partly or fully earned at certain stages. If you agreed terms at a distance, such as online, consumer rules may give you a short cancellation period in some cases. Your client care letter should explain the position.
Why do some advisers charge for a first consultation?
A first consultation often involves the adviser reading your documents, listening to your history and giving you initial advice on your situation. That takes professional time, so many advisers charge a fixed fee for it. Some offer a short free call first to understand what you need and whether they can help, before a paid consultation. Some deduct the consultation fee if you then instruct them. Ask how it works when you book.
What if my adviser stops replying?
Start by contacting them in writing, by email or letter, setting out what you need and by when. Check whether anyone else at the firm can help, such as a supervisor. If you still hear nothing, especially if you have a deadline, you may need to find another adviser quickly and ask for your file. If you are unhappy with the service, you can use the firm's complaints process.
How do I complain about an immigration adviser?
Start with the adviser's own complaints procedure, which should be set out in your client care letter. If you are not satisfied, complaints about service from solicitors, barristers and CILEx practitioners can usually go to the Legal Ombudsman. Complaints about advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority go to that authority. Conduct concerns can also be reported to the relevant regulator.
What should I do if I think I have been scammed?
Stop paying any further money and keep all messages, receipts and documents. Report the matter to Action Fraud and, if the person claimed to give immigration advice, to the Immigration Advice Authority. If your documents have been kept, ask for them back in writing. A regulated adviser can then look at where your matter stands and whether any urgent steps are needed.
Can I get my documents back from an adviser?
Your original documents belong to you, and an adviser should return them when they are no longer needed for your matter, or when you ask. If you change adviser, your file can be passed on, although there can sometimes be questions about unpaid fees. If an adviser refuses to return your documents, you can raise this through their complaints process and with their regulator.
What if I already used an unregulated adviser?
Many people only discover later that the person who helped them was not regulated. If an application has already been made, a regulated adviser can review what was submitted and explain where things stand. If you have documents or letters connected to it, gather them together. You can also report the unregulated adviser to the Immigration Advice Authority.
What if I disagree with my adviser's approach?
It is reasonable to ask your adviser to explain why they recommend a particular approach and what the alternatives are. Good advisers expect questions and should explain their reasoning in plain language. If you still have doubts, you can ask for a second opinion from another regulated adviser. Keep in mind any deadlines while you do so, and keep your current adviser informed if you are considering changing.

More questions people ask

What is a family visa?
It is a general term for immigration applications where someone comes to or stays in the UK because of a family member here. It can involve children, parents, grandparents and other relatives. We cover partners on a separate page. A regulated adviser can explain what a family application might involve for your family.
Can Leaflegal tell me which visa my family needs?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and never does. We do not ask about your immigration history or assess your family's chances. We introduce you to a firm or adviser who is regulated, and they will look at your family's circumstances and explain your options.
Can you help with relatives who are overseas?
Yes. We introduce families to regulated advisers who handle matters for people inside and outside the UK. Many advisers work by phone, video and email, so it does not matter where each family member is. Tell us where your relatives are when you make your enquiry.
My question involves several family members. Is that a problem?
Not at all. Many family matters involve more than one person. Tell us briefly who is involved when you enquire. The adviser will want to understand the whole family and can explain how they would handle linked matters together.
Can I enquire on behalf of a relative?
Yes. Family members often make the first enquiry for a relative, particularly an older parent or someone abroad. The adviser will usually need to speak to the person concerned at some point, and may need their agreement before acting for them. Tell us your relationship to the person and the best way to reach you both.
Is my enquiry confidential?
Yes. We share your enquiry only with the one firm we introduce you to, and only with your agreement. We never share it with advertising platforms. The firm will treat what you tell it as confidential under the rules of its regulator.
Why do family rules change so often?
Immigration policy is reviewed regularly by the government, and the rules for family members have changed many times. Fees, guidance and requirements can all move. That is why it is risky to rely on what worked for another family in the past. A regulated adviser explains things based on the rules that apply now.
What happens at the first consultation?
The adviser will ask about your family, where everyone lives, each person's status and what you hope to achieve. They will explain how they could help and what it might cost. You do not need every document. It is also your chance to decide whether you want to work with them.
Who in the family should attend?
Usually the family member who knows the situation best, along with anyone closely involved. Relatives abroad can often join by video. Let the firm know who will be attending, and agree among yourselves who will be the main point of contact afterwards. Having a short list of questions agreed in advance helps make sure nothing important is forgotten.
Is the first consultation free?
It depends on the firm. Some offer a short free call first, and others charge for the first consultation. The firm should tell you before the consultation whether there is a fee and how much it is, so that there are no surprises.
What should we have ready?
It helps to have passports, details of any current visas, any Home Office letters and key family documents to hand. A simple family tree with names, dates of birth and where everyone lives can be very useful. The adviser will tell you what else they need afterwards.
Should we mention past problems?
Yes. Advisers can only help properly if they know the whole family's history, including past refusals or problems with earlier visas. Leaving things out can cause bigger difficulties later. What you tell a regulated adviser is confidential under the rules of their regulator.
Can we have the consultation in our own language?
Some firms have advisers who speak other languages, and some can arrange interpreters. Mention your family's preferred language when you enquire. It is important that everyone involved understands what is being explained.
What happens after the consultation?
Most firms follow up in writing with a summary of what was discussed, what they can do for each family member and what it would cost. Read it carefully and share it with anyone else involved. If you decide to go ahead, the firm will send terms of business to agree before work starts.
