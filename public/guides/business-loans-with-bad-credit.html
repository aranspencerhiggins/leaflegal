What to know about business loans with bad credit

Updated 4 November 2026 · 6 min read · By the Loanleaf team

A poor credit history makes borrowing harder, but it doesn’t always make it impossible. Some lenders specialise in businesses whose records are less than perfect, and many look at how a business is trading now as well as at its past. The honest picture is that options become narrower, terms are often less favourable and some applications won’t succeed. Understanding how lenders view credit problems helps you decide whether to apply now, what to apply for and when it might be better to wait.

What counts as bad credit

Lenders usually check both the business’s credit file and the personal credit files of its directors. The things that most often cause concern are:

  • Missed or late payments on loans, credit cards, utilities or supplier accounts.
  • Defaults, recorded when an account has been in arrears long enough for the lender to end the agreement.
  • County court judgments (CCJs), made when a court orders the business or a director to repay a debt.
  • Insolvency events, such as a previous company liquidation, an individual voluntary arrangement or bankruptcy.
  • A thin file, where there simply isn’t much credit history to assess.

How lenders weigh credit problems

Not all credit problems are treated equally. Lenders typically consider how recent the issue is, how large it was, whether it has been settled and whether it was a one-off or part of a pattern. A small, satisfied CCJ from several years ago is viewed very differently from several recent defaults. Most negative entries stay on a credit file for six years, but their influence usually fades as they get older.

Context matters too. A missed payment during a well-documented difficult period, followed by a clean record since, tells a different story from ongoing arrears. Many lenders will listen to a clear explanation, especially if your current bank statements show the business trading steadily.

What may still be possible

Depending on your circumstances, some types of finance are less dependent on credit history than a standard unsecured loan:

  • Secured loans, where property or other assets give the lender extra comfort.
  • Asset finance, where the equipment or vehicle being bought acts as security.
  • Invoice finance, where the credit strength of your customers can matter as much as your own.
  • Merchant cash advances, which are repaid as a share of card takings and are often assessed mainly on recent card sales.

These options still involve checks and none of them is guaranteed. They can also cost more than mainstream lending, so compare the total cost carefully. A smaller amount, a shorter term or a larger deposit on an asset can sometimes make an application more acceptable to a lender, and can be a sensible way to rebuild a track record before borrowing more.

Borrowing responsibly with a weaker record

When credit is harder to find, it is tempting to accept the first offer. Take a step back first. Ask whether the borrowing will genuinely improve the business’s position, and whether the repayments are affordable in a quiet month, not just a good one. Taking on expensive debt to cover ongoing losses can make an existing problem worse.

Be cautious of anyone who promises approval regardless of credit history, asks for an upfront fee before arranging anything or pressures you to sign quickly. Read the full terms, including fees and early repayment charges, and take independent advice before signing a personal guarantee or putting property up as security. If the business is struggling with existing debts, free and impartial help is available from organisations such as Business Debtline.

Improving your position

If now isn’t the right time, there are practical steps that can strengthen a future application:

  • Check the business’s and directors’ credit files and ask for any errors to be corrected.
  • Settle outstanding CCJs where you can, and make sure they are marked as satisfied.
  • Keep every existing account up to date, as a run of on-time payments helps over time.
  • Avoid making several credit applications in a short period.
  • Keep business bank accounts tidy, with no returned payments or unarranged overdraft use.
  • Make sure filed accounts are up to date and your addresses are correct on the electoral roll and at Companies House.

Being upfront

The most useful thing you can do is be open about your history from the start. Lenders and brokers will find credit problems when they run their checks, and disclosing them early lets them focus on options that are realistic for your circumstances. A specialist finance broker can explain which lenders are more likely to consider a business with your profile, and when it may be better to wait.
