How to extend a lease

Your options when the lease on your flat is getting shorter.

  • Plain English
  • General information, not advice
  • About lease extensions
Make an Enquiry
All guides

Key points

  • There is a formal statutory route and an informal route
  • You will usually need a solicitor and a surveyor
  • Starting early gives you more options

Two main routes

  • The statutory route, a formal process using your legal right, with set steps and deadlines
  • The informal route, agreeing terms directly with your freeholder

Who you need

A solicitor who deals with leasehold, and a surveyor to value the premium. They usually work together.

Timing

Start well before you plan to sell or remortgage. A statutory extension can take several months.

The statutory route in more detail

There used to be a rule that you had to own the flat for two years before using the statutory route, but the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 removed it from 31 January 2025. Your solicitor will confirm what applies to you. Under the statutory route, your solicitor serves a formal notice on the freeholder setting out your proposed premium, and the freeholder replies with a counter notice. If you can’t agree, a tribunal can decide.

The informal route in more detail

An informal deal can be quicker and more flexible. The freeholder can set their own terms, though, so a solicitor should check the new lease carefully before you sign.

What affects the premium

  • How many years are left on the lease
  • The value of the flat
  • The ground rent
  • Whether the lease is below 80 years, which can make extending more expensive
Tip

The rules on leasehold are changing. Ask your solicitor which rules apply to your lease right now.

Extending a lease means replacing or adding to the remaining term on a leasehold property so it has many more years to run. Most people look into it because their flat is getting harder to sell or remortgage, because a buyer's lender has raised the lease length, or because they want to protect the value of their home for the long term.

Our short guide above sets out the two main routes and who you will need. This page goes into more detail about how each route works in practice, what drives the cost, what the paperwork looks like and where things can go wrong. It is general information about England and Wales. A solicitor will look at your lease and explain what applies to you.

Why lease length matters

A lease is a wasting asset. Every year it gets shorter, and as it does the flat becomes less attractive to buyers and lenders. Many lenders set a minimum remaining term at the end of the mortgage, and a lease that falls below their limit can stop a sale or remortgage in its tracks. Owners who leave it late sometimes find the extension has to be done in a hurry, with less room to negotiate.

Who can extend

Many owners of long leases of flats have a legal right to a lease extension under the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993. Owners of leasehold houses have separate rights under different legislation. Whether you can use the statutory route depends on the type of lease and property, and the rules have been changing under recent reforms. A solicitor will check this at the start.

A change worth knowing about

For many years leaseholders had to have owned their flat for at least two years before they could use the statutory route. That requirement was removed from 31 January 2025 by the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024, so new owners can now often start the statutory process sooner. Some older guidance online still mentions the two year rule, so it is worth checking the date of anything you read.

The people involved

  • You, the leaseholder, and your solicitor
  • A specialist valuer or surveyor who works out the premium
  • The landlord, which is usually the freeholder, and their solicitor and valuer
  • Sometimes an intermediate landlord, if there is more than one layer of lease
  • Your mortgage lender, which will need to consent to the new lease

The statutory route uses the legal right to an extension. It follows a set procedure with formal notices and deadlines, and if the two sides cannot agree terms, a tribunal can decide them. It gives leaseholders certainty about the outcome, because the landlord cannot refuse a valid claim.

What you get

Under the rules that have applied for many years, a statutory extension adds 90 years to the remaining term and reduces the ground rent to a peppercorn, meaning nothing is payable. The 2024 Act provides for this to change to a 990 year extension, and other terms may change as reforms come in. Your solicitor will confirm which terms apply when you start.

1. Valuation

Before any notice is served, a valuer inspects the flat and works out the likely premium. This figure guides the offer made in the notice and helps you understand the likely range of the final price.

2. The tenant's notice

Your solicitor prepares and serves a formal notice on the landlord, often called a section 42 notice. It sets out your details, the property and your proposed premium, and gives the landlord a date by which to respond. Serving the notice fixes the date for valuation and starts the formal clock. The notice has to be correct in form and content, so errors can be costly.

3. The landlord's response

The landlord usually has the right to ask for a deposit and to inspect the flat. They then serve a counter notice by the deadline in your notice. In it, the landlord either accepts the claim and proposes their own premium and terms, or disputes your right to extend. If the landlord fails to serve a valid counter notice in time, special rules apply that can favour the leaseholder.

4. Negotiation

The two valuers usually negotiate the premium, and the solicitors negotiate the wording of the new lease. Most claims settle at this stage.

5. Tribunal if needed

If terms are not agreed, either side can apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) within set time limits. Missing those limits can cause the claim to fail. Many applications still settle before a hearing.

6. Completion

Once terms are agreed or decided, the new lease is signed, the premium and any costs are paid, and the new lease is registered at HM Land Registry.

The informal route means negotiating an extension directly with the landlord without serving a formal notice. It can be quicker and cheaper in the right circumstances, and some landlords prefer it. But because it does not use the legal right, the landlord is free to set their own terms or say no.

Why people choose it

  • It can avoid some of the costs and formality of the statutory process
  • A cooperative landlord may agree a fair deal quickly
  • It allows more flexibility over the length of the extension and other terms
  • It can be used where the statutory route is not available for some reason

Things to watch

Informal offers do not always include a peppercorn ground rent. Some keep a ground rent, or include one that rises over time, which can affect value and mortgageability. Some add new terms to the lease or a shorter extension than the statutory route would give. The premium can look attractive while the overall deal is less so. A solicitor and valuer can compare an informal offer with what the statutory route might provide.

Moving between routes

It is common to start informally and switch to the statutory route if talks stall, or to serve a statutory notice to fix the valuation date and then agree terms through negotiation. Equally, an informal offer can be accepted after a statutory claim has begun if the terms suit both sides. Your solicitor will explain the timing and risks of each approach in your case.

Getting the paperwork right

Whichever route is used, the result is a legal document that will govern your home for decades. It needs to be correctly drafted, consented to by your lender and registered. Shortcuts here can cause problems when you later sell.

The premium is the price paid to the landlord for the extension. It is worked out by valuers using a set legal framework, and it is usually the largest cost involved.

What affects the premium

  • How many years are left on the lease
  • The value of the flat with the current lease and with a new long lease
  • The ground rent the landlord will lose
  • The rates used to value future income and the landlord's future interest
  • Under current rules, whether the lease is below 80 years
  • Any improvements made by the leaseholder, which are usually disregarded

The 80 year point

For many years, once a lease dropped below 80 years, the premium has included an extra element called marriage value. This is a share of the increase in value created by the extension, and it can make the premium noticeably higher. The 2024 Act provides for marriage value to be removed and for other changes to the valuation method. Whether and when these changes apply has been the subject of further regulations and legal challenge, so a valuer will tell you which rules apply on the date your claim is made.

Other costs

Alongside the premium, the leaseholder usually pays their own solicitor and valuer. Under the rules that have long applied, the leaseholder also pays the landlord's reasonable legal and valuation costs of a statutory claim, though the 2024 Act changes this in many cases once in force. There may be Land Registry fees, a fee from the lender and, in some cases, Stamp Duty Land Tax on the premium. Figures change, so ask the firm for a written estimate.

Most lease extensions follow a similar practical pattern, whichever route is used. Being organised from the start helps keep costs down and avoids missed deadlines.

What to gather

  • A copy of your lease and any deeds of variation
  • Your title number, or the property address so your solicitor can get the title
  • Details of the landlord and the managing agent
  • Recent ground rent and service charge demands
  • Details of any improvements you have made to the flat
  • Your mortgage lender's details
  • Any correspondence you have had with the landlord about extending

Finding the right team

Lease extensions are specialist work. Solicitors who do them regularly know the procedure, the time limits and the common points landlords raise. Valuers who specialise in leasehold valuation understand the methods tribunals use. The solicitor and valuer work closely together, so many firms have valuers they recommend.

Dealing with the landlord

Some landlords are large investment companies with established processes. Others are individuals who may be slow to respond. Occasionally a landlord cannot be found at all. Each situation needs a different approach. If the landlord is missing, a court process exists that can allow the extension to go ahead. Your solicitor will explain what can be done.

Keeping track of dates

The statutory route is full of deadlines, both for the landlord and for you. Missing one can mean a claim is treated as withdrawn, with costs to pay and, under the current rules, a wait before a new claim can be made. A solicitor will track the dates, but it helps to reply quickly when they ask for decisions or documents.

Many lease extensions are tied to a sale or remortgage, either because a buyer's lender wants a longer lease or because the owner wants to get ahead of the problem. Timing can make a real difference to cost and stress.

Extending before selling

Extending before putting the flat on the market means buyers see a long lease from the start, which can widen the pool of buyers and lenders. It also avoids the extension holding up the sale. The downside is that the full process has to be completed and paid for before the sale.

Assigning the notice on a sale

Under the statutory route, a seller can often serve the notice and then pass the benefit of it to the buyer as part of the sale. The buyer then completes the extension after they buy. This is common and can keep a sale moving, but it needs careful handling by both sides' solicitors, and the buyer's lender has to be comfortable with it.

Remortgaging

Some lenders will not offer a new mortgage on a short lease. Others will lend if the extension is already underway. If you are planning to remortgage, telling your mortgage adviser and solicitor about the lease length early can avoid surprises.

How long it takes

An informal extension with a cooperative landlord can be completed in weeks. A statutory claim usually takes several months, and longer if it goes to the tribunal or the landlord is slow or missing. Starting well before any deadline gives the most options.

Questions and answers

Do I still need to have owned my flat for two years?
No. The two year ownership requirement for the statutory lease extension route was removed from 31 January 2025 by the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024. Before then, new owners usually had to wait or rely on the seller serving a notice. Other conditions still apply to the statutory route, and the law continues to change, so a solicitor will check what applies to your lease when you start.
How do I find out how long is left on my lease?
The title register at HM Land Registry shows the date of the lease, the date the term started and its original length. From these you can work out the remaining years. Your lease itself shows the same details. Your mortgage statement or the documents from when you bought may also mention it. A conveyancer can confirm the exact remaining term if the wording is unclear.
When is the best time to extend?
There is no single right moment, and it depends on the lease, your plans and the law in force. Under rules that have applied for many years, extending before the lease drops below 80 years has usually avoided marriage value, though reforms aim to change this. Many owners extend before selling or remortgaging. A solicitor and valuer can look at your situation and explain the considerations.
Do I need a solicitor and a valuer?
In practice, yes for most people. The valuer works out a fair premium and negotiates it with the landlord's valuer. The solicitor deals with the notices, deadlines, new lease wording and registration. Statutory claims in particular have strict rules, and mistakes can be expensive. Some leaseholders handle informal deals with less help, but a solicitor still needs to check the new lease before it is signed.
Can I extend the lease on a leasehold house?
Owners of leasehold houses often have rights to extend their lease or buy the freehold, under different legislation from flats. The conditions, process and pricing are not the same, and recent reforms have changed parts of the system. Buying the freehold is often the route house owners consider. A solicitor who handles leasehold enfranchisement can explain the options for a particular house.
Is it better to buy the freehold with my neighbours?
Buying the freehold collectively is an alternative to extending individual leases. It gives the owners control of the building and the ability to grant themselves long leases later. It also brings responsibilities for running the building and needs enough neighbours to take part. Whether it suits a building depends on many factors. A solicitor can explain the differences between the two approaches.
What documents will my solicitor ask for first?
Most solicitors start with a copy of the lease, the title details, the name and address of the landlord and managing agent, and recent ground rent and service charge demands. They may also ask about any improvements you have made, your mortgage lender and any previous contact with the landlord about extending. Having these ready helps the valuer and solicitor give you an early view of likely costs and timing.
What is a section 42 notice?
It is the formal notice a leaseholder serves on the landlord to start a statutory lease extension claim. It takes its name from the section of the 1993 Act that sets out what it must contain. It states the leaseholder's proposed premium and the terms sought and gives the landlord a deadline to respond. It needs to be accurate and properly served, which is why it is usually prepared by a solicitor.
What is a counter notice?
A counter notice is the landlord's formal response to a statutory claim. In it, the landlord either accepts the right to extend and puts forward their own premium and terms, or says why they dispute the claim. It must be served by the date stated in the leaseholder's notice. If no valid counter notice is served in time, the law sets out what happens next, which can work in the leaseholder's favour.
Can the landlord refuse a statutory lease extension?
If the leaseholder has a valid right and follows the procedure correctly, the landlord cannot simply refuse. They can dispute the right to extend, argue about the premium and terms and, in limited circumstances, oppose the claim on specific grounds such as redevelopment. Disputes about terms can be decided by the tribunal. A solicitor will check that the claim is valid before the notice is served.
Do I have to pay a deposit?
Under the statutory route, the landlord is usually entitled to ask for a deposit once the notice has been served. It is generally a proportion of the premium proposed in the notice or a set minimum, whichever is greater. The deposit is held and counted towards the premium on completion. Your solicitor will tell you if one is requested and how it will be held.
What happens at the tribunal?
If the premium or terms cannot be agreed, the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) can decide them. The tribunal sets a timetable, each side submits evidence and valuers may give expert evidence at a hearing. Many cases settle before reaching a hearing. The tribunal has its own fee rules and cost rules. A solicitor can explain what to expect if a claim heads in that direction.
Does my mortgage lender need to be involved?
Yes. The new lease replaces the old one that the mortgage is secured on, so the lender's consent is normally needed and the mortgage has to carry over to the new lease. Lenders usually cooperate because a longer lease improves their security, but some charge a fee or have their own requirements. Your solicitor will contact the lender at the appropriate stage.
How is the premium calculated?
Valuers use a legal framework that looks at the value of the landlord's interest now and after the extension. Key factors include the remaining term, the ground rent, the value of the flat and, under current rules for leases under 80 years, marriage value. The 2024 Act provides for changes to this method. A specialist valuer will explain which rules apply and give an estimated range.
Do I have to pay the landlord's costs?
Under the rules that have long applied to statutory claims, the leaseholder pays the landlord's reasonable legal and valuation costs, though not their tribunal costs in most cases. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 provides for this to change so that each side usually pays their own costs in many cases. Whether that change applies depends on when it takes effect. Your solicitor will explain the current position.
Is there Stamp Duty on a lease extension?
Stamp Duty Land Tax can apply to the premium paid for a lease extension, depending on the amount and the rules in force. In many cases no tax is due, but it should be checked. Rates and thresholds change from time to time, so your solicitor will look at the current rules and file a return if one is needed. GOV.UK has official information.
Why do informal offers sometimes look cheap?
An informal offer might quote a lower premium than a statutory valuation, but the overall deal can be worse. It may keep a ground rent, include one that rises, offer a shorter extension or add new lease terms. These features can reduce the value of the flat or put lenders off. A valuer and solicitor can compare the informal offer with the likely statutory terms.

Can I pay for the extension with my mortgage?
Some leaseholders fund the premium and costs from savings, while others borrow, for example by remortgaging or taking further borrowing from their existing lender. Whether a lender will help depends on the lender, the current lease length and your circumstances. Some lenders will lend against the value of the flat with the extended lease. A mortgage adviser can explain the borrowing options, and your solicitor will deal with the lender's requirements.
What if my landlord cannot be found?
Sometimes the landlord is a company that has been dissolved or an individual who cannot be traced. There is a court procedure for missing landlords that can allow the extension to go ahead, with the premium paid into court. It takes longer and involves extra steps, including showing that reasonable efforts were made to find the landlord. A solicitor will explain the process.
What if a deadline is missed?
Deadlines matter a great deal in the statutory route. If the leaseholder misses the deadline to apply to the tribunal, the claim may be treated as withdrawn, the leaseholder may have to pay certain landlord costs and, under current rules, may have to wait before serving a new notice. If the landlord misses a deadline, different rules apply. Your solicitor will track deadlines and explain any consequences.
Can I withdraw a statutory claim?
Yes, a leaseholder can withdraw a claim. Under the rules that have applied for many years, withdrawing usually means paying the landlord's reasonable costs up to that point and waiting a set period before starting a new claim. Reforms may change parts of this. If you are thinking about withdrawing, your solicitor can explain the consequences in your case.
What if there is more than one landlord?
Some buildings have layers of leases, with an intermediate landlord between the leaseholder and the freeholder. In a statutory claim, one landlord usually acts as the competent landlord and deals with the claim, but other landlords may be entitled to part of the premium. This makes the process more complex. A solicitor will check the title structure at the start.
What if my landlord is a large company that is slow to respond?
Large landlords sometimes take time to reply to informal approaches. The statutory route helps here, because the notice sets a deadline for the landlord's counter notice and the procedure keeps the claim moving. Slow responses during negotiation can be managed by the tribunal timetable if needed. A solicitor familiar with lease extensions will usually know how particular landlords tend to deal with claims and can explain what to expect.
Can I sell my flat while the extension is going on?
Yes. A common approach is for the seller to serve the statutory notice and then assign the benefit of it to the buyer on completion of the sale. The buyer then completes the extension. Both sides' solicitors need to handle this carefully, and the buyer's lender must be willing to lend on that basis. Your solicitor will explain how it could work in your sale.
Will a short lease stop me getting a mortgage?
Many lenders have minimum lease lengths, often looking at how many years will remain at the end of the mortgage. If a lease falls short, the lender may decline or ask for the lease to be extended first. Requirements vary widely and change over time. A mortgage adviser can explain what different lenders accept, and a solicitor can explain the extension options.
Does extending the lease increase the value of my flat?
A longer lease usually makes a flat more attractive to buyers and lenders, and the valuation of a flat with a long lease is normally higher than with a short one. How much difference it makes depends on the property, the market and how short the lease was. A valuer can explain the likely effect for your flat. Leaflegal cannot advise on values.
How long does a lease extension take?
An informal extension with a responsive landlord can be completed in a matter of weeks. A statutory extension usually takes several months, because of the formal notice periods and negotiation. If the case goes to the tribunal, or the landlord is missing, it can take considerably longer. Starting well before you need the longer lease gives you more options and less pressure.
What happens to my service charge after extending?
A lease extension changes the length of the lease and usually the ground rent, but it does not normally change the service charge arrangements. You still pay your share of the building's running costs as the lease sets out. The new lease generally carries over the existing terms apart from the agreed changes. Your solicitor will explain exactly what the new lease says before you sign.

More questions people ask

Do I need to have owned my flat for two years?
Not any more. The two year ownership requirement used to apply, but the 2024 Act removed it from 31 January 2025. This means many people can start the process soon after buying. Your solicitor will confirm that the requirement does not apply to you before serving a notice.
Does it matter how long my lease has left?
You can usually extend however many years remain, as long as the lease was originally granted for more than 21 years. However, the number of years left affects the price under the long standing valuation rules. A lease with fewer than 80 years left can cost noticeably more to extend because marriage value applies.
Can I extend if I sublet my flat?
Letting your flat to a tenant does not usually stop you using the statutory right. You remain the leaseholder, and you can still serve a notice. Your solicitor may need to know about the tenancy, as the surveyor might need access to value the flat.
Can I extend if the flat is owned through a company?
In many cases, yes. The rules about company owners have changed over time and depend on the circumstances. Your solicitor will check whether a company owner qualifies before any notice is served.
My flat is above a shop. Can I still extend?
Usually, yes. The right to extend is available to qualifying flat owners whether or not the building contains commercial units. The stricter rules about commercial space mainly affect buying the freehold of the whole building rather than extending an individual lease.
Can I extend if I own the share of freehold?
If you already own a share of the freehold, you may be able to grant yourself a longer lease by agreement with the other freeholders, which is often simpler than the statutory route. A solicitor will look at how your freehold is held and explain the options.
What if there are two landlords?
Some flats have a head lease between the freeholder and the flat owner, so there are two or more landlords. The statutory process deals with this by involving a competent landlord who runs the claim, with the premium split between the different landlords. Your solicitor will identify everyone involved.
What is in a section 42 notice?
The notice includes details of the leaseholder and landlord, a description of the flat, details of the lease, the premium the leaseholder proposes and any other amounts, the terms proposed for the new lease, and the date by which the landlord must reply. The details must be accurate, and your solicitor will check them against the lease and the title before serving it.
How long does the freeholder have to respond?
The notice states a date by which the freeholder must serve a counter notice. That date must be at least two months after the notice is given. In the meantime, the freeholder can ask for proof of title, ask for a deposit and arrange to inspect the flat for valuation.
What if the freeholder does not reply?
If no counter notice is served in time, the leaseholder can usually apply to the county court for an order that the new lease is granted on the terms in their notice. There is a deadline for this application too. Your solicitor will act quickly if the freeholder misses its date.
What happens if we cannot agree the price?
Either side can apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) once two months have passed after the counter notice. The application must be made within a fixed window, or the claim is treated as withdrawn. Most cases still settle before a hearing, but the application protects the claim while negotiations continue.
Can I withdraw my notice?
Yes, a leaseholder can withdraw. However, you would usually have to pay certain of the freeholder's costs incurred up to that point under the rules that apply, and you normally cannot serve another notice for 12 months. Your solicitor will explain the consequences before you decide.
Is the premium fixed when I serve my notice?
The valuation is generally carried out as at the date the notice is served. This is one reason the timing of the notice matters, as later changes in the market or in the number of years left do not usually affect the figure. Your surveyor will explain how this applies to you.
Does the freeholder get to inspect my flat?
After receiving the notice, the freeholder is usually entitled to reasonable access so its surveyor can value the flat. Your solicitor will help arrange a suitable time. Refusing reasonable access can cause problems with the claim, so it is best to cooperate with a properly arranged visit.
