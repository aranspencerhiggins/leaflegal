Family visas explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Family visas is a general term for immigration applications where someone wants to come to the UK, or stay in the UK, because of a family member here. It can involve children, parents, grandparents and other relatives. Every family is different, and the immigration system treats different family relationships in different ways.

This page is about family members other than partners. If your question is about a husband, wife, civil partner, fiancé or long term partner, our page on spouse and partner visas is a better place to start.

Family matters are often more complicated than they first look. They can involve several people, several countries and many years of family history. There may be children at different stages of life, relatives with health needs, and family members with different immigration statuses living under one roof.

Why families ask for help

When family is involved, the stakes feel very high. Families want to be together, and a refused application can mean disappointment, delay and expense. The rules are detailed and change often, and what worked for another family may not work for yours. A regulated adviser looks at your family's own circumstances and explains what may be possible, and what is not.

Who we hear from

  • Parents in the UK whose children live overseas
  • Families worried about an elderly or unwell relative abroad
  • Parents in the UK who want to stay close to their children
  • Households where family members have different immigration statuses
  • Families with children whose own status is unclear
  • Families whose current visas are coming to an end

What a regulated adviser does

A regulated adviser listens to your family's story, explains what the options might be for each person and what each would involve, and helps prepare and submit applications if you decide to go ahead. They check documents, keep track of linked applications, answer your questions and deal with the Home Office if there are follow up questions. They will also tell you honestly if nothing currently seems to fit.

Leaflegal's role

Leaflegal does not give immigration advice and does not tell any family which visa they need or whether they qualify. We introduce you to one firm or adviser regulated to give immigration advice. Everything about your family's options comes from them.

Family matters usually involve more conversations, more documents and more people than a single application. Here is a general picture of how working with a regulated adviser tends to go.

Looking at the whole family

A good adviser will want to understand the whole family, not just the person who is applying. Who lives where, who looks after whom and what status each person has can all be relevant. Expect questions about family members you might not have expected to discuss.

One point of contact

With several people involved, it helps to agree who in the family will be the main contact for the adviser. That person can pass on questions, gather documents and keep everyone updated. Advisers can usually speak to other family members too when needed.

Honest advice

Sometimes families hope for one answer and an adviser gives another. A good adviser will explain honestly what may be possible and what is unlikely, even when that is hard to hear. That honesty can save a family time, money and disappointment.

Across borders

Family members may be spread across different countries and time zones. Most regulated advisers are used to this and work by phone, video and email. Ask how the firm prefers to communicate with family members abroad.

Keeping in touch

Families change. A new baby, a move, a separation or a change in a relative's health can all matter while an application is being prepared or decided. Tell your adviser about any change as soon as it happens.

Documents from different places

Family documents are often spread across countries and relatives. Some may need to be ordered from official offices abroad or translated. Your adviser will tell you which documents they need, so start gathering them early and keep copies of everything you send.

The first consultation is your chance to explain your family's situation and to find out how the adviser could help. You do not need to have everything worked out beforehand.

Who should attend

It often helps for the family member who knows the situation best to attend, along with anyone who will be closely involved. If a relative is abroad, they may be able to join by video. Let the firm know in advance who will be there.

What you might be asked

  • Who is in the family and where each person lives
  • What immigration status each person has now
  • Who the application would be for
  • Who looks after children or relatives at the moment
  • Any previous applications or refusals for anyone in the family
  • Any court orders or official decisions about the family
  • Any dates or deadlines you are working towards

What to have to hand

You do not need every document at this stage. Having passports, any current visa details, any letters from the Home Office and key family documents nearby will make the conversation more useful. A simple family tree, with names, dates of birth and where people live, can help a lot.

Questions you might ask

  • How often do you handle family matters like ours?
  • Who will work on our matter day to day?
  • Do you charge per person or for the family as a whole?
  • Are government fees included or extra?
  • How will you keep us updated?
  • Can you speak to relatives who are overseas?
  • What happens if the Home Office has questions?

After the consultation

Take time to talk things through as a family before deciding how to go ahead. You are under no obligation to instruct the firm, and it is normal to come back with more questions first.

Matters involving children often need extra care and sensitivity. Families understandably feel strongly about them, and they can raise questions that go beyond immigration.

Why families ask for help

Parents often contact an adviser when they want a child to join them, when a child's own visa is ending or when they are unsure what a child's status is. Grandparents and other relatives who care for children sometimes have questions too. Whatever the situation, a regulated adviser can look at the child's circumstances and explain what may be possible.

Telling your adviser everything

Children's lives can be complicated. They may have lived with grandparents or other relatives, moved between countries or have parents who live apart. Tell your adviser about the child's whole history, including any arrangements that worry you. They need the full picture to help properly.

Family law questions

Some children's matters also involve family law, such as questions about parental responsibility or arrangements with another parent. Laws on these things differ between countries. Your immigration adviser may suggest also speaking to a family lawyer, in the UK or abroad.

Children growing up

Children's circumstances change as they get older, and birthdays can matter. If a child in your family is approaching a significant birthday, such as turning 18, mention it to your adviser early so they can explain whether it affects anything.

Keeping children's documents safe

Birth certificates, passports, school records and any court documents about a child can all be important. Keep originals somewhere safe, keep copies, and make sure you know where they are before you need them.

Citizenship questions

Some families have questions about whether their children are British, or could become British. These are often separate from family visa questions. Our page on British citizenship has general information, and a regulated adviser can look at your child's position.

Family applications can be expensive, especially when several people are involved. It helps to understand the likely costs before anyone applies.

Government fees

Each person usually has their own application fee, and many also pay the Immigration Health Surcharge, which covers NHS care for the length of their visa. There can be other costs too, such as translations, medical reports and travel to appointments. All of these change from time to time, so check GOV.UK or ask your adviser for the current figures.

Adviser fees

Advisers often charge a fixed fee per application, and some offer a reduced fee for additional family members. More complex matters may be charged differently. Ask for a written quote that sets out what is included for each person.

Help with costs

In some situations it may be possible to ask for a fee to be waived, and legal aid is available for some immigration matters, particularly some involving children. Both are limited. A regulated adviser can tell you whether either is worth exploring for your family.

Planning as a family

Many families find it helps to work out the total cost for everyone involved, including future applications, before deciding how to go ahead. Your adviser can help you understand the likely picture.

Getting a clear quote

Ask each firm to explain what its fee covers for each family member, such as document checks, preparing the application and dealing with Home Office questions afterwards. A good firm will tell you promptly if anything is likely to cost more than first expected.

Leaflegal costs your family nothing to use. The firm we introduce you to pays us for the introduction, which makes no difference to the fees they charge you.

With several family members, there may be several visa end dates, letters and deadlines to keep track of. Missing one can cause problems for the whole family.

If you receive a Home Office letter

Read it carefully and note any deadline, as some time limits are short. Get in touch with a regulated adviser as soon as you can and have the letter ready to share. If you do not have an adviser yet, mention the deadline when you enquire so the firm knows it is urgent.

If an application is refused

A refusal can feel devastating, especially when family is involved. There may be ways of challenging a decision, and time limits can be short. Contact a regulated adviser straight away with the refusal letter and a copy of the application.

Keeping track

A simple list of every family member, their status and the date it ends can prevent nasty surprises. Put key dates in a shared calendar, and contact your adviser well before any of them arrive.

Common practical problems

Using official sources

The official place to find current fees, processing times and application forms is GOV.UK. Be careful with websites that look official but are not, and with anyone who is not regulated but offers to make applications for a fee. If you are unsure, ask a regulated adviser.

  • Names spelled differently on documents from different countries
  • Important documents lost or held by relatives abroad
  • Family members leaving out past applications or problems
  • Relying on advice from friends whose situation was different
  • Seeking help only when a deadline is very close
  • Using someone who is not regulated to give immigration advice

Most of these can be avoided by starting early, keeping good records and being open with a regulated adviser.

Fees and costs

Immigration advisers usually charge a fixed fee for each application or stage of work, and some charge hourly for complex matters. Home Office application fees and any Immigration Health Surcharge are separate from the adviser’s fee and are paid to the Home Office.

Ask for the fee and what it covers in writing before you agree to anything. A regulated adviser must explain their costs clearly and tell you how to complain if something goes wrong.

  • What is your fee, and what does it include?
  • Are Home Office fees separate, and roughly how much are they?
  • Who will handle my case day to day?

When to get help

Things to know

Check who is advising you

Anyone giving you immigration advice must be regulated. You can check a solicitor on the SRA register or an adviser on the Immigration Advice Authority register.

Fees

Immigration firms charge their own fees, and Home Office application fees are separate. The firm should explain both before you go ahead.

Free help

If you can’t afford to pay, GOV.UK lists organisations that may be able to help for free, including for asylum.

We can introduce you to a regulated adviser who handles family matters involving older relatives. They will listen to your family's situation and explain honestly what may be possible. Families in this position are often under a lot of strain, so it can help to agree within the family who will lead on contact with the adviser.

Many households include people with different immigration statuses. Each person's position can affect the others, so it often helps for one adviser to look at the whole family. Tell the adviser about everyone in the household at the start. A simple list of each person, their status and when it ends is a very useful starting point for the first conversation.
Can the adviser speak to our relative abroad directly?
Usually, yes. Many advisers are happy to speak to relatives overseas by phone or video, sometimes with an interpreter. The adviser may need the relative's agreement before discussing their personal details. Ask the firm how it usually handles this.
How much does a family visa cost?
Each person usually has an application fee, and many also pay the Immigration Health Surcharge, plus costs such as translations. These amounts change from time to time and can add up quickly for a family. Check GOV.UK for current figures or ask your adviser for a breakdown.
How do advisers charge for families?
Many charge a fixed fee per application, sometimes with a reduced fee for additional family members. Complex matters may be charged differently. Ask for a written quote that shows the fee for each person and whether government fees are included. Comparing quotes is easier if you ask each firm to break down its fee in the same way.
Is there help with the costs?
Fee waivers and legal aid are available in some limited situations, particularly some involving children. Whether either could apply depends on the matter and the family's circumstances. A regulated adviser can tell you whether they are worth exploring. Have a general idea of your household income and outgoings ready if you want to ask about this.

No. There is no charge to your family for using Leaflegal. The regulated firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and it does not change what they charge. You agree fees directly with the firm, and you are free not to instruct them at all.
Can we pay in stages?
Some firms allow fees to be paid in stages, for example part at the start and part when each application is submitted. Others ask for payment upfront. Government fees are usually paid when each application is made. Ask the firm what it offers so you can plan.
A family member's visa ends soon. What should we do?
Leaflegal cannot advise on your situation, but we can introduce you to a regulated adviser quickly. Tell us the end date when you enquire. The sooner you speak to an adviser, the more time there is to understand the options and prepare. If several family members have different end dates, list them all for the adviser.
We have received a letter from the Home Office.
Read it carefully and note any deadline, as some time limits are short. Get in touch with a regulated adviser as soon as you can and have the letter ready to share. If you do not have an adviser yet, mention the deadline when you enquire.
An application was refused. Can you help?
We can introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at the refusal. Some challenges have short time limits, so tell us the date on the decision when you enquire. Have the refusal letter and a copy of the application ready. If more than one family member was refused, have each decision letter ready, as each may need to be looked at.
How do we check an adviser is regulated?
You can check solicitors on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register and advisers on the Immigration Advice Authority register, formerly the OISC. Both are free to search online. Every firm we introduce is regulated, and a genuine adviser will give you their details if you ask.
How quickly will someone contact us?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, often sooner. If anything is urgent, such as a visa ending or a deadline in a letter, say so in your enquiry so the firm knows to prioritise it. Make sure the phone number and email you give us are checked regularly, including spam folders.
Do we have to go ahead after the consultation?
No. Your family is under no obligation to instruct the firm, and nothing is agreed until you accept their terms. Take whatever time you need to talk it over. If you decide not to go ahead, there is no need to give a reason, and we will not send your enquiry anywhere else without asking.
I have just been refused. What should I do first?
Many people start by finding the date of the decision and any deadline in the letter, keeping every page and contacting a regulated adviser quickly. An adviser can read the decision and explain what it means. Leaflegal can introduce you to one, and we pass the deadline to the firm so they can prioritise your matter.
Can Leaflegal tell me if I can appeal?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We cannot tell you whether you can appeal or what your options are. We introduce you to a regulated adviser who can read your decision and explain what may be possible.
Is there a deadline?
Many refusals come with time limits, and some are short. The letter usually mentions any time limit and when it starts. Because the details matter, a regulated adviser can confirm exactly how long you have. When you enquire, tell us the date on your letter.
Does a refusal mean I did something wrong?
Not necessarily. Refusals happen for many reasons, and sometimes the Home Office itself makes mistakes. The reasons in the letter are the starting point. A regulated adviser can explain them in plain language and talk through what they mean for you.
Can I stay in the UK after a refusal?
That depends on your circumstances and the decision, and it is a question for a regulated adviser. It is one of the first things many people ask about, and an adviser can usually explain your position quickly once they have seen the letter and your immigration history.
Should I just apply again?
Some people do make a new application after a refusal, and for others a different step is more suitable. Which approach fits your situation depends on the reasons for refusal and your circumstances. A regulated adviser can explain the options and the risks of each.
I do not understand the letter. Is that normal?
Very normal. Refusal letters are written in formal language and refer to rules and guidance that most people have never read. Advisers read these letters every day. They can explain what each part means and what the Home Office is saying.
Will a refusal affect future applications?
It may need to be mentioned on future forms, and the reasons for refusal can be relevant later. How much it matters depends on the decision and your circumstances. A regulated adviser can explain how your refusal might affect any future plans, including applications for family members.

More questions people ask

What is the difference between naturalisation and registration?
They are two different ways of applying to become a British citizen. Naturalisation is the term usually used for adults. Registration is used for children and some other people in particular situations. Which, if either, is relevant to you is a question for a regulated adviser who can look at your circumstances.
Is citizenship the same as indefinite leave to remain?
No. Indefinite leave to remain is an immigration status that lets a person stay in the UK without a time limit. Citizenship is a nationality. They are separate, with separate applications. A regulated adviser can explain what each means and how they relate to each other in your situation.
Could I already be British without knowing it?
Sometimes people are British without realising, usually because of a parent's or grandparent's history. Nationality law is complicated and has changed many times. A regulated adviser who works with nationality law can look at your family history and explain whether this might be worth exploring.
Will I have to give up my other nationality?
The UK side is something your adviser can explain. Whether you can keep your existing nationality also depends on the law of the other country, which varies. Many people check the position with the other country's embassy or authorities before they apply.
Can Leaflegal tell me whether I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration or nationality advice and does not do so. We introduce you to a regulated adviser, who can look at your situation and explain your options.
What rights does citizenship give?
British citizens can live in the UK without immigration permission, hold a British passport and take part fully in UK democratic life. The full picture depends on your circumstances, including any other nationality you hold. A regulated adviser can explain what becoming British would mean for you.
Is applying for citizenship compulsory?
No. Applying is a personal choice, and many people with long term status in the UK decide not to apply, or to wait. Some think about cost, some about their other nationality and some about family plans. A regulated adviser can explain the practical differences so that you can make an informed decision.
