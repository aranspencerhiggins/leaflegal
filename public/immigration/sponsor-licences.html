Sponsor licence in brief

Employers need a sponsor licence before they can sponsor skilled workers from overseas. A sponsor licence application and the duties that follow are detailed, and errors can lead to suspension. Leaflegal introduces employers to one regulated immigration firm that handles sponsor licences.

We do not give immigration advice. The regulated firm advises on your application and compliance duties.

Sponsor licences explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A sponsor licence is permission from the Home Office that lets a UK organisation sponsor certain workers from overseas. The licence belongs to the business, not to any individual worker, and it stays with the business for as long as it holds it. Holding a licence brings ongoing responsibilities as well as the ability to recruit.

Most employers start looking into sponsorship when they have a particular person in mind. It might be a candidate who has applied from abroad, a graduate already working for them, or a member of staff in an overseas office who is needed in the UK. Others look into it because they have struggled to recruit for some roles and want to understand whether sponsorship is something they could explore.

Sponsorship touches several parts of a business at once. It involves the owners or directors, whoever runs HR and payroll, the managers who will work alongside sponsored staff and, of course, the workers themselves. That is one reason many employers want a regulated adviser involved from the very beginning.

Why employers ask for help

The sponsorship system is detailed and the guidance for sponsors is long. It also changes often, sometimes several times a year. Employers who have sponsored before can find that things have moved on since they last looked. A regulated adviser keeps up with these changes and looks at how they affect your business in particular.

  • Care providers and healthcare businesses
  • Technology and engineering firms
  • Restaurants, hotels and hospitality groups
  • Schools, universities and research organisations
  • Construction and manufacturing businesses
  • Overseas companies opening a UK office
  • Start-ups and growing small businesses

What a regulated adviser does

A regulated adviser looks at your business and your plans and explains what sponsorship would mean for you. They can prepare and submit applications, help you set up the systems you will need, train the people who will manage the licence and deal with the Home Office on your behalf. They can also tell you honestly if sponsorship does not look like a good fit for your business right now.

Some employers use an adviser for a single application. Others build a long term relationship, with the adviser acting almost as an extension of their HR team for immigration matters. There is no right answer, and the firm can explain the options it offers.

Every adviser works a little differently, but most sponsor licence instructions follow a similar shape. Knowing what to expect can help you get the most out of the relationship and plan your own time.

Getting to know your business

A good adviser starts by understanding how your business works. They will want to know what you do, how you are structured, how many people you employ, how you recruit and what roles you are thinking about. They are building a picture so that their advice fits your business rather than a generic one.

Looking at your HR set-up

Many advisers look at how your business handles staff records, right to work checks and changes in employment. Some offer a formal review, others look at it more informally. Either way, it is a chance to find any gaps before the Home Office does.

Preparing the application

If you decide to go ahead, the adviser will explain what they need from you and when. They will usually prepare the application, check your documents and explain each step before anything is submitted. They may ask the same question more than once to make sure the details are consistent.

After a decision

Many employers keep their adviser on after a licence is granted. Ongoing help can include training the people who will use the licence, helping with individual workers, dealing with changes in the business and preparing for any Home Office visit. Some firms offer this through a monthly arrangement and others charge per task.

Who does what

It helps to agree early on who in your business will be the main point of contact for the adviser. Sponsorship involves deadlines, and things move more smoothly when one person is responsible for keeping track of them on your side.

Communication

Ask at the start how the firm will keep you updated and how quickly it usually replies to messages. Many firms use email, client portals or regular calls. Knowing what to expect helps avoid frustration, particularly when you have a candidate waiting to hear about a start date.

The first conversation with an adviser is usually about understanding your situation and explaining how they could help. It is not a test, and you do not need to have all the answers. It is also your chance to decide whether this is the right firm for you.

What they are likely to ask

  • What your business does and how long it has been trading
  • How the business is owned and run
  • The roles you want to fill and why
  • Whether you already have someone in mind
  • Whether you have held a licence before
  • Any deadlines you are working towards
  • Any letters you have received from the Home Office

What to have ready

There is no need to gather a full set of documents before the first call. Having your company details, a rough description of the roles and any key dates to hand will make the conversation more useful. If you have had any contact with the Home Office about immigration before, have the letters nearby.

Questions you might ask

  • How often do you handle sponsor licence work for businesses like ours?
  • Who will actually work on our matter?
  • Is your fee fixed, and what does it include?
  • Are Home Office fees included or separate?
  • What support do you offer once a licence is in place?
  • How do you keep clients updated?
  • How quickly do you usually respond if something urgent comes up?

After the call

Most firms will follow up in writing with a summary of what they can do and what it would cost. Take time to read it. You are under no obligation to go ahead, and it is perfectly normal to ask further questions before you decide.

Employers often find that sponsorship takes longer than they first expected. It helps to think of it in stages rather than as a single application.

Getting ready

The time it takes to prepare depends a great deal on the business. An established business with tidy records may be ready quite quickly. A newer business, or one whose HR records need attention, may need longer. Your adviser will give you a realistic view once they understand your position.

Waiting for a decision

The Home Office publishes its current processing times on GOV.UK, and they change from time to time. Faster services are sometimes offered for an extra fee, but availability can be limited. Your adviser can tell you what is realistic at the point you apply.

Bringing people on board

A licence decision is not the end of the timeline. Each worker then has their own process, which has its own timescale. If you have a candidate waiting or a start date in mind, tell your adviser at the very first conversation so that they can explain how the stages fit together.

Planning ahead

Businesses that plan recruitment months ahead tend to find sponsorship less stressful than those who start when a role is already urgent. If you think you may want to sponsor in future, an early conversation with a regulated adviser can help you understand the likely timescale for your business.

Busy periods

Processing times can lengthen when the Home Office is busy, for example after a change in the rules when many businesses apply at once. Advisers can also be busier at these times. Getting in touch early gives everyone more room to work without pressure.

Sponsorship involves two main kinds of cost. One is what the government charges. The other is what your adviser charges.

Government fees and charges

The Home Office charges fees connected with the licence and with each worker who is sponsored. Some charges are paid by the employer and some by the worker. The amounts change from time to time, sometimes significantly, so always check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask your adviser for an up to date breakdown before you budget.

How advisers usually charge

Many firms offer a fixed fee for a licence application. Some offer packages that include a review of your HR systems, training for staff or ongoing support. More complex or urgent work, such as responding to a Home Office letter, may be charged by the hour or quoted separately.

Getting a clear quote

Ask the firm to set out in writing what is included, what would cost extra and whether government fees are part of the price. A good firm will be happy to explain its fees before you commit, and will tell you if something changes.

Thinking about the long term

Many businesses look at the cost of sponsorship over several years rather than just the first application. Ongoing support, help with individual workers and changes in the business can all carry costs. Your adviser can help you understand the likely picture for your plans.

Most sponsor licence holders deal with the Home Office from time to time. Some contact is routine. Some is serious and needs a quick response.

If you receive a Home Office letter

Read it carefully and note any deadline straight away. Letters about a licence can have short time limits. Contact a regulated adviser as soon as possible and send them a copy. If you do not have an adviser yet, mention the deadline when you make an enquiry so the firm knows how urgent it is.

If the Home Office wants to visit

The Home Office can visit sponsors, sometimes with little or no notice. Many employers ask their adviser to help them prepare, or to carry out a practice review beforehand so that they know what a visit is likely to involve.

If your licence is suspended or at risk

This can affect every worker you sponsor, so it needs urgent attention. Speak to a regulated adviser straight away. Do not wait to see what happens next.

Common practical problems

Keeping good records of your own

Keep copies of every letter and email you receive from the Home Office, along with a note of when it arrived. If a problem does come up, having everything in one place makes it much quicker for your adviser to understand what has happened and respond.

  • Only one person in the business understands the licence, and they leave
  • Changes in the business are not passed on to whoever manages the licence
  • Records are kept in different places and are hard to find
  • Advice from another business, or from old guidance online, is relied on without checking
  • Help is sought only once a problem has become urgent

Most of these are about organisation rather than law, and a regulated adviser can help you put simple systems in place to avoid them.

Can I enquire on behalf of a relative?
Yes. Family members often make the first enquiry for a relative, particularly an older parent or someone abroad. The adviser will usually need to speak to the person concerned at some point, and may need their agreement before acting for them. Tell us your relationship to the person and the best way to reach you both.
Is my enquiry confidential?
Yes. We share your enquiry only with the one firm we introduce you to, and only with your agreement. We never share it with advertising platforms. The firm will treat what you tell it as confidential under the rules of its regulator.
Why do family rules change so often?
Immigration policy is reviewed regularly by the government, and the rules for family members have changed many times. Fees, guidance and requirements can all move. That is why it is risky to rely on what worked for another family in the past. A regulated adviser explains things based on the rules that apply now.
What happens at the first consultation?
The adviser will ask about your family, where everyone lives, each person's status and what you hope to achieve. They will explain how they could help and what it might cost. You do not need every document. It is also your chance to decide whether you want to work with them.
