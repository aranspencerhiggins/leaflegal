Skilled worker visa in brief

The skilled worker visa is the main UK work visa route, and applications depend on the sponsor, the job and supporting documents. Leaflegal introduces individuals and employers to one regulated immigration firm that handles work visa matters.

We do not advise on work visa requirements or eligibility. The regulated firm advises on your situation and the application.

Work visas explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A work visa is permission from the Home Office to come to the UK, or to stay in the UK, in order to work. There is no single work visa. There are several different kinds, each designed for a different purpose, and they change over time as government policy changes. Working out which, if any, could fit your situation is exactly the kind of question a regulated adviser can help with.

This page is for individuals. If you are an employer, our page on sponsor licences covers the business side of things.

People look for help with work visas at many different points in their lives. Some have just received a job offer from a UK employer and want to know what happens next. Some are already in the UK and are thinking about their next step. Others are thinking about a move years from now and want to understand the process before they plan.

Why people ask for help

Work visa applications are detailed, and a mistake can be costly in time, money and stress. The rules and guidance are long and change often. Information from friends, colleagues and social media can be out of date or simply not apply to your situation. A regulated adviser looks at your own circumstances and explains things based on the rules as they stand today.

Who we hear from

  • People who have just received a job offer from a UK employer
  • Graduates thinking about their next step after university
  • Workers whose current visa is coming to an end
  • People thinking about changing jobs
  • Specialists and senior staff being moved to the UK by their employer
  • Partners and families of workers who want to understand their own position

What a regulated adviser does

A regulated adviser listens to your situation, explains what your options might be and what each would involve, and helps you prepare and submit an application if you decide to go ahead. They check documents, answer your questions along the way and deal with the Home Office if there are follow up questions or problems. They can also be honest with you if no option currently seems to fit.

Leaflegal's role

Leaflegal does not give immigration advice and cannot tell you whether you qualify for any visa. We introduce you to one firm or adviser that is regulated to give immigration advice. Everything about your options and your application comes from them.

Many people have never used an immigration adviser before and are not sure what to expect. Here is a general picture of how working with one usually goes.

A first conversation

The adviser will want to understand your background, your current situation and your plans. They will explain how they can help and what it would cost. Some firms offer a short free first call, and others charge for a full consultation. Ask about this when they first get in touch.

A plan

If you decide to go ahead, the adviser will usually set out the next steps in writing. That might include a list of documents to gather, a timetable and an explanation of what they will do and what you will do. Keep this somewhere safe and refer back to it.

Preparing the application

Your adviser will usually prepare or check the application with you, review your documents and point out anything that needs attention before it is submitted. They may ask for the same information in different ways. That is normal and helps make sure everything is consistent.

After it is submitted

Once the application is in, your adviser can deal with any questions from the Home Office and explain the decision when it arrives. If something goes wrong, they can talk you through what can be done next.

Working remotely

Many people applying for work visas are overseas, or are busy working long hours. Most regulated advisers are used to working by phone, video and email, and across time zones. Ask how the firm prefers to communicate and when you can expect replies.

Your part

The process works best when you reply to your adviser's questions promptly, send documents when asked and tell them straight away if anything changes, such as a new job offer, a new address or a change in your family. Small changes can matter, so it is always better to mention something than to assume it is not important.

The first consultation is the most important conversation in the whole process. It is where the adviser learns about you and where you learn whether you want to work with them.

What you might be asked

  • Your nationality and where you live now
  • Your current immigration status, if you are in the UK
  • Details of any job offer or employer
  • Your work history and qualifications in general terms
  • Any previous visa applications, in the UK or elsewhere
  • Who else in your family might be involved
  • Any dates you are working towards

Be open

Advisers can only help properly if they know the full picture. If something in your history worries you, such as a past refusal or a problem with an earlier visa, mention it at the start. What you tell a regulated adviser is confidential, under the rules of their regulator.

What to have to hand

You do not need every document at the first meeting. Having your passport, any current visa details, any job offer letter and any letters from the Home Office nearby will make the conversation much more useful.

Questions you might ask

  • Have you handled many matters like mine?
  • Who will be working on my case day to day?
  • What is your fee, and what does it include?
  • Are government fees included or separate?
  • How long do you expect the whole process to take?
  • How will you keep me updated?
  • What happens if the Home Office asks for more information?

Afterwards

Most firms follow up after the first consultation with a note of what was discussed and what they could do. Take your time to read it. You are under no obligation to go ahead, and it is normal to come back with more questions before you decide.

Timing is one of the most stressful parts of a work visa. Start dates, notice periods, house moves and current visa end dates all need to fit together, and the Home Office decision is not entirely in anyone's control.

Preparation

Gathering documents often takes longer than people expect. Some may need to be ordered, translated or certified. Appointments may need to be booked. Starting early gives you room to deal with anything unexpected.

The decision

The Home Office publishes its current processing times on GOV.UK. They differ depending on where the application is made and can change with demand. Faster services are sometimes available for an extra fee. Your adviser can tell you what is realistic for your situation.

If you have a date to meet

If you have a start date, a visa end date or any other deadline, tell your adviser at the first conversation. They can explain how the process is likely to fit around it and where the risk of delay lies.

Avoid leaving it late

Many people only seek help a few days before an important date. That leaves very little time. Getting in touch with a regulated adviser as early as possible gives you more choices and less pressure.

Keeping your employer informed

If you have a job offer, keep your future employer up to date on progress. Employers are used to the process taking time, and a clear picture helps them plan your start date. Your adviser can tell you what is sensible to share and when.

The cost of a work visa is made up of government fees and, if you use one, an adviser's fee. Knowing what to expect helps you plan.

Government fees

Most applications have an application fee, and many people also pay the Immigration Health Surcharge, which covers NHS care for the length of the visa. There can also be smaller costs for things like translations and appointments. All of these change from time to time, so check the current amounts on GOV.UK or ask your adviser.

Adviser fees

Many advisers offer a fixed fee for an application, sometimes with separate fees for family members. More complicated situations may be charged differently. Ask for the fee in writing, and check what is included, such as document checks and dealing with Home Office questions.

Who pays

Some employers pay for their worker's visa and adviser. Some pay part, and some leave it to the worker. Ask your employer early what they will cover so that there are no surprises. If anyone asks you to pay for a job itself, ask a regulated adviser about it before you pay anything.

Planning your budget

Moving to a new country or changing jobs often comes with other costs too, such as travel, deposits on a home and time off work for appointments. It can help to write down all the likely costs in one place, including the visa itself, so that you have a realistic picture. Your adviser can help with the immigration side of that list.

Sadly, people looking for work in the UK are sometimes targeted by scams and by people offering immigration help without being allowed to. Knowing the warning signs can protect you.

Check who is advising you

In the UK, only regulated people can give immigration advice. That includes solicitors regulated by the SRA and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, formerly the OISC. You can check both on public registers online. Every firm we introduce is regulated.

Warning signs

  • Someone offers you a UK job in return for a fee
  • Someone promises that your visa will definitely be granted
  • Someone tells you to leave information out of your application
  • Someone asks for payment in cash or to a personal account
  • Someone will not give you their registration details
  • An offer seems too good to be true

If something feels wrong

Do not pay anything until you have checked. A regulated adviser can look at an offer or a document and tell you whether anything seems unusual. If you have already paid someone and are worried, keep any messages and receipts and speak to a regulated adviser.

Keep your own records

Keep copies of everything you send to the Home Office and everything you receive back, including emails. Note the dates of applications and decisions. Having your own records makes it much easier for any adviser to help you later.

Using official sources

The official place to apply for UK visas and to find current fees and processing times is GOV.UK. Be careful with websites that look official but are not, especially if they charge extra to submit an application for you. If you are unsure whether a website or message is genuine, ask a regulated adviser.

Immigration advisers usually charge a fixed fee for each application or stage of work, and some charge hourly for complex matters. Home Office application fees and any Immigration Health Surcharge are separate from the adviser’s fee and are paid to the Home Office.

Ask for the fee and what it covers in writing before you agree to anything. A regulated adviser must explain their costs clearly and tell you how to complain if something goes wrong.

Check who is advising you

Why do the rules keep changing?
Immigration policy is set by the government and reviewed regularly, often in response to the economy and the jobs market. Changes can affect fees, guidance and the kinds of visa available. This is why advice from a few years ago, or from someone whose situation was different, may not help you now. A regulated adviser works from the current rules.

Will my employer talk to my adviser?
Often, yes. Where an employer is involved, advisers usually need information from them, and it helps if both sides can communicate. Ask your adviser how they normally work with employers, and let your employer know who is helping you. Keeping everyone on the same page reduces the risk of mistakes, such as details that do not match between documents.
My employer has its own adviser. Do I need one too?
Not necessarily. An employer's adviser acts for the employer, and some people are happy with that arrangement. Others want independent advice about their own position, especially for family matters. A regulated adviser can explain the difference and help you decide.
What if my job changes?
Changes to your job, employer or pay can matter for a work visa. If any change is proposed, speak to a regulated adviser before you agree to it. They can explain whether it affects your position and what, if anything, would need to happen. Keep copies of any letters about the change, such as a new contract or a letter confirming a pay rise or new job title.
What if I lose my job?
Losing your job can be worrying, and it can affect your immigration position. The timing can be important. Speak to a regulated adviser as soon as you know your job is ending, and keep copies of your contract, payslips and any letters from your employer.
Can my employer see what I tell my adviser?
If you instruct your own adviser, what you tell them is confidential under the rules of their regulator. If your employer has instructed the adviser, the position can be different, because the employer is the client. Ask at the start who the client is and how information will be shared, so that you understand the arrangement.
How much does a work visa cost?

Check GOV.UK for the current figures, or ask your adviser for a full breakdown before you apply. If family members are applying too, the total can be much higher, so it is worth getting a full picture early.

Can I pay in instalments?
Some firms allow fees to be paid in stages, for example part at the start and part when the application is submitted. Others ask for payment upfront. Government fees are usually paid when the application is made. Ask the firm what payment arrangements it offers before you commit.
Are cheaper advisers just as good?
Price is only one part of the picture. It is worth looking at what is included, who will handle your matter, how experienced they are and whether they are properly regulated. A clear written quote helps you compare firms fairly and avoid surprises later.
My visa ends soon. What should I do?
Leaflegal cannot advise on your situation, but we can introduce you to a regulated adviser quickly. Tell us the date your visa ends when you enquire. The sooner you speak to an adviser, the more time there is to understand your options and prepare.
I have received a letter from the Home Office.

My application was refused. Can you help?
We can introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at the refusal. There may be short time limits for some kinds of challenge, so tell us the date on the decision when you enquire. Have the refusal letter and a copy of your application ready for the adviser.
How quickly will an adviser contact me?

What is a sponsor licence?
It is permission from the Home Office that lets a UK organisation sponsor certain workers from overseas. The licence belongs to the business and comes with ongoing responsibilities for as long as it is held. A regulated adviser can explain what holding a licence would involve for your business in practice, and whether it is something worth exploring.

We have sponsored workers before. Do we still need advice?
Many employers who have sponsored before still use an adviser, because the rules and guidance change often. What applied a few years ago may not apply now. A regulated adviser can look at your current position and explain anything that has changed since you last dealt with sponsorship.
Can an adviser help an overseas business?
Yes. Many regulated advisers work with overseas companies that are setting up in the UK or moving staff here. Tell us a little about your plans when you enquire. The adviser will explain how they can help and what they would need from you. Having a short description of your group structure and your plans for the UK will make the first conversation more useful.
What if we only need one worker?
Plenty of businesses that look into sponsorship only have one or two roles in mind. Advisers work with businesses of all sizes, from sole employers to large groups. A regulated adviser can talk through your plans and explain what would be involved, whatever the number of roles.
Why do sponsor licence rules change so often?
Immigration policy is set by the government and is reviewed regularly, often in response to the economy and the labour market. Changes can affect fees, guidance and the way the system works. This is one reason why employers find it helpful to have a regulated adviser who keeps up to date and can explain what any change means for their business.
What happens in the first conversation?
The adviser will usually ask about your business, the roles you have in mind and any deadlines. They will then explain how they could help and what it might cost. It is a chance for both sides to see whether they are a good fit. You do not need to prepare a full set of documents beforehand.
