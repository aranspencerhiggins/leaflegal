Indefinite leave to remain in brief

Indefinite leave to remain, often called ILR or settlement, is a significant application and mistakes can be costly. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated immigration firm that handles ILR applications.

We do not advise on whether you qualify for indefinite leave to remain. The regulated firm advises on your situation and the application.

Indefinite leave to remain explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Settlement means permission to live in the UK permanently, without a time limit on your stay. It is most often granted as indefinite leave to remain, which people usually shorten to ILR. For many people it is the end of a long journey through the immigration system, sometimes after many years of renewing visas, paying fees and keeping careful records.

Because settlement is permanent, it often feels like the most important application a person will make. It can affect where you live, how you work, how you plan your family's future and whether you can later think about British citizenship. It is natural to want a professional to look at everything before anything is sent to the Home Office.

Every person's history is different. Two people who arrived in the UK in the same year, on the same type of visa, can have very different paths to settlement because of what has happened since. That is why general information can only go so far, and why the details of your own case are a matter for a regulated adviser.

This page explains, in plain terms, what working with a regulated adviser on a settlement matter usually looks like. It covers why people ask for help, what the first conversation is like, what to have ready, how to choose and check an adviser, how fees and timescales usually work, and what to do if a letter or deadline arrives.

A matter that rewards care

A settlement application often draws on many years of a person's life. Old payslips, previous visas, letters from employers and records of travel can all become relevant. Bringing this together takes time and care, and it is one of the main reasons people value having someone experienced alongside them.

What Leaflegal does

Leaflegal does not give immigration advice and cannot tell you whether you can apply for settlement. We are not regulated to do so. We introduce you to one regulated adviser, either a solicitor regulated by the SRA or an adviser registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, who can look at your full history and explain your options.

Some people make their settlement application without help. Others prefer to have a regulated adviser involved from the start, or at least to have their case checked. There is no single right answer, and the choice is yours. These are some of the reasons people commonly give for asking for help.

Peace of mind

Settlement applications are detailed and the Home Office fee is significant. Many people simply want reassurance that their application is complete and that nothing has been missed before they submit it.

A long or complicated history

People who have held several different visas, changed employers, moved home many times or spent time abroad often find it hard to piece their history together. An adviser is used to working through long histories in a structured way.

Worries about the past

Some people are anxious about something in their past, such as a late application, a previous refusal or a police matter. These worries can feel heavy. Talking them through with a regulated adviser, in confidence, often helps people understand where they stand.

Family applications

When a partner or children are involved, there can be several applications to plan at once. People often want help coordinating the timing and paperwork for the whole family.

Changing rules

Settlement policy has changed many times, and further changes have been announced. People who planned years ago sometimes want to check how current policy affects them. A regulated adviser keeps up with these changes as part of their work.

Time pressure

When a visa is close to running out, there can be very little time to prepare. People in this position often want an adviser who can act quickly and keep things on track.

Someone to deal with the Home Office

Waiting for a decision can be stressful, particularly if letters arrive or questions are asked. Some people like knowing that a professional is handling communication with the Home Office for them and can explain each step as it happens.

Every adviser works in their own way, but most settlement matters follow a broadly similar pattern. Knowing what to expect can make the process feel less daunting.

1. Initial enquiry

After you make an enquiry through Leaflegal, the adviser usually gets in touch to arrange a first conversation. This might be a phone call, a video call or a meeting in person, depending on the firm.

2. First consultation

In the first consultation, the adviser listens to your story, asks questions and looks at the documents you have. They will explain what they think the options are and what further information they need. Some firms charge for this consultation and some do not, so it is worth asking in advance.

3. Letter of engagement

If you decide to go ahead, the adviser sends you a client care letter or terms of business. This sets out what they will do, what they will charge, who will work on your case and how to complain if something goes wrong. It is worth reading it carefully and asking about anything that is unclear.

4. Gathering information

The adviser will usually send you a list of documents and information they need. This stage often takes longer than people expect, especially where older records have to be found. Many advisers use a secure online portal for uploading documents.

5. Preparing the application

The adviser checks the documents, prepares or reviews the online form and may write a covering letter explaining your circumstances. You will usually be asked to check everything carefully before it is submitted, because you are responsible for the accuracy of what is said.

6. Submission and waiting

Once the application is submitted, there is usually a biometrics or identity appointment. Then there is a waiting period. Your adviser can deal with any letters or requests from the Home Office during this time.

7. The decision

When a decision arrives, your adviser can explain what it means. If the outcome is positive, they can tell you what happens next. If not, they can explain what options may be available and how quickly you need to act.

The first consultation is a chance for the adviser to understand your situation and for you to decide whether you want to work with them. Being prepared helps you get the most from it.

What to have ready

You do not need to have every document in order before the first meeting. But the more the adviser can see, the more useful the conversation is likely to be. In general terms, it helps to gather the following.

  • Your current passport and any old passports
  • Your current visa or eVisa details and the date it ends
  • Copies of previous Home Office decisions and letters
  • A rough timeline of your time in the UK
  • A note of any trips abroad you can remember
  • Details of any family members who might be involved
  • Anything you are worried about, written down so you do not forget it

Being open

Advisers can only help with what they know about. Many people feel embarrassed about something in their past, but advisers deal with difficult histories every day. What you tell a regulated adviser is confidential, and being open from the start helps them give you an accurate picture.

Questions you might ask

  • Who will work on my case day to day
  • How will we keep in touch, and how quickly do you usually reply
  • What will this cost, and what is included
  • What could make the cost go up
  • What do you need from me, and by when
  • How long might the whole process take
  • What happens if the Home Office asks for more information

Bringing someone with you

Some people like to have a partner, relative or friend with them at the first meeting, either for support or to help remember what was said. Most advisers are happy with this, though they may want to speak to you alone for part of the conversation. Taking notes, or asking the adviser to confirm key points by email, can also help.

After the meeting

Take time to think about what the adviser said. You are under no obligation to instruct them. If you do go ahead, it is common to receive written confirmation of the next steps soon after.

Immigration advice in the UK is tightly regulated. It is a criminal offence for someone to give immigration advice or services unless they are qualified and regulated to do so. Checking who you are dealing with is one of the most useful things you can do.

Who can advise

Regulated immigration advice can be given by solicitors regulated by the SRA, chartered legal executives and others regulated by CILEx Regulation, barristers, and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, which was previously called the OISC. Advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority are authorised at different levels, and the level determines the kind of work they can do.

Checking registration

The SRA, CILEx Regulation and the Immigration Advice Authority each have public registers you can search online. A genuine adviser will be happy to give you their registration details. If someone offering help cannot or will not tell you how they are regulated, that is a warning sign.

Other things to look for

  • Clear information about fees before any work starts
  • A written client care letter or terms of business
  • A named person responsible for your case
  • Experience with settlement matters
  • A complaints procedure you can understand
  • Professional indemnity insurance, which regulated firms are required to hold

Warning signs

Be cautious of anyone who guarantees a result, asks for large cash payments without paperwork, encourages you to leave information off a form or pressures you to decide on the spot. No regulated adviser can promise what the Home Office will decide.

Getting a feel for the adviser

Beyond checking registration, many people simply want an adviser they feel comfortable with. A good adviser listens, explains things in plain language, tells you honestly what they can and cannot do, and is clear about cost. If something does not feel right in the first conversation, you are free not to go ahead.

Every firm or adviser Leaflegal introduces is regulated to give immigration advice.

There are usually two kinds of cost in a settlement matter. One is the fee charged by the Home Office. The other is the fee charged by your adviser.

Home Office fees

The Home Office charges an application fee for each person, and there may be other official costs depending on the case. These fees are set by the government and change regularly, sometimes more than once a year. Check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask your adviser.

Adviser fees

Many advisers offer a fixed fee for a settlement application, which may include an initial review, preparing the application and dealing with routine follow up. Some offer a lower fee for a check of an application you have prepared yourself. More complex matters may be charged by the hour. Ask for the fee in writing and check whether Home Office fees are included.

Timescales

The time from first contact to a decision varies a great deal. Gathering documents can take weeks, and Home Office processing times change. The Home Office publishes its current service standards on GOV.UK, and your adviser can give you an idea of how long things are taking at the moment.

Planning ahead

Many people find it helps to speak to an adviser well before a visa is due to end, rather than in the final weeks. Starting early leaves time to find records, ask questions and make decisions without pressure.

Paying in stages

Some advisers ask for payment in stages, for example part at the start and the rest before the application is submitted. Others ask for the whole fee up front. Many firms hold money for official fees in a client account. Your client care letter should explain how and when payment is due.

What does indefinite leave to remain mean?
Indefinite leave to remain, often called ILR, is permission to stay in the UK without a time limit. It is the most common form of settlement. Whether it is relevant to you, and how it might apply to your situation, is something a regulated adviser can explain after looking at your history.
Is settlement the same as British citizenship?
No. Settlement is a type of immigration status, while British citizenship is a nationality. They are separate and involve separate applications. Many people hold settlement for years without applying for citizenship, and some never do. A regulated adviser can explain the differences and what each means in practice for you.
Can Leaflegal tell me if I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We cannot tell you whether you can apply or when. We introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at your circumstances and explain your options.
Why does everyone say the rules keep changing?
Settlement policy has changed many times over the years, and the government has announced further changes. This means information from friends, older websites or social media may be out of date. A regulated adviser works with the current rules every day and can explain how they apply to you.
What is an eVisa?
An eVisa is a digital record of a person's immigration status, linked to a UK Visas and Immigration account. The Home Office has moved away from physical cards towards eVisas. It is used to prove status to employers, landlords and others. Your adviser can explain how this works if you are granted permission.
Does settlement affect my family?
It can, because family members often have their own immigration status and their own applications to think about. How your application relates to theirs depends on your circumstances. A regulated adviser can look at the whole family's position and explain how applications might be planned.
What happens after I make an enquiry?
Leaflegal passes your enquiry to one regulated firm or adviser. They usually contact you within one working day, often sooner, to arrange a first conversation. They will ask some questions about your situation and explain how they can help and what it might cost.
Will I have to meet the adviser in person?
Not necessarily. Many advisers work by phone, video call and email, and some also offer face to face meetings at their offices. Ask the adviser how they usually work. Many people find remote meetings convenient, especially if they work long hours or have caring responsibilities.
How involved will I need to be?
You will need to provide documents, answer questions and check the application carefully before it is submitted, because you are responsible for what it says. The adviser does the legal work, but they rely on you for accurate information. Most advisers will give you a clear list of what they need.
Can I speak to the adviser in my own language?
Some advisers speak other languages, and some firms can arrange interpreters. It is worth mentioning this when you enquire so the firm can tell you what is available. Being able to explain your history clearly can make the process easier and less stressful.
What if I am unhappy with the service?
Regulated advisers must have a complaints procedure, which should be explained in your client care letter. If a complaint is not resolved, you may be able to take it further, for example to the Legal Ombudsman or the Immigration Advice Authority, depending on who regulates the adviser.
Can the adviser deal with the Home Office for me?
Yes. Once instructed, your adviser can usually act as your representative, which means the Home Office may write to them about your application. They can respond to requests for information and keep you updated. Ask how they will let you know when something arrives.
How do I know the adviser is regulated?
You can search the public registers kept by the SRA, CILEx Regulation and the Immigration Advice Authority. A regulated adviser will give you their registration details if you ask. Every firm or adviser Leaflegal introduces is regulated to give immigration advice.
What should I bring to the first consultation?
In general, it helps to have your passport, details of your current visa, any Home Office letters and a rough timeline of your time in the UK. A note of trips abroad and any worries you have is also useful. The adviser will tell you if they need anything else.
I cannot find some old documents. Is that a problem?
Many people have gaps in their paperwork, especially from years ago. Advisers are used to this and can explain what other records might help. Sometimes old records can be requested from employers, banks or government departments. Mention any missing documents at the start so there is time to deal with them.
Should I tell the adviser about something I am worried about?
Advisers can only work with what they know. What you tell a regulated adviser is confidential, and they deal with difficult histories regularly. Raising a concern early gives them the chance to explain how it might be relevant and avoids surprises later.
How early should I start?
There is no fixed answer, and it depends on your situation. Many people find it helps to make contact well before their current permission ends, so there is time to gather documents and ask questions. If your visa is due to end soon, say so when you enquire so the firm knows it is urgent.
Can I prepare my own application and ask an adviser to check it?
Some advisers offer a checking service for applications people have prepared themselves, usually for a lower fee than full representation. Others prefer to handle the whole matter. Ask the adviser what they offer and what a check would cover.
Do I need to keep copies of everything?
It is sensible to keep copies of every document you send and every letter you receive. Your adviser will also keep a file. Having your own copies can help in future, for example if you later apply for citizenship or need to prove your history.
Do I need to have everything organised before I call?
No. Many people make contact with documents in a box or spread across emails. The adviser can help you work out what is needed and how to organise it. Having your passport and current visa details to hand for the first call is usually enough to get started.
How much will a settlement application cost?
There are Home Office fees, which change regularly and are listed on GOV.UK, and separate adviser fees. Many advisers offer fixed fees for settlement work. Ask for the full cost in writing before you agree, and check whether Home Office fees are included.
Why do advisers charge different amounts?
Fees vary depending on the adviser's experience, location, how complex the case is and what the fee includes. A higher fee may include more support, such as dealing with follow up requests. Comparing what is included, not just the headline price, gives a clearer picture.
How long will it take to get a decision?
Processing times vary and change over time. The Home Office publishes its current service standards on GOV.UK, and some applications may have a faster service available. Your adviser can tell you how long things are currently taking and what might affect the timing in your case.
Is legal aid available?
Legal aid is not usually available for most settlement applications, although it can be available in some limited circumstances. A regulated adviser can tell you whether any form of funding might apply to you.
Does Leaflegal charge me anything?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm or adviser we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that does not change what you pay them.
Will I be charged for the first consultation?
Some advisers offer a short free first conversation and others charge a fixed fee for a full consultation. If there is a charge, it should be explained before the meeting. It is worth asking when the firm first contacts you so there are no surprises.
I have received a letter from the Home Office. What do I do?
Read it carefully and note the date it was sent and any deadline it mentions. If you already have an adviser, send it to them straight away. If not, many people contact a regulated adviser quickly, because some letters have short time limits.
My visa ends soon. Can someone help quickly?
Many advisers can act quickly where there is a deadline. When you make an enquiry, tell us the date your visa ends so the firm can see it is urgent. They will explain what they can do in the time available.
What if the Home Office asks for more documents?
Requests for further information usually have a deadline. Missing it can affect the outcome. If your adviser is representing you, they will usually receive the request and help you respond. If you receive it directly, it helps to pass it to them as soon as possible.
Is my enquiry kept private?
Yes. Leaflegal only shares your details with the regulated firm or adviser we introduce you to, and only with your consent. We never share them with advertising platforms. Once you speak to the adviser, they have their own professional duties of confidentiality.
Can I travel while my application is being decided?
Travel while an application is pending can affect some applications, and the position depends on your circumstances. This is a question to raise with your adviser before you book anything, so they can explain how it might affect you.
What is the difference between naturalisation and registration?
They are two different ways of applying to become a British citizen. Naturalisation is the term usually used for adults. Registration is used for children and some other people in particular situations. Which, if either, is relevant to you is a question for a regulated adviser who can look at your circumstances.
Is citizenship the same as indefinite leave to remain?
No. Indefinite leave to remain is an immigration status that lets a person stay in the UK without a time limit. Citizenship is a nationality. They are separate, with separate applications. A regulated adviser can explain what each means and how they relate to each other in your situation.
Could I already be British without knowing it?
Sometimes people are British without realising, usually because of a parent's or grandparent's history. Nationality law is complicated and has changed many times. A regulated adviser who works with nationality law can look at your family history and explain whether this might be worth exploring.
Will I have to give up my other nationality?
The UK side is something your adviser can explain. Whether you can keep your existing nationality also depends on the law of the other country, which varies. Many people check the position with the other country's embassy or authorities before they apply.
Can Leaflegal tell me whether I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration or nationality advice and does not do so. We introduce you to a regulated adviser, who can look at your situation and explain your options.
What rights does citizenship give?
British citizens can live in the UK without immigration permission, hold a British passport and take part fully in UK democratic life. The full picture depends on your circumstances, including any other nationality you hold. A regulated adviser can explain what becoming British would mean for you.
Is applying for citizenship compulsory?
No. Applying is a personal choice, and many people with long term status in the UK decide not to apply, or to wait. Some think about cost, some about their other nationality and some about family plans. A regulated adviser can explain the practical differences so that you can make an informed decision.
What happens after I enquire?
Leaflegal passes your details to one regulated firm or adviser. They usually contact you within one working day, often sooner, to arrange a first conversation, find out more about your situation and explain how they can help and what it might cost.
