Refusals and appeals explained

Pick a topic to read more.

Being refused by the Home Office is hard. A refusal can put a job, a home, a relationship or a family's plans in doubt, and the letter that brings the news is often long, formal and difficult to follow. Many people feel shocked, frightened or angry when it arrives. Those feelings are completely understandable.

A refusal is a decision, and decisions can sometimes be questioned, revisited or followed by a different approach. What, if anything, can be done depends entirely on the decision itself, the reasons given and the person's circumstances. The letter will usually explain what the Home Office considers your options to be, and it may mention an appeal, a review or neither.

What almost every refusal has in common is time pressure. Many decisions come with deadlines, and some options are lost once those deadlines pass. This is why people who have been refused often want to speak to a regulated adviser as quickly as possible.

It can help to remember that you are not alone. Many people receive a refusal at some point in their immigration journey, including people who later go on to settle in the UK. A refusal is a serious moment, but it is often the start of a conversation with an adviser rather than the end of the road.

This page explains, in general terms, what tends to happen after a refusal, how working with a regulated adviser usually goes, what to have ready, how to check an adviser and how fees and timescales usually work. It does not explain whether you can challenge your decision. Only a regulated adviser who has read your letter can do that.

  • Settlement and indefinite leave to remain

The refusal letter is the most important document you have after a decision. How you handle it in the first few days can make a real difference to what options remain open.

Keep the whole letter

Keep every page of the letter, any attachments and the envelope or email it came in. The way and date it was sent can matter when working out deadlines. If it came by email, keeping the original email rather than a forwarded copy can help.

Find the dates

Look for the date of the decision and any deadline mentioned. Write them down somewhere you will see them. If the letter mentions a time limit, it is usually counted from a particular date, and an adviser can confirm exactly what it means.

Informal opinions

Friends, family and online groups often mean well, but their experience may not match your situation, and rules change. Time spent waiting for informal views is time lost from any deadline. A regulated adviser can give you an accurate view based on your actual letter.

Other letters

Sometimes a refusal is followed by further letters, for example about reporting to the Home Office, your current permission or removal from the UK. Keep these too, and share them with your adviser as soon as they arrive. They can change how urgent your situation is.

Contact details

It helps if the Home Office, and any adviser, has your current address, email and phone number. Letters that go astray can mean missed deadlines. If you have moved since you applied, mention this to your adviser so they can explain how to update your details.

When you enquire

When you make an enquiry through Leaflegal, include the date on your decision letter and any deadline it mentions. We pass this to the firm so they can see how urgent your matter is and prioritise it.

After a refusal, the first conversation with a regulated adviser is usually focused and practical. The adviser needs to understand quickly what has been decided, why, and how much time there is.

What the adviser will want to see

In general terms, it helps to have the following ready. If you do not have something, tell the adviser and they will work with what you do have.

  • The full refusal letter and any attachments
  • A copy of the application that was refused, if you have it
  • The documents you sent with the application
  • Your passport and current immigration status details
  • Any earlier Home Office letters or decisions
  • Any new documents or information that has come to light since

What the adviser will ask

Expect questions about how the application was made, who prepared it, what was sent, what has happened since and what matters most to you now. The adviser may ask about your family, your work and your life in the UK. They are trying to understand the whole picture, not just the refusal.

What the adviser will explain

After reviewing the decision, a regulated adviser can explain what the refusal means, what options they think may exist, the time limits, the likely costs and the risks. They should also be honest if they think there is little that can be done. No adviser can promise a particular outcome.

Looking after yourself

A refusal can affect sleep, work and relationships. Some people find it helps to involve a trusted family member or friend in the practical side, such as finding documents or keeping a note of dates. Advisers are used to working with people who are anxious and will take time to explain things.

Being honest about what happened

Sometimes a refusal follows a mistake on the original application, a missing document or information that was not mentioned. People can feel embarrassed about this. A regulated adviser needs the full story to help, and what you tell them is confidential. Advisers deal with situations like this all the time.

If you had help before

If someone helped with the original application, it is useful to tell the new adviser who it was and to ask the previous adviser for a copy of their file. Some people stay with the same adviser after a refusal, and others prefer a fresh view. Either is fine.

Once you decide to instruct a regulated adviser, the work usually moves at a pace set by any deadline. Here is a general picture of what it can look like.

Many people say the hardest part is the uncertainty. A good adviser helps by explaining what is happening at each stage, what is likely to come next and roughly when. Even when there is no news, a short update can make waiting easier.

Engagement

The adviser sends a client care letter setting out the work, the fee and how to complain. In urgent cases this can happen very quickly, sometimes on the same day. Read it and ask about anything that is unclear.

Acting on any deadline

Where there is a time limit, the adviser will usually deal with it first. Sometimes that means taking an initial step to protect your position before all the evidence is gathered.

Building the case

The adviser may then ask for further documents, statements from you or other people, and other evidence. They may instruct a barrister if there is a hearing. This stage can be demanding, and good communication helps.

Hearings and decisions

Some matters involve a hearing in front of a judge, either in person or by video. Others are decided on paper. Your adviser will explain what to expect and help you prepare if you need to take part.

Staying in touch

Telling the adviser about changes

Circumstances can change while a matter is ongoing. A new job, a pregnancy, a new relationship, illness or a change in a family member's status may all be relevant. Telling your adviser about changes as they happen allows them to consider whether they matter.

Refusal matters can be stressful and can take months. Agreeing at the start how and how often you will hear from your adviser can make things easier. If your contact details or circumstances change, tell your adviser straight away.

After a refusal, people can feel desperate, and that can make them vulnerable to poor or unlawful advice. Taking a moment to check who you are dealing with is worthwhile, even when time is short.

Who can help

Only regulated people can lawfully give immigration advice in the UK. They include solicitors regulated by the SRA, people regulated by CILEx Regulation, barristers and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, formerly the OISC. Advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority are authorised at different levels, and work involving tribunals and appeals usually needs the higher levels. It is reasonable to ask an adviser whether they are authorised for the work you need.

How to check

Each regulator has a public register you can search online. A genuine adviser will give you their registration details if you ask.

Signs of a good adviser

  • They read the actual decision before giving a view
  • They are clear about deadlines and act on them
  • They explain risks as well as possibilities
  • They give fee information in writing
  • They never guarantee success
  • They tell you who is handling your case

Warning signs

Be wary of anyone who promises a definite result, asks for cash with no receipt, suggests giving false information or will not say how they are regulated. Unregulated help, however well meant, can make a difficult situation worse.

Questions to ask

  • Have you dealt with refusals like mine before
  • Are you authorised for the kind of work my case may need
  • What do you think the deadline is, and how will you meet it
  • What will the first stage cost, and what might come after
  • Who will be my main contact, and how quickly do you reply
  • Will a barrister be involved, and how is that charged

Costs after a refusal depend on what the adviser recommends and how complex the matter is. There are usually official fees and adviser fees.

Official fees

Some processes after a refusal carry a Home Office, tribunal or court fee, and making a fresh application also involves a fee. Some people may be able to get help with tribunal or court fees. These amounts change, so check GOV.UK or ask your adviser.

Adviser fees

Many advisers offer a fixed fee for reviewing a refusal letter and explaining the options. Further work may be charged as a fixed fee for each stage or by the hour. If a barrister is needed for a hearing, their fee may be extra. Ask for an estimate of the total in writing.

Legal aid

Legal aid is available for some immigration matters, such as asylum and some other protection or detention cases, but not for most others. A regulated adviser can tell you whether legal aid might be available to you.

Timescales

Some steps after a refusal happen within days. Others, such as hearings, can take months to arrange. Your adviser can give you an idea of current timescales and what might affect them.

Comparing options

Where more than one option exists, the adviser can usually set out the likely cost and time of each. Knowing both helps many people make a decision that fits their circumstances and budget. The decision is always yours.

Keeping track of costs

If a matter moves into a new stage, such as preparing for a hearing, a good adviser will usually tell you before the cost changes and confirm any new estimate in writing. It is reasonable to ask for an update on costs at any point.

Refusals and appeals questions and answers

Is there a deadline to challenge a refusal?
There can be very short time limits. Tell the adviser the date of your decision straight away.
Can Leaflegal tell me if I should appeal?
No. Only a regulated adviser can advise you.
How fast can someone call?
Urgent enquiries are flagged to the firm so they can prioritise them.
Can Leaflegal tell me if I qualify for a visa?
No. We are not allowed to give immigration advice and we don’t. Only a regulated adviser can tell you about your options. We introduce you to one.
Who will contact me?
One firm regulated to give immigration advice, such as a firm of solicitors regulated by the SRA or an adviser registered with the Immigration Advice Authority.
Does it cost anything?
Our introduction is free. The firm will explain its own fees before you agree to anything.
I have just been refused. What should I do first?
Many people start by finding the date of the decision and any deadline in the letter, keeping every page and contacting a regulated adviser quickly. An adviser can read the decision and explain what it means. Leaflegal can introduce you to one, and we pass the deadline to the firm so they can prioritise your matter.
Can Leaflegal tell me if I can appeal?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We cannot tell you whether you can appeal or what your options are. We introduce you to a regulated adviser who can read your decision and explain what may be possible.
Is there a deadline?
Many refusals come with time limits, and some are short. The letter usually mentions any time limit and when it starts. Because the details matter, a regulated adviser can confirm exactly how long you have. When you enquire, tell us the date on your letter.
Does a refusal mean I did something wrong?
Not necessarily. Refusals happen for many reasons, and sometimes the Home Office itself makes mistakes. The reasons in the letter are the starting point. A regulated adviser can explain them in plain language and talk through what they mean for you.
Can I stay in the UK after a refusal?
That depends on your circumstances and the decision, and it is a question for a regulated adviser. It is one of the first things many people ask about, and an adviser can usually explain your position quickly once they have seen the letter and your immigration history.
Should I just apply again?
Some people do make a new application after a refusal, and for others a different step is more suitable. Which approach fits your situation depends on the reasons for refusal and your circumstances. A regulated adviser can explain the options and the risks of each.
I do not understand the letter. Is that normal?
Very normal. Refusal letters are written in formal language and refer to rules and guidance that most people have never read. Advisers read these letters every day. They can explain what each part means and what the Home Office is saying.
Will a refusal affect future applications?
It may need to be mentioned on future forms, and the reasons for refusal can be relevant later. How much it matters depends on the decision and your circumstances. A regulated adviser can explain how your refusal might affect any future plans, including applications for family members.
How quickly can an adviser help?
Firms usually contact people within one working day of an enquiry, often sooner. When there is a deadline, we flag it to the firm. Many advisers can act quickly in urgent cases. If your deadline is very close, say so clearly when you enquire.
What will the adviser need from me?
In general, the refusal letter, a copy of the original application, the documents you sent, your passport and any other Home Office letters. They will also want to hear what has happened since. If something is missing, tell them and they will work with what you have.
Can I use the same adviser who did my original application?
You can, and some people prefer to. Others want a fresh pair of eyes after a refusal. Either way, the adviser handling things now will need the full file. Your previous adviser should be able to provide a copy if you ask.
Will I have to go to a hearing?
Some matters involve a hearing and others do not. If there is a hearing, it may be in person or by video, and your adviser will explain what to expect and help you prepare. Interpreters can be arranged if needed.
Can a family member contact an adviser for me?
Yes. A relative or friend can make the enquiry, which can help if you are abroad, unwell or in detention. The adviser will need to speak to you directly before acting for you, and will explain how they can take your instructions.
What if I cannot afford an adviser?
Legal aid is available for some types of immigration matter but not most. Some advisers offer fixed fees for an initial review, which can help you understand your options before committing to more. A regulated adviser can tell you whether any funding might apply.
Can the adviser speak to the Home Office for me?
Once instructed, your adviser can usually act as your representative, so official letters may go to them as well as or instead of to you. They can deal with correspondence and keep you informed. Ask how they will let you know when anything arrives.
How do I know an adviser is genuine?
You can search the public registers kept by the SRA, CILEx Regulation and the Immigration Advice Authority. A genuine adviser will give you their registration details. Every firm or adviser Leaflegal introduces is regulated to give immigration advice.
Can any regulated adviser handle an appeal?
Not always. Advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority are authorised at different levels, and tribunal work usually needs a higher level. Solicitors and barristers have their own rules. It is reasonable to ask any adviser whether they are authorised and experienced in the work you need.
What if someone promises I will win?
No regulated adviser can promise the outcome of a Home Office or tribunal decision. A guarantee is a warning sign. A good adviser will explain the possibilities and the risks honestly, and give their view in writing if you ask.
Is it a problem if help is offered by someone in my community?
Informal support can be valuable, but only regulated people can lawfully give immigration advice. Unregulated advice can lead to missed deadlines or mistakes. You can check whether someone is regulated using the public registers.
What if I am unhappy with my adviser?
Regulated advisers must have a complaints procedure, which should be in your client care letter. If a complaint is not resolved, you may be able to go to the Legal Ombudsman or the Immigration Advice Authority, depending on who regulates the adviser.
Should I ask the adviser for their view in writing?
Many people find it helpful to have the adviser's main points confirmed by email or letter, especially deadlines, options and costs. It gives you something to refer back to and share with family. Most advisers will confirm the key points in writing after the first conversation.
How much does it cost to deal with a refusal?
It depends on the work involved. There may be official fees, which change and are listed on GOV.UK, and adviser fees. Many advisers offer a fixed fee to review the decision first. Ask for the full likely cost in writing before you agree to further work.
Will I need a barrister?
Some matters, particularly those with a hearing, may involve a barrister instructed by your adviser. Their fee may be separate. Your adviser will explain if a barrister is likely to be needed and what it might cost.
How long will it all take?
It varies widely. Some steps take days and others take months. Tribunal and court timescales depend on their workload. Your adviser can tell you what to expect at each stage and what might cause delay.
Is legal aid available?
Legal aid covers some immigration matters, including asylum and some detention cases, but not most others. A regulated adviser can tell you whether you might be eligible for legal aid or any other funding.
Does Leaflegal charge me?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm or adviser we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that does not change what you pay them.
Is there a charge for the first conversation?
Some advisers offer a short free conversation and charge for a full review of the decision, while others charge from the start. Any charge should be explained before the work begins. It is worth asking when the firm first contacts you.
I have been told I will be removed from the UK. Can anyone help?
This is very urgent. A regulated adviser can look at your situation and explain whether anything can be done. When you enquire, say clearly that removal has been arranged and give any dates you have, so the firm can act as quickly as possible.
Someone I know has been detained. What can I do?
A family member or friend can make an enquiry on their behalf. Have their full name, date of birth, Home Office reference if known and where they are held. Legal aid may be available for some detention matters, and an adviser can explain more.
I missed a deadline. Is it too late?
Not always. In some cases late steps can still be taken if there is a good reason for the delay, but the longer the delay, the harder it can be. A regulated adviser can look at what happened and explain what options remain.
Is my enquiry confidential?
Yes. Leaflegal only shares your details with the one regulated firm or adviser we introduce you to, with your consent, and never with advertising platforms or the Home Office. The adviser then has their own duty of confidentiality.
My visa has been cancelled or cut short. Is that the same as a refusal?
It is a different type of decision, but it can have similar effects and may come with its own deadlines. The letter should explain the reasons and dates. A regulated adviser can read the decision and explain what it means and what options may exist.
What is the EU Settlement Scheme?
It is the scheme set up after Brexit to protect the rights of many EU, EEA and Swiss citizens and their family members who had made their lives in the UK. People granted status under it receive either settled status or pre-settled status, which is held digitally. A regulated adviser can explain how it relates to you.
What is the difference between settled and pre-settled status?
Settled status is permanent permission to stay. Pre-settled status is permission for a limited period. What your status means for you, and what may happen next, depends on your situation. A regulated adviser can look at your status and explain it in plain terms.
Can Leaflegal tell me whether I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We cannot tell you whether you can apply or what your status means. We introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at your situation and explain your options.
Has the scheme changed since it opened?
Yes. The Home Office has changed how the scheme works several times, including how it handles some types of status and applications. Information from a few years ago, or from friends, may be out of date. A regulated adviser works with the current position and can explain it.
Do I get a card or certificate?
Status under the scheme is usually digital. You can view it online and prove it using a share code. Some people still have older physical documents, but these do not usually replace the digital record. Your adviser can explain how this works for you.
Is the scheme the same as a visa?
The scheme is separate from the visa routes in the main Immigration Rules and has its own rules. People sometimes confuse the two. A regulated adviser can explain how the scheme relates to other parts of the immigration system in your situation.
Do Irish citizens need to think about the scheme?
Irish citizens have a separate position in the UK because of long standing arrangements between the UK and Ireland. Their family members may have different questions. A regulated adviser can explain how the scheme does or does not relate to you and your family.
How do I prove my status to an employer or landlord?
You can usually generate a share code on GOV.UK and give it to them with your date of birth. They then check your status online. GOV.UK explains the different checking services. If an employer or landlord has difficulty, the Home Office has guidance for them.
