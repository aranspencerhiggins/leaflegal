British citizenship in brief

A British citizenship application, including naturalisation, is the last step for many people who have settled in the UK. Leaflegal introduces you to one regulated immigration firm that handles citizenship applications.

We do not advise on eligibility for British citizenship. The regulated firm advises on your situation and the application.

British citizenship explained

Pick a topic to read more.

British citizenship is a nationality, not a visa. A British citizen can hold a British passport and live in the UK without needing immigration permission. For many people who have made their home here, becoming British feels like the moment they finally belong, and it can be an emotional step as much as a legal one.

People become British in different ways. Some are British from birth without ever applying. Others apply, either as adults or on behalf of their children. The terms you may hear are naturalisation, which is the word usually used for adults applying to become British, and registration, which is used for children and some other people. Which, if any, is relevant to you is something a regulated adviser can explain.

Nationality law is one of the oldest and most technical parts of UK law. It has changed many times over the last century, and the way it applies can depend on events that happened before a person was born. That is why two people with apparently similar stories can be in very different positions.

Citizenship applications also carry a significant Home Office fee, which is not usually refunded if an application does not succeed. For that reason, many people like a regulated adviser to look at their case before they decide whether and when to apply.

It is also a decision that people often make together as a family. Parents may be thinking about their own position and their children's at the same time, and grown up children sometimes look into citizenship because of questions about their parents' history. A regulated adviser can look at how each person's situation fits together.

Many people also find the process brings back memories of earlier, harder parts of their immigration journey. A good adviser understands this, takes time to explain each step and helps the process feel manageable rather than overwhelming.

Who this page is for

  • Adults thinking about becoming British
  • Parents thinking about citizenship for a child
  • People who wonder whether they might already be British
  • People whose families have a complicated nationality history
  • People who have had a citizenship application refused before
  • People who simply want to understand the process before deciding

Plenty of people apply for citizenship on their own. Many others choose to involve a regulated adviser, for a whole application or just a check. These are some of the reasons people commonly give.

The fee is high

Because the Home Office fee is substantial and not usually refunded on refusal, many people see advice as a way of reducing the risk of losing that money. Whether advice is worth it for you is your choice.

Something in the past

Many people worry about a matter from years ago, such as a fine, a police matter, a dispute with HMRC or a problem with an earlier immigration application. An adviser can talk this through in confidence and explain how it may be relevant.

Family history

Some people suspect they may already be British, or may have a claim to citizenship, because of a parent or grandparent. Working this out can involve old birth, marriage and passport records. Advisers who specialise in nationality law are used to this kind of research.

Children

Parents often want help understanding their children's position, especially where children were born in different countries or at different times. A regulated adviser can look at each child's circumstances.

Another nationality

Some people want to understand how becoming British fits with their existing nationality before they apply. An adviser can explain the UK side and may suggest checking the position with the other country's authorities.

A previous refusal

People who have been refused before often want to understand why, and whether anything has changed since. An adviser can read the refusal letter and explain what it means.

Confidence about the form

The application form asks detailed questions, and many people worry about answering something wrongly or leaving something out. Having an adviser go through the form with them, question by question, gives many people confidence that what they are submitting is accurate and complete.

Busy lives

Some people simply do not have the time to research the process, gather everything and keep track of correspondence. They prefer to hand the organising to a professional and focus on work and family.

The first conversation with a regulated adviser is where they learn about you and you learn about them. It is not a test, and there is no obligation to go ahead afterwards.

What usually happens

The adviser will ask about your background, your time in the UK, your family and any previous applications. They may ask for copies of documents in advance so that they can review them before you speak. By the end, they will usually explain what they think your options are, what they would need from you and what it would cost.

What to have ready

In general terms, it helps to have the following to hand. Your adviser will tell you if they need anything else.

  • Your passport and any older passports
  • Your current immigration status details
  • Any letters or decisions from the Home Office
  • Birth and marriage certificates, if you have them
  • Details of family members whose status might matter
  • A list of anything you are unsure or worried about

For family history questions

If your question is about whether you may already be British through a parent or grandparent, any old family documents can help. These might include birth certificates, old British passports, naturalisation or registration certificates, or records of where and when relatives were born. Do not worry if you have only some of them.

After the consultation

You do not have to decide anything on the day. Many people go away, think about what was said and talk it over with their family. If you want to go ahead, the adviser will confirm the next steps in writing. If you decide not to, you are under no obligation.

Only people who are qualified and regulated can lawfully give immigration and nationality advice in the UK. Giving that advice without being regulated is a criminal offence. Checking who you are dealing with protects you.

Regulated advisers

Regulated advice can come from solicitors regulated by the SRA, people regulated by CILEx Regulation, barristers, and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, which used to be called the OISC. Advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority are authorised at different levels, which limits the kind of work each can do.

How to check

Each regulator keeps a public register that you can search online. You can ask any adviser for their registration details and check them yourself. A genuine adviser will not mind being asked.

Signs of a good adviser

  • They explain things in plain language
  • They are honest about what they do not know yet
  • They give you clear fee information in writing
  • They tell you who will handle your case
  • They never promise a particular outcome
  • They respond within the timescales they set

Signs to be wary of

Be cautious of anyone who guarantees success, asks you to pay in cash without a receipt, suggests leaving anything off a form, or is not willing to say how they are regulated. Informal help from friends, community groups or online forums can feel reassuring, but unregulated people cannot lawfully give immigration advice.

Some people also look at an adviser's website, read about their experience and ask how long they have worked on nationality matters. These things do not replace checking registration, but they can help you decide who you feel comfortable with.

If you ever feel unsure about advice you have been given, you can ask the adviser to explain their reasoning or put it in writing. A regulated adviser expects questions and should be able to answer them clearly.

Every case is different, but most citizenship matters handled by an adviser follow a similar shape. Your adviser will explain exactly what applies to you.

1. Getting in touch

After you enquire through Leaflegal, the adviser usually contacts you within one working day to arrange a first conversation.

2. Review

The adviser looks at your history and documents and explains what they think the options are. If they think it would be better to wait, or that another step is needed first, they will usually say so.

3. Agreeing the work

If you decide to go ahead, you will receive a client care letter setting out the work, the fee and how to complain. Read it carefully and ask about anything you do not understand.

4. Preparing the application

The adviser gathers the information needed, prepares or checks the application and supporting documents, and asks you to review everything before it is submitted. You remain responsible for making sure the information is true and complete.

5. Waiting

After submission, there is usually an identity or biometrics step and then a waiting period that can last several months. The adviser can deal with any questions the Home Office raises.

6. After a decision

Your adviser will explain the decision and any next steps. If an application succeeds, there are further steps before a person becomes British and before they can hold a British passport, and the adviser can walk you through them. If it does not succeed, they can explain what options may be available.

Keeping in touch

During the process, many advisers send updates by email and let you know as soon as anything arrives from the Home Office. If you have not heard anything for a while, it is perfectly reasonable to ask for an update. Agreeing at the start how you will keep in touch can avoid worry later.

If your circumstances change

Life does not stop while an application is being prepared or decided. If something changes, such as a move, a new job, a trip abroad, a change of name or contact with the police, tell your adviser so they can explain whether it matters.

There are usually two types of cost. The Home Office charges its own fees, and your adviser charges for their work.

Home Office fees

Citizenship fees are set by the government and change from time to time. There may also be separate costs later, such as for a first British passport. Check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask your adviser.

Adviser fees

Many advisers offer a fixed fee for a citizenship application, and some offer a lower fee for checking an application you have prepared yourself. Research into family history or a complicated case may be charged differently, often by the hour. Ask for the fee in writing before work begins.

Families

Where several family members are involved, some advisers offer a reduced fee for each additional person. It is worth asking how the adviser charges for family matters.

Timescales

From a first conversation to a final outcome can take many months. Gathering documents, especially old family records, can take time, and Home Office processing times change. The Home Office publishes current service standards on GOV.UK, and your adviser can tell you what to expect.

Payment

Advisers differ in how they ask to be paid. Some take the full fee at the start, some split it into stages and some ask for money on account. Official fees are often paid directly to the Home Office as part of the online application. Your client care letter should make the arrangements clear.

What is the difference between naturalisation and registration?
They are two different ways of applying to become a British citizen. Naturalisation is the term usually used for adults. Registration is used for children and some other people in particular situations. Which, if either, is relevant to you is a question for a regulated adviser who can look at your circumstances.
Is citizenship the same as indefinite leave to remain?
No. Indefinite leave to remain is an immigration status that lets a person stay in the UK without a time limit. Citizenship is a nationality. They are separate, with separate applications. A regulated adviser can explain what each means and how they relate to each other in your situation.
Could I already be British without knowing it?
Sometimes people are British without realising, usually because of a parent's or grandparent's history. Nationality law is complicated and has changed many times. A regulated adviser who works with nationality law can look at your family history and explain whether this might be worth exploring.
Will I have to give up my other nationality?
The UK side is something your adviser can explain. Whether you can keep your existing nationality also depends on the law of the other country, which varies. Many people check the position with the other country's embassy or authorities before they apply.
Can Leaflegal tell me whether I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration or nationality advice and does not do so. We introduce you to a regulated adviser, who can look at your situation and explain your options.
What rights does citizenship give?
British citizens can live in the UK without immigration permission, hold a British passport and take part fully in UK democratic life. The full picture depends on your circumstances, including any other nationality you hold. A regulated adviser can explain what becoming British would mean for you.
Is applying for citizenship compulsory?
No. Applying is a personal choice, and many people with long term status in the UK decide not to apply, or to wait. Some think about cost, some about their other nationality and some about family plans. A regulated adviser can explain the practical differences so that you can make an informed decision.
What happens after I enquire?
Leaflegal passes your details to one regulated firm or adviser. They usually contact you within one working day, often sooner, to arrange a first conversation, find out more about your situation and explain how they can help and what it might cost.
Do I need to meet in person?
Not always. Many advisers work by phone, video and email, which can be easier around work and family life. Some also offer office meetings. Ask the adviser how they usually work and choose what suits you.
Will the adviser handle everything?
The adviser can prepare and submit the application and deal with the Home Office on your behalf. You will still need to provide information and documents, and check the application carefully before it is sent, because you are responsible for what it says.
Can the adviser help with my children's applications?
Many advisers handle children's citizenship matters. Children's situations can differ from their parents' and from each other's, so the adviser will usually look at each child separately. Mention all the children involved when you enquire.
What if I want a check rather than full help?
Some advisers offer a document check or a review of an application you have prepared yourself, usually at a lower cost. This does not suit every case, and the adviser will tell you if they think more help is needed. Ask what a check would cover.
Can I bring a family member to the meeting?
Usually yes. Many people bring a partner or relative for support or to help remember what was said. The adviser may want to speak to you on your own for part of the meeting. Mention it when arranging the appointment.
How do I check that an adviser is regulated?
The SRA, CILEx Regulation and the Immigration Advice Authority each have public registers you can search online. Any regulated adviser will give you their details if you ask. Every firm or adviser Leaflegal introduces is regulated to give immigration and nationality advice, but you are always welcome to check for yourself.
What should I have ready for the first conversation?
In general, your passport, details of your current status, any Home Office letters and any birth or marriage certificates you have. If your question is about family history, any old family documents help. Your adviser will tell you if they need more.
I am worried about something in my past. Should I mention it?
Advisers can only help with what they know, and what you tell a regulated adviser is confidential. Raising a concern at the start gives them the chance to explain how it might be relevant. Advisers deal with worries like this regularly and will not judge you.
I do not have my parents' documents. Can I still get help?
Yes. Many people start with only partial records. Advisers can explain what other records might help and where they might be found, such as official registers or archives. Gathering old records can take time, so it helps to mention gaps early.
Should I wait before contacting an adviser?
That is up to you. Some people find it helpful to speak to an adviser well before they plan to apply, so there is time to gather documents and understand the process. An early conversation does not commit you to anything.
Do I need translations of foreign documents?
Documents not in English or Welsh usually need a certified translation for Home Office purposes. Your adviser can explain what kind of translation is needed and may be able to recommend a translator. Translation costs are usually separate from adviser fees.
Should I keep copies of what I send?
It is sensible to keep copies of every document and form, as well as any letters you receive. Your adviser will keep their own file, but your own copies can be useful in future, for example for passport applications.
What if I have changed my name?
Many people have used different names over the years, for example after marriage or because of different spellings in different countries. Advisers see this often. Having any documents that show a change of name, such as a marriage certificate or deed poll, can help the adviser understand your records.
How much does a citizenship application cost?
There is a Home Office fee, which changes from time to time and is listed on GOV.UK, plus your adviser's fee. There may be later costs too, such as a first British passport. Ask your adviser for a full estimate in writing.
Is the Home Office fee refunded if I am refused?
Generally, no. That is one reason many people get advice before applying. Your adviser can explain the current position on fees and any limited circumstances where a refund or waiver may apply.
How long does it take?
It varies. Gathering documents can take weeks, and Home Office processing times change. The Home Office publishes current service standards on GOV.UK. Your adviser can tell you how long things are currently taking and what might affect your timescale.
Are there fee waivers?
There are limited circumstances in which the Home Office may waive some fees, particularly for some children. The rules on this are narrow and change over time. A regulated adviser can tell you whether it is worth exploring.
Does Leaflegal charge for the introduction?
No. Leaflegal is free to use. The firm or adviser we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that does not change what you pay them.
Can I pay the adviser in instalments?
Some advisers offer staged payments, especially for larger pieces of work, and others ask for the fee up front. Ask the adviser what options they have before you agree. Home Office fees are usually paid in full when the application is submitted.
I have had a letter from the Home Office about my application. What now?
Note the date it was sent and any deadline in it. If you have an adviser, send it to them straight away. Some letters ask for information within a set time, and missing it can affect the outcome.
My application was refused. Can an adviser help?
Yes. An adviser can read the refusal letter, explain the reasons and talk through what options may exist. Some options have time limits, so it helps to make contact soon after the decision arrives. Tell us the date of the decision when you enquire.
Will my enquiry be kept confidential?
Yes. Leaflegal only shares your details with the one regulated firm or adviser we introduce you to, with your consent, and never with advertising platforms. The adviser then has their own professional duty of confidentiality.
What if I move house while my application is pending?
It is important that the Home Office and your adviser can reach you. If your address, email or phone number changes, tell your adviser straight away so they can explain how to update the Home Office. Missed letters can cause delays or problems.
Can the adviser help after I become British?
Many advisers can explain the practical steps after a successful application, such as applying for a first British passport or updating records. Some people also return to the same adviser later for questions about their children or other family members. Ask what ongoing help the adviser offers.
What is a work visa?
It is permission from the Home Office to come to the UK, or stay in the UK, to work. There are several kinds of work visa, each for a different purpose, and they change over time. A regulated adviser can explain which, if any, might fit your situation, based on the rules as they stand today.
Can Leaflegal tell me which visa I need?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We do not ask about your immigration history or assess your chances. We introduce you to a firm or adviser who is regulated, and they will look at your situation and explain your options.
Can you find me a job?
No. Leaflegal is not a recruitment agency and cannot help you find work. We introduce you to a regulated immigration adviser who can help with the visa side of things. If you already have a job offer, mention it when you enquire so the adviser has the full picture. Be cautious of anyone who offers to find you a UK job in return for a payment.
I am already in the UK. Can you still help?
Yes. We introduce people both inside and outside the UK to regulated advisers. If you are already here, tell us when your current visa ends when you make your enquiry, so that the adviser knows how much time there is. Having the exact date to hand, and any letters you have received about your current visa, will help the first conversation go smoothly.
I am overseas. Can a UK adviser help me?
Yes. Many regulated advisers in the UK help people who are applying from abroad, working by phone, video and email. Tell us where you are when you enquire. The adviser will explain how they work with clients overseas and how they handle time differences.
Is it worth getting advice if my employer is handling things?
Some employers arrange advice for their workers, and some do not. Even when an employer is involved, some people like to have their own adviser to explain things from their point of view, particularly if family members are involved. A regulated adviser can explain how they would work alongside your employer.
Why do the rules keep changing?
Immigration policy is set by the government and reviewed regularly, often in response to the economy and the jobs market. Changes can affect fees, guidance and the kinds of visa available. This is why advice from a few years ago, or from someone whose situation was different, may not help you now. A regulated adviser works from the current rules.
What happens at the first consultation?
The adviser will ask about your background, your current situation, any job offer and your plans. They will explain how they could help and what it would cost. You do not need a full set of documents for this first conversation. It is also your chance to decide whether you want to work with them.
