EU Settlement Scheme explained

Pick a topic to read more.

The EU Settlement Scheme was set up after the UK left the European Union. Its purpose is to protect the rights of many citizens of EU countries, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland, and members of their families, who had made their lives in the UK. Millions of people have applied, and most now hold status under it.

There are two main types of status under the scheme, known as settled status and pre-settled status. Settled status is permanent. Pre-settled status is for a limited time. Status is held digitally rather than as a card or a stamp in a passport, and people prove it online.

For many people, the scheme has been simple. For others, questions keep coming up long after they first applied. Some people are not sure whether they applied at all, or whether a parent applied for them as a child. Others are unsure what their status means now, how to prove it, or what happens if they move abroad for a while. Family members may have their own questions, and some people have received letters or decisions they do not understand.

The scheme has its own rules, separate from other parts of the UK immigration system, and the way the Home Office runs it has changed several times since it opened. Information that was correct a few years ago may not be correct now. That is one reason many people want a regulated adviser to look at their own situation.

Questions about the scheme also tend to arise at important moments in life, such as starting a new job, renting a flat, applying for a mortgage, having a baby or caring for a parent. At those moments, a quick and accurate answer about your status can matter a great deal.

What Leaflegal does

Many people with questions about the scheme can find general information on GOV.UK. People usually turn to a regulated adviser when their situation does not fit neatly into general guidance, or when something has gone wrong.

There is no single reason, and many people come to an adviser with more than one question at once. These are some of the situations people commonly describe.

Uncertainty about status

Some people are simply not sure what status they hold, or whether they hold any. This can happen where an application was made many years ago, where a parent applied for a child, or where emails and account details have been lost. An adviser can help work out the position.

Not having applied

Some people never applied, perhaps because they did not know they needed to, because they were unwell, or because they assumed someone else had done it. Discovering this can be frightening, especially if an employer, landlord or bank asks for proof of status. A regulated adviser can look at what happened and explain what may be possible.

Family members

Families often have several people with different nationalities and different types of status. Partners, children and older relatives may each have their own questions, and some may be outside the UK. An adviser can look at the family as a whole.

Time abroad

People who have spent time abroad, or are planning to, often want to understand how this might affect their status. An adviser can explain how it applies to them.

Letters and decisions

Some people receive letters from the Home Office about their status, or a decision they did not expect. These can be hard to understand and may have deadlines.

Problems proving status

Some people hold status but cannot show it, because they cannot log in to their account, their details are out of date or an employer or landlord does not understand the online system. An adviser can help work out what has gone wrong.

Looking ahead

Others want to understand how their status fits with future plans, such as buying a home, bringing family to the UK or becoming British.

Status under the scheme is held digitally. Understanding how the digital system works can prevent a lot of stress, and it is one of the areas people most often ask about.

Some people find the digital system easy. Others, especially those who are less comfortable online or who applied many years ago with help from someone else, find it confusing. Neither is unusual, and help is available.

Your UKVI account

Status is linked to a UK Visas and Immigration account, sometimes called a UKVI account. The account is connected to the passport or identity document used when applying, and to an email address and phone number. Keeping these details up to date matters, because they are used to sign in and to link your status to your travel document.

Proving your status

You can view your status online on GOV.UK. To prove it to someone else, such as an employer, landlord or bank, you can usually generate a share code and give it to them with your date of birth. They then check your status online. There are separate services for different types of check, and GOV.UK explains which to use.

New passport or identity card

If you get a new passport or identity card, the details linked to your status may need updating. Travelling with a document that is not linked to your status can cause delays at the border or when checking in with an airline.

Changed email or phone number

If you can no longer access the email or phone number linked to your account, there is a process for updating them. It can take time, so many people deal with it before they need to prove their status urgently.

When things go wrong

Sometimes the online record shows the wrong information, a share code does not work or an employer does not understand the system. The Home Office has helplines and online forms for these problems. If they do not resolve things, a regulated adviser can help work out what has gone wrong and what can be done.

A tip for your records

Many people keep a note of their application reference number, the email used, the identity document linked to their status and the date of any decision. Keeping this somewhere safe can save time if anything needs sorting out later.

Your first conversation with a regulated adviser is a chance to explain your situation and to understand how they can help. There is no obligation to go ahead afterwards.

Some people feel nervous before speaking to an adviser, particularly if they are worried about their status. Advisers are used to this. The aim of the first conversation is to understand your situation calmly and explain things in plain language, not to judge.

What usually happens

The adviser will ask about your background, your time in the UK, your family and any applications or decisions so far. They may ask you to send documents before the conversation. By the end, they will usually explain what they think your options are, what they need and what it would cost.

Some advisers speak other European languages, and some firms can arrange interpreters. Mentioning this when you enquire helps the firm plan the conversation.

After the consultation

You do not need to decide anything on the day. Many people take time to think about what the adviser said and talk it through with family. If you go ahead, the adviser will send you a client care letter explaining the work, the fee and how to complain. If you do not, there is no obligation.

Only people who are qualified and regulated can lawfully give immigration advice in the UK. This applies to advice about the EU Settlement Scheme as much as to any other immigration matter.

Who can advise

Regulated advisers include solicitors regulated by the SRA, people regulated by CILEx Regulation, barristers, and advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority, which was previously called the OISC. Advisers registered with the Immigration Advice Authority are authorised at different levels, and the level affects what work they can do.

How to check

Each regulator keeps a public register that anyone can search online. You can ask any adviser for their registration details and check them yourself.

Free and paid help

Some charities and community organisations have offered free help with the scheme, and some still do. Availability varies by area and has changed over time. Leaflegal introduces people to regulated advisers who charge for their work, so it is worth asking about fees at the start and comparing options if cost is a concern.

Warning signs

Be cautious of anyone who promises a particular result, charges large amounts in cash without paperwork, suggests leaving information out of an application or will not say how they are regulated. Help offered on social media or through informal contacts may not be regulated.

Signs of a good adviser

  • They explain your status and options in plain language
  • They give clear fee information in writing before starting
  • They are honest about uncertainty and never promise a result
  • They tell you who will handle your matter
  • They reply within the timescales they set out

The costs involved depend on what help you need. There may be official costs and adviser fees.

Official costs

Applying to the scheme has generally not carried a Home Office application fee, but other official costs can arise, for example for documents, translations or other applications. Fees and policies change, so check GOV.UK or ask your adviser for the current position.

Adviser fees

Many advisers offer a fixed fee for common tasks, such as reviewing your situation or helping with an application. More complex matters, or those involving decisions and challenges, may be charged differently. Ask for the fee and what it covers in writing before you agree.

Timescales

How long things take varies. Some account problems can be fixed fairly quickly, while applications and other matters may take months. The Home Office publishes information about processing on GOV.UK, and your adviser can tell you what to expect.

Letters and deadlines

If you receive a letter or decision from the Home Office, note the date and any deadline, and keep the whole letter. Some decisions have short time limits. When you enquire through Leaflegal, include the date of any letter so the firm can see how urgent your matter is.

Planning ahead

Many people find it less stressful to look into questions about their status before they need to prove it urgently, for example before a job change, a house move or a long trip. Sorting out account problems or questions in good time can avoid last minute worry.

What is the EU Settlement Scheme?
It is the scheme set up after Brexit to protect the rights of many EU, EEA and Swiss citizens and their family members who had made their lives in the UK. People granted status under it receive either settled status or pre-settled status, which is held digitally. A regulated adviser can explain how it relates to you.
What is the difference between settled and pre-settled status?
Settled status is permanent permission to stay. Pre-settled status is permission for a limited period. What your status means for you, and what may happen next, depends on your situation. A regulated adviser can look at your status and explain it in plain terms.
Can Leaflegal tell me whether I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We cannot tell you whether you can apply or what your status means. We introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at your situation and explain your options.
Has the scheme changed since it opened?
Yes. The Home Office has changed how the scheme works several times, including how it handles some types of status and applications. Information from a few years ago, or from friends, may be out of date. A regulated adviser works with the current position and can explain it.
Do I get a card or certificate?
Status under the scheme is usually digital. You can view it online and prove it using a share code. Some people still have older physical documents, but these do not usually replace the digital record. Your adviser can explain how this works for you.
Is the scheme the same as a visa?
The scheme is separate from the visa routes in the main Immigration Rules and has its own rules. People sometimes confuse the two. A regulated adviser can explain how the scheme relates to other parts of the immigration system in your situation.
Do Irish citizens need to think about the scheme?
Irish citizens have a separate position in the UK because of long standing arrangements between the UK and Ireland. Their family members may have different questions. A regulated adviser can explain how the scheme does or does not relate to you and your family.
How do I prove my status to an employer or landlord?
You can usually generate a share code on GOV.UK and give it to them with your date of birth. They then check your status online. GOV.UK explains the different checking services. If an employer or landlord has difficulty, the Home Office has guidance for them.
I cannot log in to my account. What can I do?
The Home Office has processes for people who cannot sign in, have lost access to their email or phone, or have a new passport. There are online forms and a helpline. If the problem continues, a regulated adviser can help work out what has gone wrong.
I have a new passport. Do I need to do anything?
If your status is linked to an old passport or identity card, the details may need updating so your status is linked to the new document. This can help avoid delays when travelling. GOV.UK explains how to update your details, and your adviser can help if there are problems.
My status shows the wrong information. What now?
Mistakes in the online record can sometimes be corrected through the Home Office's processes. If something important is wrong, such as your name, date of birth or type of status, a regulated adviser can explain what can be done and whether any time limits apply.
What should I keep a record of?
Many people keep their application reference number, the email address used, the document linked to their status and copies of any decision letters. Keeping these safely can save a lot of time if anything needs sorting out in future.
My bank asked about my status. Is that normal?
Banks, employers, landlords and others may ask for proof of immigration status. Sharing a code through the official online service is the usual way to prove it. If you are being refused a service because of your status, a regulated adviser can explain your options.

Can the adviser help my whole family?
Many advisers help families where several people have questions about their status. Each person may be in a different position. Mention everyone involved when you enquire, including family members outside the UK, so the adviser can plan the conversation.
Can I speak to an adviser in my own language?
Some advisers speak other languages and some firms can arrange interpreters. Mention your preferred language when you enquire, and the firm will tell you what is available.
Will the adviser deal with the Home Office for me?
Once instructed, an adviser can usually act as your representative, dealing with correspondence and responding to requests. You will still need to provide information and check what is submitted. Ask how they will keep you updated.
How do I know the adviser is regulated?
You can search the public registers of the SRA, CILEx Regulation and the Immigration Advice Authority. Any regulated adviser will give you their details if asked. Every firm or adviser Leaflegal introduces is regulated to give immigration advice.
Can a family member make the enquiry for me?
Yes. A relative or friend can make an enquiry for you, which can help if you are abroad, unwell or busy. The adviser will need to speak to you directly before acting for you, and will explain how they can take your instructions.
I have received a letter about my status. What do I do?
Keep the whole letter, note the date and any deadline, and read it carefully. If you have an adviser, send it to them. If not, many people contact a regulated adviser quickly, because some letters have short time limits. Include the date when you enquire.
I think I never applied. Is it too late?
Only a regulated adviser can look at your circumstances and explain what may be possible. Many people in this situation feel worried, and an adviser will deal with it in confidence. When you enquire, explain briefly what happened so the firm understands the urgency.
My application was refused. Can an adviser help?
Yes. An adviser can read the decision, explain the reasons and talk through any options. Some options have short time limits, so it helps to make contact soon after the decision arrives. Tell us the date on the letter when you enquire.
The Home Office has asked me for more information. What now?
Requests for information usually have a deadline, and missing it can affect the outcome. If an adviser is acting for you, pass the request to them straight away. If not, a regulated adviser can help you understand what is being asked.
I am planning to move abroad for a while. Should I get advice first?
Whether time abroad affects your status depends on your circumstances. Many people find it helpful to speak to a regulated adviser before making long term plans, so they understand how their status may be affected. The decision about whether to seek advice is yours.
What if I move house or change my phone number?
It helps to keep your contact details up to date with the Home Office and with any adviser, so that letters and messages reach you. GOV.UK explains how to update your details. Missed letters can mean missed deadlines, so many people update their details as soon as anything changes.
Does it cost anything to apply to the scheme?
Applying to the scheme has generally not carried a Home Office application fee, but policies change and other costs can arise. Check GOV.UK for the current position. Adviser fees are separate and should be explained in writing before work starts.
How much do advisers charge?
It depends on the work. Many advisers offer fixed fees for common tasks, while more complex matters may be charged differently. Ask for the fee and what it includes in writing before you agree.
Is there free help available?

What is a partner visa?
It is permission for someone to live in the UK with their husband, wife, civil partner, fiancé, fiancée or long term partner. People often call it a spouse visa. A regulated adviser can explain what a partner application would involve for you and whether it may be an option in your circumstances.
Can Leaflegal tell us if we qualify?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and never does. We do not ask about your immigration history or assess your chances. We introduce you to a firm or adviser who is regulated, and they will look at your situation and explain your options.
Is a spouse visa the same as a partner visa?
People use the two terms in different ways, and the Home Office treats different kinds of relationship in different ways. A regulated adviser will explain how your relationship would be treated and what that means for any application. Mention whether you are married, in a civil partnership, engaged or living together when you enquire, as it helps the adviser prepare for the first conversation.
My partner is overseas. Can you help?
Yes. We introduce couples to regulated advisers who handle applications made from outside the UK. Many advisers work by phone, video and email, so it does not matter where each of you is. Tell us where your partner is when you make your enquiry.
We are already in the UK together. Is this page for us?
It can be. Many couples who are already living here look for advice about their next step, such as a change of visa or a renewal. Tell us a little about your situation when you enquire, and the adviser will explain how they can help.
Can I enquire on behalf of my partner?
Yes, either partner can make the enquiry. The adviser will usually want to speak to both of you at some point, because they will need information from each of you. Let us know the best way to reach you both. If your partner is in a different time zone, tell us the best times to reach each of you.
Why do partner rules change so often?
Immigration policy is reviewed regularly by the government, and the rules for partners have been changed several times over the years. Fees, guidance and requirements can all move. That is why it is risky to rely on what worked for friends or family in the past. A regulated adviser explains things based on the rules that apply when you apply.
What happens at the first consultation?
The adviser will ask about your relationship, where each of you lives, your immigration histories and your plans. They will explain how they could help and what it might cost. You do not need a full set of documents. It is also your chance to decide whether you want to work with them.

More questions people ask

What is a work visa?
It is permission from the Home Office to come to the UK, or stay in the UK, to work. There are several kinds of work visa, each for a different purpose, and they change over time. A regulated adviser can explain which, if any, might fit your situation, based on the rules as they stand today.
Can Leaflegal tell me which visa I need?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We do not ask about your immigration history or assess your chances. We introduce you to a firm or adviser who is regulated, and they will look at your situation and explain your options.
Can you find me a job?
No. Leaflegal is not a recruitment agency and cannot help you find work. We introduce you to a regulated immigration adviser who can help with the visa side of things. If you already have a job offer, mention it when you enquire so the adviser has the full picture. Be cautious of anyone who offers to find you a UK job in return for a payment.
I am already in the UK. Can you still help?
Yes. We introduce people both inside and outside the UK to regulated advisers. If you are already here, tell us when your current visa ends when you make your enquiry, so that the adviser knows how much time there is. Having the exact date to hand, and any letters you have received about your current visa, will help the first conversation go smoothly.
I am overseas. Can a UK adviser help me?
Yes. Many regulated advisers in the UK help people who are applying from abroad, working by phone, video and email. Tell us where you are when you enquire. The adviser will explain how they work with clients overseas and how they handle time differences.
Is it worth getting advice if my employer is handling things?
Some employers arrange advice for their workers, and some do not. Even when an employer is involved, some people like to have their own adviser to explain things from their point of view, particularly if family members are involved. A regulated adviser can explain how they would work alongside your employer.
Why do the rules keep changing?
Immigration policy is set by the government and reviewed regularly, often in response to the economy and the jobs market. Changes can affect fees, guidance and the kinds of visa available. This is why advice from a few years ago, or from someone whose situation was different, may not help you now. A regulated adviser works from the current rules.
