Spouse and partner visas explained

Pick a topic to read more.

A partner visa, often called a spouse visa, is permission for someone to live in the UK with their husband, wife, civil partner, fiancé, fiancée or long term partner. For most couples it is about something very simple. They want to build their life together in one place.

The application itself is far from simple. Partner applications are some of the most closely examined in the immigration system, and the paperwork can feel overwhelming. That is why so many couples choose to work with a regulated adviser before they apply.

Couples reach this point in many ways. Some met abroad and want to start married life in the UK. Some are already living here together and want to understand their next step. Others have been living apart for months or years and want to know what would be involved in being together.

Why couples ask for help

A partner application touches almost every part of a couple's life together, from their history and their home to their work and their plans. Getting something wrong can mean a refusal, a lost fee and more time apart. A regulated adviser looks at your own circumstances, explains what applies to you and helps you prepare everything carefully.

Who we hear from

  • British citizens whose partner lives overseas
  • UK residents whose partner wants to join them
  • Couples already living in the UK together
  • Engaged couples planning a wedding
  • Couples whose partner visa is coming up for renewal
  • People whose relationship has changed while on a partner visa

What a regulated adviser does

A regulated adviser listens to both of you, explains what your options might be and what each would involve, and helps you prepare and submit an application if you decide to go ahead. They check documents, answer questions as they come up and deal with the Home Office if there are follow up questions. They can also tell you honestly if the timing does not look right yet.

This page is about partners. For children, parents and other relatives, see our page on family visas.

A partner application is usually a joint effort between the couple and their adviser. Here is a general picture of how it tends to work.

Both of you are involved

Advisers usually need information from both partners. One of you may be in the UK and the other abroad, possibly in a different time zone. Most advisers are used to this and can arrange calls and messages that suit you both. Ask how they would like to communicate with each of you.

Telling your story

Every relationship is different. Your adviser will want to understand how you met, how your relationship has developed and what your plans are. This can feel personal, but it helps them understand your situation properly. What you tell a regulated adviser is confidential, under the rules of their regulator.

A plan and a checklist

If you decide to go ahead, the adviser will usually explain the steps in writing and tell you what each of you needs to gather. Sharing the work between you, and keeping everything in one place, makes the process much easier to manage.

Checking before anything is sent

Your adviser will usually check the application and documents before they are submitted, looking for gaps and anything that does not match. They may ask you to explain things that seem obvious to you. That is normal, and it helps avoid problems later.

After the application

Once the application is in, your adviser can deal with any questions from the Home Office and explain the decision when it comes. If anything goes wrong, they can talk you through what might be done next.

Looking after yourselves

Partner applications can be stressful, particularly for couples who are apart. Many people find it helps to agree how you will share the work, set aside regular time to go through things together and lean on your adviser for answers rather than searching online late at night. Your adviser is there to answer questions, so ask them.

The first consultation is where you and your adviser get to know each other. It is a conversation, not a test, and you do not need to have every answer ready.

Can you both attend?

Many couples like to attend the first consultation together, even if one of you joins by video from abroad. It means you both hear the same information and can both ask questions. Let the firm know in advance who will be joining.

What you might be asked

  • How and when you met
  • Whether you are married, in a civil partnership or engaged
  • Where each of you lives now
  • Each partner's immigration status and history
  • Any previous marriages or relationships that are relevant
  • Any previous visa applications or refusals
  • Any dates you are working towards

What to have to hand

There is no need for a full bundle of documents at this stage. Having both passports, any marriage or civil partnership certificate, details of any current visa and any letters from the Home Office nearby will make the conversation more useful.

Questions you might ask

  • How many partner applications like ours do you handle?
  • Who will work on our matter day to day?
  • Is your fee fixed, and what does it cover?
  • Are government fees included or extra?
  • How long do you expect preparation to take?
  • How will you keep both of us updated?
  • What happens if the Home Office has questions?

After the consultation

Most firms follow up in writing with a summary of what was discussed, what they can do and what it would cost. Read it together. You are under no obligation to go ahead, and it is normal to come back with more questions before you decide.

When couples are apart, every week matters. It is natural to want to apply as quickly as possible. Even so, an application that is rushed and incomplete can lead to a much longer wait in the end.

Preparation

Preparation is usually the longest part, and the part you have most control over. Documents may need to be ordered, translated or collected from different places, and appointments may need booking. Your adviser will give you a realistic idea of how long this is likely to take in your case.

The decision

The Home Office publishes its current processing times on GOV.UK. They differ depending on where the application is made and can change. Faster services are sometimes available for an extra fee in some places. Your adviser can explain what is realistic.

Planning around life events

Weddings, house moves, new jobs and new babies can all affect timing. If something big is coming up, mention it to your adviser early. They can explain how it might affect the process.

Renewals

Many partner visas need renewing at some point. Put the end date in both your calendars and get in touch with an adviser well before it arrives, so there is plenty of time to prepare.

Time apart

If you are living apart while you prepare, it can help to keep in regular contact with your adviser so that you always know where things stand. Knowing what is happening, and what comes next, can make the waiting a little easier.

Partner applications involve government fees and, if you use one, an adviser's fee. Together they can add up to a significant sum, so it is worth understanding them early.

Government fees

There is usually an application fee, and most applicants also pay the Immigration Health Surcharge, which covers NHS care for the length of the visa. There can also be costs for things like translations, tests and travel to appointments. All of these change from time to time, so check the current figures on GOV.UK or ask your adviser.

Adviser fees

Many advisers offer a fixed fee for a partner application. The fee may depend on where the application is made from and how much work is involved. Ask for the fee in writing, including what is covered and whether government fees are included.

Help with costs

In some situations it may be possible to ask the Home Office to waive a fee. Whether this is available depends on the application and your circumstances, and a regulated adviser can tell you whether it is worth exploring.

Thinking ahead

Many couples find it helpful to think about the costs over several years, not just the first application, as renewals and later applications also have fees. Your adviser can help you understand the likely picture.

Getting a clear quote

When comparing firms, check that each quote covers the same work, such as document checks, preparing the application and dealing with any Home Office questions afterwards. A good firm will be happy to explain what is and is not included, and will tell you promptly if anything is likely to cost more.

Life does not stand still while a visa is in place. Relationships change, people move, jobs come and go and families grow. Some changes are routine. Others can matter a great deal for a partner visa.

Moving house or changing jobs

Everyday changes, such as moving home, changing jobs or getting a new passport, can sometimes matter for a partner visa or for a later application. If you are unsure whether a change is important, ask your adviser. It is a quick question that can save trouble later.

Letters from the Home Office

If you receive a letter or email from the Home Office, read it carefully and note any deadline, as some time limits are short. Get in touch with a regulated adviser as soon as you can and have the letter ready to share. If you do not have an adviser yet, mention the deadline when you enquire.

If your relationship ends

A relationship ending is hard enough without immigration worries on top. If you hold a partner visa and your relationship has broken down, speak to a regulated adviser as soon as you can. They can explain how it may affect you.

If you are not safe

If you are experiencing domestic abuse, your safety comes first. Support services and helplines can help you stay safe and find somewhere to go. A regulated adviser can then talk to you about your immigration position when you are able to.

Keeping records

Many couples keep a simple folder of important documents, letters, payslips and travel dates as they go. It makes later applications much easier to prepare, and it means you have everything to hand if a question ever comes up.

Old advice

The rules for partners have changed many times. Advice from friends who applied a few years ago, or from old posts online, may no longer be right. A regulated adviser works from the rules as they stand now.

What is a partner visa?

Is a spouse visa the same as a partner visa?
People use the two terms in different ways, and the Home Office treats different kinds of relationship in different ways. A regulated adviser will explain how your relationship would be treated and what that means for any application. Mention whether you are married, in a civil partnership, engaged or living together when you enquire, as it helps the adviser prepare for the first conversation.
My partner is overseas. Can you help?
Yes. We introduce couples to regulated advisers who handle applications made from outside the UK. Many advisers work by phone, video and email, so it does not matter where each of you is. Tell us where your partner is when you make your enquiry.
We are already in the UK together. Is this page for us?
It can be. Many couples who are already living here look for advice about their next step, such as a change of visa or a renewal. Tell us a little about your situation when you enquire, and the adviser will explain how they can help.
Can I enquire on behalf of my partner?
Yes, either partner can make the enquiry. The adviser will usually want to speak to both of you at some point, because they will need information from each of you. Let us know the best way to reach you both. If your partner is in a different time zone, tell us the best times to reach each of you.
Why do partner rules change so often?
Immigration policy is reviewed regularly by the government, and the rules for partners have been changed several times over the years. Fees, guidance and requirements can all move. That is why it is risky to rely on what worked for friends or family in the past. A regulated adviser explains things based on the rules that apply when you apply.
What happens at the first consultation?
The adviser will ask about your relationship, where each of you lives, your immigration histories and your plans. They will explain how they could help and what it might cost. You do not need a full set of documents. It is also your chance to decide whether you want to work with them.
Should we both attend?
It often helps. When both partners attend, you both hear the same information and can both ask questions. If one of you is abroad, a video call can usually include you both. Let the firm know in advance who will be joining.
Is the first consultation free?
It depends on the firm. Some offer a short free call before a paid consultation, and others charge from the start. The firm should tell you before the consultation whether there is a fee and how much it is, so there are no surprises.

It helps to have both passports, any marriage or civil partnership certificate, details of any current visa and any letters from the Home Office to hand. If either of you has applied for a visa before, have the dates and outcomes in mind. The adviser will tell you what else they need.
The questions feel very personal. Is that normal?
Yes. Partner applications are about your relationship, so advisers need to understand it. They are not judging you. They are trying to understand your situation properly so that they can help. What you tell them is confidential under the rules of their regulator.
Should we mention a past refusal?
Yes. Advisers can only help properly if they know the full history of both partners, including anything that worries you. A past refusal or problem does not stop an adviser helping, but they need to know about it at the start rather than later.
What does the adviser actually do?
A regulated adviser looks at your circumstances, explains your options, helps you prepare the application and checks your documents before they are sent. They can deal with the Home Office if there are questions and explain the decision when it arrives. Ask exactly what is included in their service.
How will we share documents?
Many firms use secure email or an online portal for documents. Ask how the firm prefers to receive them and how it keeps them safe. Keeping your own copies of everything you send is always a good idea. If you are sending documents from abroad, ask whether originals or copies are needed, as posting originals can take time and carries some risk.
What if our circumstances change during the process?
Tell your adviser straight away. A new job, a house move, a pregnancy or a change in your relationship can all matter. It is always better to mention something and be told it does not matter than to leave it out.
Can we use an adviser in a different part of the UK?
Yes. Immigration rules apply across the whole of the UK, and many advisers work remotely. You do not need to choose a firm near where you live, although some couples like to meet in person. Ask the firm how it usually works. What matters most is that the adviser is regulated, experienced in partner matters and easy for you both to talk to.
What if we are unhappy with the service?
Regulated firms must have a complaints procedure, and you can ask for a copy at the start. If a complaint is not resolved, you may be able to take it further, for example to the Legal Ombudsman or to the adviser's regulator. The firm should explain how this works.
Can the adviser help if one of us does not speak English well?
Some firms have advisers who speak other languages, and some can arrange interpreters. It is important that both partners understand what is being explained and what is being signed. Mention any language needs when you enquire, and ask the firm what it can offer.
How much does a partner visa cost?
There is usually an application fee and, for most applicants, the Immigration Health Surcharge, plus costs such as translations and travel to appointments. These amounts change from time to time and can add up to a significant sum. Check GOV.UK for current figures or ask your adviser for a breakdown. If children are applying as well, the total can be considerably higher.
How much do advisers charge?
Many advisers offer a fixed fee for a partner application, which may vary depending on where you apply from and how much work is involved. Ask for the fee in writing, and check what is covered and whether government fees are included, before you agree to anything.
Can we pay in stages?
Some firms allow fees to be paid in stages, and others ask for payment upfront. Government fees are usually paid when the application is made. Ask the firm what payment arrangements it offers before you commit, so you can plan your budget. Remember that renewals and later applications will usually have their own fees too.
Does Leaflegal charge us?
No. Leaflegal costs couples nothing to use. The regulated firm we introduce you to pays us a fee for the introduction, and that has no effect on the price they charge you. Any fees are agreed between you and the firm directly, and you are never obliged to instruct them.
Are cheaper advisers just as good?
Price is only one part of the picture. It is worth looking at what is included, who will actually handle your application, how experienced they are with partner matters and whether they are properly regulated. A clear written quote from each firm helps you compare fairly.
The current visa ends soon. What should we do?
Leaflegal cannot advise on your situation, but we can introduce you to a regulated adviser quickly. Tell us the end date of any current visa when you enquire. The sooner you speak to an adviser, the more time there is to understand your options.
We have received a letter from the Home Office.
Read it carefully and note any deadline, as some time limits are short. Get in touch with a regulated adviser as soon as you can and have the letter ready. If you do not have an adviser yet, mention the deadline in your enquiry so the firm knows it is urgent.
Our application was refused. Can you help?
We can introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at the refusal. Some challenges have short time limits, so tell us the date on the decision when you enquire. Have the refusal letter and a copy of the application ready for the adviser.
How do we check an adviser is regulated?
You can check solicitors on the Solicitors Regulation Authority register and advisers on the Immigration Advice Authority register, formerly the OISC. Both are free online. A genuine adviser will give you their details if you ask. Every firm we introduce is regulated.
How quickly will someone contact us?
The firm usually gets in touch within one working day of your enquiry, often sooner. If you have an urgent deadline, say so in your enquiry. Make sure you check the phone number and email you give us, including spam folders. The first contact is usually a short call to understand what you need and arrange a consultation.

What if one of us is not safe at home?
Your safety comes first. If you are experiencing domestic abuse, contact the police in an emergency, or a domestic abuse helpline or support service for help and somewhere safe to go. When you are able to, a regulated adviser can talk to you about your immigration position in confidence.
What does indefinite leave to remain mean?
Indefinite leave to remain, often called ILR, is permission to stay in the UK without a time limit. It is the most common form of settlement. Whether it is relevant to you, and how it might apply to your situation, is something a regulated adviser can explain after looking at your history.
Is settlement the same as British citizenship?
No. Settlement is a type of immigration status, while British citizenship is a nationality. They are separate and involve separate applications. Many people hold settlement for years without applying for citizenship, and some never do. A regulated adviser can explain the differences and what each means in practice for you.
Can Leaflegal tell me if I can apply?
No. Leaflegal is not regulated to give immigration advice and does not do so. We cannot tell you whether you can apply or when. We introduce you to a regulated adviser who can look at your circumstances and explain your options.
Why does everyone say the rules keep changing?
Settlement policy has changed many times over the years, and the government has announced further changes. This means information from friends, older websites or social media may be out of date. A regulated adviser works with the current rules every day and can explain how they apply to you.
What is an eVisa?
An eVisa is a digital record of a person's immigration status, linked to a UK Visas and Immigration account. The Home Office has moved away from physical cards towards eVisas. It is used to prove status to employers, landlords and others. Your adviser can explain how this works if you are granted permission.
Does settlement affect my family?
It can, because family members often have their own immigration status and their own applications to think about. How your application relates to theirs depends on your circumstances. A regulated adviser can look at the whole family's position and explain how applications might be planned.
What happens after I make an enquiry?
Leaflegal passes your enquiry to one regulated firm or adviser. They usually contact you within one working day, often sooner, to arrange a first conversation. They will ask some questions about your situation and explain how they can help and what it might cost.
Will I have to meet the adviser in person?
Not necessarily. Many advisers work by phone, video call and email, and some also offer face to face meetings at their offices. Ask the adviser how they usually work. Many people find remote meetings convenient, especially if they work long hours or have caring responsibilities.
